Farewell, France
We have some news to share with you.
Summary
Starting November 24, Quad9 will no longer be able to offer its free cyberprotection service to users located in France.
The French courts yesterday handed down an unfavorable ruling against Quad9 in a major case that undermines the transparency and stability of the internet. This decision in favor of beIN forces Quad9 to block the recursive DNS resolution for a number of domain names for users located in France.
The good news is the court significantly reduced the financial penalty, from €580,000 to €1,000 per day. However, for Quad9, this has never been just a financial issue: it is a matter of principle.
We cannot comply with this ruling over the long term while remaining faithful to our mission to protect your privacy, defend you against cyber threats, and preserve the integrity of the internet. For us, recursive DNS resolution is a neutral and passive service. We categorically refuse to alter or lie to you about the DNS responses you receive in a way that opposes your expectations.
This ruling forces an untenable position upon us. Compliance is technically unfeasible, and implementation directly contradicts our mandate to serve the public. As a result, we have made the difficult decision to withdraw Quad9 services from France as of Tuesday, November 24. We deeply regret that French internet users will be left without an alternative that can adequately protect their cybersecurity while remaining compliant with European privacy law.
Background
beIN, a Qatari company with offices in France, previously sought to force Quad9 to block resolution for a set of domain names they assert are participating in copyright infringement of its audiovisual rights on sports event broadcasts. Quad9 has no association with these domain names, nor with the operators of the websites. The French courts have fundamentally misunderstood the working of the internet and the role of the domain name system and have allowed a financial penalty to apply to Quad9 if we do not block DNS responses to users in France.
Quad9 is a Swiss public-benefit, nonprofit foundation established for the explicit purpose of protecting the cybersecurity of internet users. We charge nothing for our service and serve all people and organizations which seek to improve the security and privacy of their internet use. Our core values are to make privacy, security, and integrity available to all internet users, and our mission is to universally apply these guiding principles to recursive DNS resolution services in order to serve end users.
We want to be very clear: we do not condone content piracy. Quad9 exists to create a safer, more trustworthy internet experience for end users. We offer global blocking as an option for malicious cyberactivity against users worldwide. We do not block content, and we give users the choice to use our threat blocking services, or not, based on their interests and needs. Content piracy needs to be dealt with at the source: the place where the infringing content is published. Forcing uninvolved third parties to break the fundamental operating principles of the internet to benefit individual parties to the detriment of the global internet-using public is misguided, counter-productive, and distracts from actual solutions addressing the hosting of the content directly, or the market pressures which cause piracy to exist in the first place. Not only are DNS recursive resolvers, like Quad9, the wrong actors to address piracy, but applying legal pressure to Quad9 will also have vast unintended consequences harming the cybersecurity of hundreds of millions of innocent internet users.
For Quad9, complying with this ruling is impossible. We have no database of users associating them with jurisdictions. Our service is free to the public and does not require sign-up or proof of identity. Recursive DNS is so fundamental to the underlying operation of the internet that there is no method to map users’ jurisdictions reliably, nor do we think there should be a way to force users to identify themselves before they can connect to the internet. Our core goals – privacy of user data and honesty of responses - are directly opposed to the methods necessary to implement the blocking this court ruling would require us to perform. There is no way we can determine which users this ruling applies to – we have no list of French users, because we do not keep dossiers of our users at all.
If we do not comply, we will face a daily penalty of 1.000 euros until the blocking concludes on November 14 (i.e., 24.000 euros) not including our legal defense. Quad9 cannot survive the accumulation of penalties yet to be assessed in 20 other pending cases, nor those of future cases similar to the one brought by beIN. Inaction is, therefore, not an option to challenge this decision.
Can Quad9 just comply via global blocking?
We could add these names to our global malicious domain blocking database, which is what we use to provide internet users with malware and phishing protection. This is a single, monolithic set of names applied to all users of our protective services (9.9.9.9 & secondaries) globally but not to our nonprotective services (9.9.9.10 & secondaries). Blocking these names for all users globally would satisfy the requirements of the court.
However, this would have the “extraterritorial” effect of applying French legal decisions to every internet user worldwide, when they are meant to solely apply to France, for the commercial benefit of a single private, foreign-owned company. We suspect if other nations wished to impose their national legislation within France, there would be a strong reaction from French users and authorities. The converse is equally true: the findings of a French court are not applicable to the rest of the world. Such a system would set a precedent that any nation could impose their laws, morals, economic demands, or social constructs upon any other nation by the means of blocking DNS names, and thus blocking communication and content.
Therefore, this global blocking method is entirely unworkable for us as a solution to be compliant with the court order.
What is “France” from the perspective of the internet?
There are more important reasons geoIP blocking will not work. We already prevailed in a similar legal case in Germany in 2021-2023 in which we learned an important lesson: we tried to use geoIP blocking as a “hack” in a similar circumstance at the start of the legal proceedings, allowing us to continue services in Germany until the case concluded. The plaintiff (Sony Music Entertainment GmbH) then used a VPN to access the blocked content from Germany in circumvention of the geoIP blocking. The court agreed with Sony in their assertions of noncompliance, and we were fined for contempt of court.
From this experience, we know that the geoIP-based model is not only unfit from a technical perspective, but we have proven, historical evidence that using geoIP methods results in fines being levied against us by courts. To any technology-aware person, this seems absurd and outrageous – but this is what we face in these lawsuits where there is a disconnect between the courts’ understanding and how (and why) the technical protocols of the internet actually operate. We cannot risk this negative outcome in this or any other future instance, as absurd decisions apparently are de rigueur in these cases.
We eventually won that case against Sony, but it took several years, six figures of cost to Quad9, and massive wasted time – all of which set us back in our ability to deliver services and is a resource debt we are still paying for today. This case is just more of the same – the opposition has more lawyers than we have money and time.
The decision of Quad9
Given these conditions and as a result of our unwavering mission, our historical experience, our desire to remain within the law, and the outcome of the court decision, we are left with an uncomfortable choice.
The demands lead us to an impossible requirement: we must adhere to the court’s decision, or we face growing risks to our existence. However, in implementing the blocking, we must either extend French law to the whole world (blocking globally) or create a vulnerability condition whereby we continue to be at risk because the available technical methods are imprecise, impossible to validate, and do not meet any user privacy and security requirements.
This leads us to the only conclusion we can understand to eliminate our legal risk and keep us true to our mission: we must make our cybersecurity protection and general services unavailable to French users. Our departure is scheduled for November 24, 2026.
In any event, for Quad9, this is a matter of principle regarding the potential misuse of these types of blocking measures. Allowing private corporations to have the ability to block what is seen on the internet seems to us to be a dangerous and infinitely deep problem. Actions that seem reasonable and infrequent today will tomorrow become an avalanche of petty demands used to suppress political, religious, economic, or social outcomes. We will not be even a small part of that degradation of the freedom of knowledge. Once the tools of censorship are built, they are rarely used sparingly or justly.
This case with beIN is not the only litigation we face in France. There are approximately 20 different lawsuits pending against Quad9 (and other recursive resolver operators) that demand we comply with blocking hundreds of different names. We expect many of them to escalate to the same conditions as we have seen with beIN, which puts Quad9 at risk regardless of the outcome of this case.
Based on the generalized, high-level traffic statistics we keep, we know we have many users in France. With our departure, those users will be put at risk from the threats we have protected them from for nearly 9 years. We deeply regret that result, but we have no other choice. Below is a partial list of the networks from which we see traffic in France. You will see many infrastructure, national security, finance, education, and other networks of critical importance in addition to ISP users and mobile users. All these users will lose service on November 24 because of the legal risks facing Quad9 in France.
What is the purpose of this whole lawsuit, anyway?
The most troubling part of this decision is that it is ineffective in practice. Forcing Quad9 to block domain names does not take the content out of circulation and has no effect on the servers that house the data.
We offer a free service, and there are thousands of other open DNS systems that do what Quad9 does but with lower trust, safety, and reliability. Users seeking blocked content will simply shift their DNS to use one of those other free services, possibly using systems in other nations that have less-than-honorable intentions from a privacy and security perspective. This is like holding a piece of newspaper in front of a stadium full of people to block the view of a concert. It is an unsolvable problem via recursive DNS, and the effect of legal rulings like this is not measurable outside of the amounts paid to lawyers and the number of words in press releases declaring hollow victory.
Even beIN will not see any changes. Perhaps that is understood and the cruelty may be the point - we cannot say with certainty why such expenditures have been made against a small nonprofit like Quad9, which tries only to bring safer, trusted service to users. But of this we are certain: these actions are a tragic waste of time and resources and create a real, negative outcome for the internet community of France, whether or not they are our users.
What we are doing on November 24
Quad9 will not block the domains in question - we wish to remain committed to our integrity of providing a complete view of the DNS for users who wish that service. However, starting on November 24, we will no longer serve French users, thus keeping Quad9 out of danger from similar, perhaps even larger risks in the future. Our hope is that 30 days will be sufficient time for conversion, though we regret that this message will undoubtedly not reach some users in time.
An appeal is pending, and we will continue to legally defend our position in court in this case and as many other cases as we are able. We will continue to oppose court-ordered DNS blocking of this nature everywhere. Our resources are limited, but we are determined to keep the internet open and free. You can donate here to help us continue to deliver on our mission and assist us in the legal costs we incur in this and other fights.
What you can do next
We think this problem is reversible. French citizens: tell your legislators that looking up names is not a criminal offense. The internet needs to remain open and freely available to anyone, and neutral intermediaries like Quad9 (or any DNS resolver) should not be implicated in legal proceedings because we map names to IP addresses. Further, we know there are potential plans in consideration in France (and other European nations) to give private corporations and others automated and unvalidated abilities to filter DNS content that they alone determine to be unwanted, making it possible for a wide range of badly-managed or anti-democratic activities to be implemented. We believe you disagree with this as much as we do. Make yourself heard, or you will lose your rights silently.
ARCOM is your regulatory authority, and you should tell them this precedent places France at risk both from a practical and strategic perspective.
Let them know that under their mandate, they have to balance your digital rights as citizens as well as the audiovisual rights on sports event broadcasts of rights holders. Balance cannot be claimed when the privacy and security of digital users are the collateral damage.
In conclusion
Fighting piracy via DNS resolvers blocking is ineffective. It is disproportionate and misguided, ill-fitted and futile to fight piracy, as the source content remains completely available to resurge under another name. Forcing Quad9 to depart harms French digital strategy and sovereignty in the long run relating to security, privacy, and integrity - contradicting France’s supreme national interests in exchange for a short-term, imaginary commercial victory.
We deeply regret that we must leave our French users without cybersecurity protection, but we hope the future will see a more reasonable interpretation of the law which allows us to return.
Sincerely,
The Quad9 Team

About the data
- Public power is common where electricity is cheap, so we also compared utilities within each state. Investor-owned prices rose faster in 38 of 48 states.
- Most of the gap opened from 2020 to 2023, during the energy price spike: 25% at investor-owned utilities, 11% at public utilities and co-ops. Before and since, both rose at about the same pace.
- After inflation, investor-owned prices rose 7.9%. Public and co-op prices fell 4.3%.
- Co-op homes have the highest bills, $159 a month in 2025, because they use the most power.
- Homes that buy power from a retail supplier aren't in the investor-owned figures. Counting them, prices on investor-owned lines rose 36%.
- Price is what homes paid divided by the kilowatt-hours they used, from EIA's yearly survey of utilities. Federal utilities and the smallest ones are left out.
- The data shows what homes paid, not why. Investor-owned utilities earn a return for shareholders on what they build. Public utilities and co-ops have no shareholders. See the rate cases for increases utilities are asking for now.

Learn to sing the songs you love. · singonkey.app
Saving shower singers from themselves since 2026.
Me singing along to a song OnKey learned from one play. The full demo, with sound.
OnKey is a free, open-source singing trainer for the Mac. Play a song in Spotify, Apple Music, or YouTube in a browser, and sing along. The first time, you get the lyrics, and OnKey learns the singer's melody when the song ends. After that, the notes scroll towards you, your pitch is drawn over them, and you get a score with a report on every line.
OnKey doesn't provide, stream or download music. Your own music app plays it.
You need a Mac with Apple Silicon (M1 or later) and macOS 14.2 or later.
Download OnKey.dmg (from the latest release), open it and drag OnKey to Applications.
Setup asks for the microphone, to hear you, and for system audio recording, to hear the music app. It also asks whether OnKey may follow Spotify and Apple Music: choose Listen only and it follows songs by their sound alone. Getting started walks through it, and the FAQ answers the usual questions.
When a song ends, OnKey learns it on your Mac: a model separates the lead vocal from the music, CREPE tracks the singer's pitch, and the GAME note model turns it into one bar per syllable. On later plays it finds its place from the music itself and tracks your voice live. Singing an octave down counts, and being a touch late isn't punished. How it works and Scoring have the details.
OnKey never saves the music it listens to. It analyses the sound in memory as it plays. Your recordings and the songs it learns stay on your Mac (a take sung over speakers can contain some of the music). There's no account, no analytics and no crash reporting.
These requests do go out:
- Shazam (Apple): a short signature of the music app's sound, not the audio and never your mic, to name a song OnKey hasn't learned when the music app doesn't say what's playing.
- LRCLIB: the song's artist, title, album and length, to fetch lyrics.
- iTunes Search and Lookup (Apple): an artist and title, or the ID Shazam returned, for covers and the song's length. The cover images come from Spotify's, Apple's or YouTube's image hosts.
- GitHub: a check for a new version a minute after launch and once a day, and its download.
Settings has a switch for Shazam, for lyrics and for the update check. Privacy lists everything OnKey keeps and sends, and scripts/release-hosts.txt lists every web address in a release.
OnKey is for practising at home, with music you're entitled to play. Please:
- Keep what it learns to yourself. Its transcription of each song's melody is for your practice, so don't share or publish it.
- To share a take, use Share on its page. It makes a short video of your voice and your pitch line, with no music, and you can add the song from Instagram's own music library, which is licensed for this. Headphone takes are best: sung on speakers, some of the song is in your recording. Don't post screen recordings with the music playing.
- Use a licensed karaoke service for a bar or an event. OnKey's lyrics come from a free community database.
- Don't modify OnKey to save the music.

Abstract
Background
Research demonstrates significant gender- and sexual orientation–based differences in orgasm rates from sexual intercourse; however, this “orgasm gap” has not been studied with respect to age.
Aim
The study sought to examine age-related disparities in orgasm rates from sexual intercourse by gender and sexual orientation.
Methods
A survey sample of 24 752 adults from the United States, ranging in age from 18 to 100 years. Data were collected across 8 cross-sectional surveys between 2015 and 2023.
Outcomes
Participants reported their average rate of orgasm during sexual intercourse, from 0% to 100%.
Results
Orgasm rate was associated with age but with minimal effect size. In all age groups, men reported higher rates of orgasm than did women. Men’s orgasm rates ranged from 70% to 85%, while women’s ranged from 46% to 58%. Men reported orgasm rates between 22% and 30% higher than women’s rates. Sexual orientation impacted orgasm rates by gender but not uniformly across age groups.
Clinical Translation
The persistence of the orgasm gap across ages necessitates a tailored approach in clinical practice and education, focusing on inclusive sexual health discussions, addressing the unique challenges of sexual minorities and aging, and emphasizing mutual satisfaction to promote sexual well-being for all.
Strengths and Limitations
This study is the first to examine the orgasm gap with respect to age, and does so in a large, diverse sample. Findings are limited by methodology, including single-item assessments of orgasm and a sample of single adults.
Conclusion
This study revealed enduring disparities in orgasm rates from sexual intercourse, likely resulting from many factors, including sociocultural norms and inadequate sex education.
Keywords: orgasm, aging, orgasm gap, sexual orientation, gender differences
Orgasm is a psychophysiological response to sexual stimulation involving muscle contractions, hormonal changes, and tension release.1 While not essential for sexual pleasure,2,3 orgasms are frequently sought in both solitary and partnered sexual activity, contributing to sexual satisfaction and well-being.4-8 The absence of orgasm can cause distress and relationship challenges.9-11 In sexual intercourse, orgasm rates vary across genders and sexual orientations, with women—particularly heterosexual women—reporting lower orgasm rates than men and lesbian women (ie, the orgasm gap).12-14 Prior investigations into these disparities have revealed a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors influencing sexual pleasure. However, the impact of age on the orgasm gap is unexplored. Studying age in this context can uncover developmental influences on sexual health, offering insights for interventions that enhance sexual functioning across the lifespan. This study assessed differences in orgasm rates from sexual intercourse by gender, sexual orientation, and age.
The orgasm gap
The orgasm gap between genders is substantial, with reported differences ranging from 25% to 52%.12-20 For example, a recent study found that 82% of men reported orgasming during their most recent casual sexual encounter compared with only 32% of women in the study.20 Factors contributing to this gap include physiological, anatomical, and sociocultural elements. For women, orgasm variations are partly due to anatomical differences, such as clitoral-vaginal distance, and responses to different types of stimulation.21-24 Hormonal fluctuations, like changes in estrogen and testosterone during the menstrual cycle and menopause, also affect women's libido and orgasmic capacity.25-28 Conversely, men’s sexual function is more consistently linked with testosterone levels, which can decline with age.29-31
Orgasm is influenced by interpersonal and sociocultural factors. Relationship dynamics and partner behaviors impact orgasm frequency, particularly for women. Open sexual communication and prioritization of foreplay by partners are positively associated with women’s orgasm.32-34 Sociocultural influences, including patriarchy, sexism, inadequate sexual education, and the cultural overvaluation of penetrative sex, contribute to orgasm discrepancies between young heterosexual men and women.35 These factors lead to disparities in pleasure-centric sexual behaviors, reinforcing the imbalance in orgasm rates.4,36-38 This bias might extend to sex education, in which male pleasure is emphasized more than female pleasure in heteronormative contexts.39
The association between gender and orgasm rates is often moderated by sexual orientation. While men’s sexual orientation does not seem to affect orgasm rates, lesbian women report higher orgasm rates than heterosexual women and perceive their partners as experiencing more orgasms than heterosexual men.12-14 Lesbian women are more likely to engage in and receive oral sex, with encounters often lasting longer than those of heterosexual women.40 These differences suggest an egalitarian approach to sexual pleasure among lesbian couples, contrasting with heterosexual dynamics. The disparity in orgasm rates, potentially rooted in differing expectations and expressions of sexual intimacy, emphasizes the need to understand how sexual orientation influences orgasm rates from intercourse.
Beyond gender and sexual orientation, age—and the intersection of age, gender, and sexual orientation—may also influence the orgasm gap. Aging introduces physiological and psychological shifts that can affect sexual behavior.41-51 For women, these challenges include hormonal changes impacting sexual functioning and desire. Older women are less likely than older men to engage in sexual activities or to report the desire to do so.44,52 Women also experience a decline in sexual thoughts earlier than men.44 Given that women’s orgasm rates are already lower than men’s, age may further widen the orgasm gap. However, older women report higher levels of sexual satisfaction compared with younger women,52 suggesting that sex may become more pleasurable with age, potentially leading to higher orgasm rates. This implies the orgasm gap between men and women may be smaller in younger age groups.
Current study
We conducted a secondary analysis of data from the annual, cross-sectional Singles in America (SIA) study to examine orgasm rates from sexual intercourse and determine if the orgasm gap persists across adult age groups. Our sample includes nearly 25 000 single (ie, romantically unpartnered) participants, 18 to 100 years of age. While focusing on singles omits adults in romantic relationships, singles constitute approximately one-third of the U.S. adult population,53 making our findings broadly relevant. Sexual satisfaction, including orgasm, remains significant for singles, challenging the notion that orgasm is only relevant within romantic relationships.54-56 Despite focusing solely on singles, the sample’s size and diversity provide a unique perspective on the orgasm gap. This research is the first to explore age’s influence on the orgasm gap, offering insights for further exploration in diverse populations.
Given the lack of studies on the orgasm gap related to age, our study was exploratory, guided by 3 research questions: (1) Is age associated with orgasm rate?; (2) Is age significantly associated with gender and sexual orientation in predicting orgasm rate?; and (3) Does the orgasm gap exist in all adult age groups? These findings enhance our understanding of orgasm experiences throughout the lifespan and provide deeper insights into the factors influencing orgasm rates during sexual intercourse.

Discussion
Using a cross-sectional sample of 24 752 U.S. adults, we explored the persistence of the orgasm gap across the adult lifespan, from emerging (18-24 years of age) to elder adulthood (65+ years of age). Overall, participants reported orgasming from sexual intercourse 65% of the time, with orgasm rates remaining relatively stable across age before accounting for differences by gender and sexual orientation. Results showed the orgasm gap persists across all age groups: men’s orgasm rates ranged from 70% to 85%, while women’s ranged from 46% to 58%. Men reported orgasm rates between 22% and 30% higher than women’s rates.
These findings align with previous research showing higher orgasm rates among men than women12-14 and highlight the stability of this gap with age. The presence of the orgasm gap across adulthood may be attributed to sociocultural influences and norms, such as the undervaluing of women’s sexual satisfaction, biased sexual education, and the emphasis on penetrative sex. These societal attitudes likely shape individuals’ sexual behaviors and expectations, perpetuating gendered dynamics favoring men’s pleasure across the lifespan. Further, inadequate inclusive sexual education overlooks mutual pleasure61 and may reinforce attitudes and behaviors that uphold the orgasm gap.
Our findings on age-related orgasm rates, particularly among different sexual orientations and genders, highlight the complexity of sexual satisfaction. Older gay and bisexual men and lesbian women reported higher orgasm rates than their younger counterparts, suggesting that age may enhance understanding of one’s own sexual needs and preferences. However, the persistent gap between men and women across all ages indicates that age alone does not address the underlying factors contributing to orgasm disparities.
This study also highlights the critical need to include sexual orientation in discussions about sexual pleasure and aging. Sexual minorities are often underrepresented in gerontological research. Studies on lesbian aging indicate that, in response to societal marginalization, these individuals may develop resilient strategies to enhance their well-being, potentially prioritizing sexuality.62,63 In late middle adulthood (50+ years of age), gay and bisexual men reported lower orgasm rates compared with heterosexual men, contrasting with similar rates in younger cohorts. Emerging research suggests that gay men may face unique aging-related stigma, which, combined with physiological aging, could impact sexual opportunities and experiences.64 Further research is needed to explore sexual pleasure and well-being among aging sexual minorities to address these disparities.
Clinical implications
The findings on the association between age and the orgasm gap have significant clinical implications for healthcare providers, therapists, and sex educators. The persistence of the orgasm gap, influenced by sociocultural norms and insufficient sexual education, calls for a more inclusive approach to sexual health discussions and interventions. Clinicians should recognize the diverse experiences of sexual pleasure among different genders and sexual orientations and the unique challenges faced by sexual minorities, especially as they age. Tailoring sexual health education to address the full spectrum of sexual experiences and emphasizing mutual satisfaction and communication could help mitigate the orgasm gap. Additionally, recognizing and addressing the specific needs and challenges of older adults, particularly sexual minorities, is crucial. This involves creating safe spaces for discussions about aging and sexuality, combating stigma, and providing resources to support a fulfilling sexual life into later adulthood. A nuanced understanding of the factors contributing to the orgasm gap can inform targeted interventions to promote sexual well-being for all individuals, regardless of age, gender, or sexual orientation.
Limitations, future directions, and conclusion
Results of this study have notable limitations. The survey had relatively small participation from sexual minorities, particularly in older age groups, limiting some comparisons. The smaller proportion of sexual minority participants also precluded further intersectional analyses with respect to race or ethnicity. Future studies should oversample sexual minority adults, especially in middle and elder adulthood, and aim for larger samples of people of color, as racial differences in the orgasm gap have been observed.16
Further, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the orgasm rate measurement used in this research. Orgasm rates reported here are based on an unvalidated, single-item measure. While single-item measures can demonstrate considerable validity and reliability compared with multi-item measures,65,66 they lack the ability to capture the nuance of participants’ experiences in a multifaceted fashion. Additionally, the absence of validation and reliability testing for this specific measure raises concerns about its appropriateness and suitability for accurately assessing orgasm rates. Future research should incorporate validated and reliable measures of orgasm rate to improve the accuracy and generalizability of findings. Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of the survey prevents tracking individual changes over time. Future researchers should design longitudinal panel studies to observe how specific individuals’ orgasm rates change over their lifespan.
In conclusion, this study confirms the persistent orgasm gap throughout the adult lifespan, tied to a complex web of physiological, psychological, and sociocultural factors. Our findings align with prior literature, showing variability in orgasm rates by gender and sexual orientation. Despite the richness of our data, methodological limitations such as inadequate diversity in participants’ sexual orientation and relationship status, and reliance on cross-sectional and single-item assessments, highlight the need for future longitudinal studies with intentional recruitment around participants’ identities. Such efforts would provide a more detailed understanding of orgasm rates across the lifespan and pave the way for interventions aimed at bridging the orgasm gap.
