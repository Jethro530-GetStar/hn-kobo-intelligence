C# with nothing underneath
A fork of bflat compiles C# straight to a small native program: no .NET runtime, no garbage collector, nothing to install where it runs.
You keep C#'s syntax, type checking and generics. Memory comes from arenas you free all at once, and a small library gives you strings, collections, input and output on those terms. The same source runs on Linux, macOS and Windows, on WebAssembly, and on DOS on a 386.

The language stays, the runtime goes
The compiler still checks everything it checks in .NET. What's missing is what a runtime would do while your program runs.

- Real genericsA specialized copy for each type, and static abstract members.

- Records, tuples, anonymous typesValue equality, with, and the generated ToString.
- Lambdas and queriesWhere, Select and GroupBy, from a small library instead of LINQ.
- ThreadsParallel.For runs a loop on every core.
You do without
- Garbage collectorArenas free their memory all at once, when you say.

Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis
Joint statement by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative.
On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library comprising 17 million lines of code.
We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement.
Our rationale is outlined below.
The codebase depends directly only on Mathlib. Over the past day, part of the Lean and Mathlib communities have rallied to verify the proof's correctness. We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct. The four criteria in https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/ are all satisfied.
- The statement of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct. This statement is part of Mathlib: RiemannHypothesis and has been carefully vetted by experts in Lean and number theory.
- Alpöge’s library compiles correctly. By now, dozens of people have reported correct compilation of the codebase, reproducing the same raw proof export file. The library compiles without errors or build failures.
-
The code proves the correct statement. Crucially, the community has added a file that isolates the statement of the Riemann Hypothesis and imports only Mathlib. That statement was compared against the theorem proved by Alpöge using two independent tools (comparator and con-leche –compare), both confirming that the theorem proved in Alpöge’s library is exactly Mathlib’s statement of the Riemann Hypothesis.
-
The Lean proof is valid. The exported raw proof has been checked by several proof checkers, including Lean’s official kernel, the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and several independent checkers contributed by third parties and implemented in different programming languages. The checkers con-leche and con-ron come with a formal proof, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof, thus addressing concerns that Lean’s logic itself might have flaws that the proof could exploit. An extensive overview is available at https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/. These checks ran on an inert export of the proof, out of reach of hypothetical malicious code in the Lean proof sources.
Thanks are owed to everyone in the community who provided resources for the verification.
We look forward to all the activity in the mathematical community that will help explain the new ideas in this proof.

LLaVA-OneVision 0.5B · camera traps and the Galápagos
Using frontier AI to turn a free vision model into a Galápagos wildlife expert
I gave frontier AI models the weights of a free vision model with half a billion parameters and asked them to make it better at naming animals. They opened it up, worked out what particular weights were doing and wrote new numbers straight in, with no training run. The small model now tells a Galápagos mockingbird from a brown noddy 98% of the time. Google’s SpeciesNet camera-trap classifier has no label for that mockingbird, answers “bird”, and scores 3%.
Mockingbird vs brown noddy98%up from 71%
Land iguana vs green iguana81%up from 50%
Rat photos named as rats81%up from 3%
Camera traps, 45 cameras82%up from 64%
The comparisons use balanced accuracy on test photos kept out of the practice data: each side counts equally, so 50% is a coin flip. On the Galápagos photos SpeciesNet scored 3% on the birds and 12% on the iguanas, because a broad answer like “bird” or “reptile” counts as wrong.
The free model already sees the animals
LLaVA-OneVision is an open vision-language model. You give it a photo and a question, and it writes an answer. It works in three stages. An image encoder called SigLIP turns the photo into a grid of number vectors. A connector, a small two-layer network, translates those vectors for the language model. The language model, Qwen2, then writes the reply one token at a time, where a token is a word or a piece of one.
You can also skip the language model and ask the image encoder directly. SigLIP was trained alongside a text encoder, so a photo and an animal name both become vectors that can be compared. Take the image features for a photo, compare them with the text vectors for each animal name on a list, and pick the closest name. I call this the readout. It needs no labelled examples.
On a first sample of wild Galápagos photos, the readout told Galápagos mockingbirds from other birds 98% of the time, and native land and marine iguanas from the introduced green iguana 80% of the time. Ask the model itself and it calls almost every iguana native. That scores 50%, a coin flip. The knowledge sits in the model’s own image features, and its answers don’t use it.
Google’s SpeciesNet has no label for the Galápagos land iguana, the marine iguana or the Galápagos mockingbirds. On the same photos it mostly answered “reptile”, “bird” or “animal”.
Frontier models debugged it like a program
I gave frontier models the job of getting that knowledge into the answers. Each worked in a Python notebook with the model loaded. It could read every weight, run the model on labelled practice photos, see the score the model gave every possible next token, solve equations and overwrite weights. Gradient training, the usual method that nudges every weight a little at a time from labelled examples, was switched off. Every change is a number a frontier model calculated and wrote in.
The method was built on camera traps first, because they come with a large labelled test: 1,611 photos from 45 cameras. The task is to name the animal in a cropped photo, choosing from that camera’s own list of 2 to 12 animals. A program reads the reply, so it has to be a bare option letter or name.
Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra Pro took turns on this edit, each picking up the weights and notes the last one left. Fable took the first and last turns. The diagram below follows each fix into the model, and the quotes are Fable’s own words from its saved reasoning.
Where each fix sits in the model
The language model has 24 layers, called blocks, that each update the whole sequence of vectors. After the last block, the vector at the end of the sequence is the model’s final state. An output table then scores which token comes next. Each row of that table is a list of 896 numbers for one token. Multiply the row against the final state, add it up, and you have that token’s score.
The finished camera-trap edit changed fifteen rows of the output table, for the stop fix and the letter fix, and three weight arrays in the connector, for the name hint. The image encoder and all 24 language blocks kept their original weights.
The model knew the answer and kept talking
One fox reply began “B. The image shows a fox…” and ran on for a paragraph. B was fox on that camera’s list, so the model had the animal right, and the program still had to reject the reply. Fable read the token scores and saw it:
“the first token is already the right letter in nearly every case.”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
So the problem was stopping. Fable went after the word that kept the reply going:
“I'm considering zeroing the lm_head rows for "The" and " The", since those tokens start the verbose continuations”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
The lm_head is the output table. A row of zeros scores zero, so “The”, with and without a leading space, lost its head start. Then the other half of the fix:
“That suggests the format failures could be avoided if the model stops right after the letter or the name.”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
Fable multiplied the end-of-answer row by 1.5, so stopping scores higher right after the letter. That is three rows. On the 1,611 test photos, unusable replies fell from 237 to zero. Set just those three rows back to their original values and 212 unusable replies return, and the score drops from 81.76% to 72.03%.
Fable wired the animal’s name into the connector
The next fix lets the photo suggest the answer. The language model reads every token as a fixed vector of 896 numbers, called its embedding. Fable fitted, from labelled practice photos and the readout, how strongly the image features point to each of 58 camera-trap animals. It wrote those fits into the connector’s hidden units, the small neurons between its two layers. When a unit responds to a photo, it adds that animal name’s embedding to the vectors going into the language model. Fable described the goal:
“so the language model would read it as if the user had typed the name.”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
Each unit passes its input through GELU, a smooth bend that squashes negative inputs towards zero. A bend would distort the animal score on its way through, so Fable found a pairing that cancels it:
“since GELU(z) − GELU(−z) equals z exactly, two hidden units per channel would give a clean linear pass-through.”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
The hint goes into two fixed positions in the sequence of image vectors, which are slots in the model’s input and not places in the photo. Fable picked units that barely respond to real photos and reused them. Its summary, where projector is another name for the connector:
“I rewired about 120 near-dead projector units so that position emits the token embedding of the predicted animal's name.”Claude Fable 5.2, summary
Then Fable tested the channel by feeding it a lie:
“I'm sketching a constant-hint design where one fixed class is emitted at both cells, so any flip can be traced directly to that hint.”Claude Fable 5.2, reasoning
It wired in a constant “rabbit” hint. 18 of 32 practice photos of other animals flipped to rabbit, where none of the original replies had said rabbit. The hint steers the answer, so the fitted scores decide how good the answers get.
The name becomes this camera’s letter
Fox might be B on one camera and E on the next. The model’s final state carries both the hinted name and the camera’s list from the prompt, so one fixed correction to the letter rows can turn that pair into the right letter.
“A ridge readout of the final hidden state is added to the 11 letter rows.”Claude Fable 5.2, summary
A ridge readout is a least-squares fit with a penalty that keeps the numbers small. Fable fitted it on final states from labelled practice photos and added it to the rows for letters A to K.
Camera traps went from 64% to 82%
On the 1,611 test photos from 45 cameras, balanced accuracy rose from 64.09% to 81.76%, with each camera counting equally. The readout scores 82.79% on the same photos. It is what the image encoder already knows, so the answers now use nearly all of it.
The same construction, aimed at the Galápagos
The Galápagos is where this pays off, because the readout already separates the natives and SpeciesNet has no names for them. OpenAI’s Codex research assistant, a GPT model, built an island edit the same way. It solved ridge regressions from the model’s image features to the readout’s score for each name, on 846 labelled practice photos from Isabela. It wrote the result into the connector as name hints and corrected the letter rows. The 697 test photos came from Santa Cruz and other places the fitting never used.
The edit beat the readout it was fitted from on both iguana comparisons, 81.22% against 78.29% and 80.37% against 77.45%, and tied it on the birds at 98.33%. The unedited model got 0 of 57 green iguanas right. The island edit got 36 of 57 while keeping 60 of 60 land iguanas and 58 of 59 marine iguanas native. It named all 60 brown noddies, against 26 before. Given a list of rodents, it named 88 of 112 invasive rat photos as rats, against 3 before.
One model for both jobs, built two ways
Each edit is a specialist. The island edit scores 72.14% on the camera traps, against 81.76% for the camera-trap edit. So Codex built one model for both jobs two ways, both starting from the camera-trap edit and learning from the same 2,902 labelled practice photos. One was a direct edit, fitted with algebra and written into the connector like the first two. The other was a LoRA fine-tune, the standard way to adapt a model, which uses gradient training on a small add-on to the weights.
Camera traps: 1,611 photos from 45 cameras. Island columns: the 697-photo island test. Bold marks the best of the five LLaVA models in each column. The reference models ran as released, and SpeciesNet was scored on the island test only.
The fine-tune carries both jobs in one model: 87.58% and 91.69% on the iguanas, 93.33% on the birds and 88.20% on rats, with camera traps at 79.22%. The direct edit for both kept camera traps at 80.70% and reached 55.49% on the iguanas and 17.70% on rats. It took about an hour to build, against about three hours of fine-tune training, both on one desktop computer. So a direct edit is the quick fix for one job, and a few hours of fine-tuning packs several jobs into one model.
A free, general model with half a billion parameters, edited by frontier AI with no training run, scores 81.76% on the camera traps, level within noise with the 84.07% of BioCLIP 2, the open image model built to name species and trained on photos across the tree of life. I ran BioCLIP 2 as released on the same photos. On the island test it scores 94.49% and 94.48% on the iguanas, 100.00% on the birds and 83.29% on rats, having trained on 399 of the 684 iNaturalist photos in that test. It is a stronger pair of eyes than the SigLIP encoder inside this model, and putting it inside the language model is my next experiment.
What the frontier models did, and what I did
What still gets me is the reasoning. Fable saw from token scores that the letter was already right and went after the stop, then proved its name channel worked by feeding it a deliberate lie. I didn’t hand-write a single weight. My part was asking questions and checking the numbers. Frontier AI turns the rest of us into model debuggers.
Most open vision-language models are built from the same three parts, an image encoder, a connector and a language model. You can point a frontier model at the one you have, for your place or your problem, and ask what it already knows.
The code, weight changes and saved answers are on GitHub, the four models are on Hugging Face, and the paper has the full method and every number.
