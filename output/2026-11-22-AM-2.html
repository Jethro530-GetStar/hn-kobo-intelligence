A pill that can shrink and potentially eliminate pancreas tumours is being hailed as the biggest breakthrough treatment for the cancer in decades.
In one in 12 of the patients given the drug, daraxonrasib, during a trial, the cancer became undetectable, and the tumours stopped growing in almost all of the 61 patients. The trial participants had recently been diagnosed with advanced pancreatic cancer that had spread to other parts of the body.
Though doctors have said the pill cannot yet be considered a cure, and they do not know how long it can keep the disease at bay, they have said it is encouraging news because it appears it will offer patients more time, even if it does not cause full remission.
Half of all people diagnosed with pancreatic cancer die within three months, and only about 3% of patients survive for five years when the disease spreads to other organs. It has the lowest survival rate of all common cancers in the UK and has a similarly poor prognosis globally.
Patients were followed for a year and a half during the trial on average and some did not see the cancer return during this period. Longer studies will be conducted to see if any of the cancers can be put into remission. During the trial, tumours stopped growing in 90% of patients, just over 60% of patients had their tumours shrink or disappear, and for about 8% of patients tumours became undetectable on CT scans.
A year after starting treatment, 76.6% of patients were alive; usually 40-45% of patients given chemotherapy alone survive a year.
Dr Wungki Park, a gastrointestinal medical oncologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York, who presented the findings, said “this is the closest to cure”, adding that he had never seen so many tumours disappear in patients whose cancer had spread.
He presented the findings at the EORTC-NCI-AACR Symposium on Molecular Targets and Cancer Therapeutics in Barcelona. “We are beginning to see not only a higher percentage of tumour response rates, but also deeper responses and a near doubling in the duration of disease control, which may eventually translate into longer survival,” he said.
“The median follow-up is 18 months. Just imagine, in an average of 18 months your disease just disappears on the CT scan … that’s closest to complete remission … it’s incredible.”
Daraxonrasib is a KRAS inhibitor treatment, which works by locking on to and shutting off certain mutations found in most pancreatic cancers. These mutations drive tumour growth.
There are hopes the treatment could work for dozens of other cancers because they can have similar mutations.
Michelle Mitchell, Cancer Research UK’s chief executive, said more research was needed, but added: “This is the biggest leap forward we’ve seen in decades for pancreatic cancer patients and could offer real hope to people facing one of the deadliest and most difficult-to-treat cancers.
“These results raise the exciting possibility that this breakthrough could eventually pave the way for treating other cancers that carry Kras gene mutations. More trials are now needed before this treatment can be assessed for use on the NHS and despite this advance, pancreatic cancer survival is still very low.”

Introduction
Nothing really exists in isolation. Almost every part of our lives exists in a network. Consciousness arises from what neurons do together, life from what genes and proteins do together, society from what we do together. When researchers began mapping real-world networks in the 1990s – from the World Wide Web to Hollywood actors linked by films – they found that wildly different systems shared architectural features that their components alone could not explain. This suggested common organizing principles for how networks form and develop.
In this episode, two of the pioneers of network science – Albert-László Barabási and co-host Steven Strogatz – discuss how the field took shape, the ideas that underpin it, and why this is more than graph theory under a different name. The conversation then turns to health and nutrition. Disease-related genes cluster in “network neighborhoods”, Barabási explains, which may help identify better drug targets and predict which patients will respond to which drugs. That work led him to his Foodome project, which catalogs the molecules found in the food we eat and asks what they do inside our cells. By his count, around 135,000 compounds lie outside conventional nutrition, which concerns itself mainly with energy, building blocks, and vitamins. These compounds, which he calls the “dark matter of nutrition,” and their effects on the body are only beginning to be understood.

Written by the speakers and attendees of AIE Code Summit 2026
In 2001, 17 software engineers and leaders got together at a ski lodge in Snowbird, Utah to understand the biggest challenge of their time: heavyweight, documentation-driven software development processes.
Out of this we got the Agile Manifesto, four values and twelve principles that changed how most of the industry builds software.
Today we convene to build our own manifesto - the No AI Slop manifesto.
AI increasingly helps us write our code, docs, plans, and messages. We've all been on the receiving end of slop, and most of us have sent some too. Through this work, we have come to value:
Outcomes over output
Understanding over speed
Our own thinking over a model's first draft
Better work over easier work
Improving the system over patching the output
The world moves especially fast these days, but the goal of these resolutions is to embrace our important human role in harnessing AI safely, responsibly, in a way that creates value for everyone, and that will be relevant for years to come.
We believe:
- AI is the most useful tool most of us have ever worked with, but a swarm of agents can produce more work in a single day than a person can review in our lifetime.
- Slop is when we put less effort into producing something than we ask the consumer to spend understanding it.
- Using AI doesn't necessarily make something slop. Slop happens when we pass AI output along without taking responsibility for the quality or the reader's experience.
- AI cannot replace human judgement, it can only amplify our judgement (or lack thereof).
- Slop is asymmetric. The cost of producing a thousand-line PR or a ten-page doc is approaching zero, while the cost of reviewing it remains constant.
- In code, even before AI, slop proliferates when we repeatedly work around problems instead of solving them correctly. It comes from tools that lean HEAVILY toward adding rather than subtracting.
- A lot of slop comes from pressure. Companies are pushing their people to redefine what is humanly possible.
- Slop compounds. The more slop sits in our knowledge base, in chat, in code, the more of it the next agent reads and copies.
- The more slop flying around a team, the more we lose the will to fight it. Communication and quality get worse over time, not better.
Thus we the undersigned resolve that:
- Human time and attention is incredibly valuable, so we optimize for using it wisely.
- We fight slop because AI should make our work better, not just easier.
- We optimize systems, tools, and products for efficient elicitation of human intent and for efficient human understanding.
- We do our own thinking when working with a model. We explore resources, code, and prior art to truly understand the problem before the work starts.
- We own the intent, taste, and consequences of what we ship, however much AI helps us make it.
- When an agent wrote something we're sending, we make that clear, and we check the parts that matter before it goes out.
- When someone sends us slop, we tell them, kindly, and ask them to review it first. Most of us have needed that same note at some point.
- There will always be alpha in reviewing something. As models improve, we may stop reading most of the code. We may still review a plan, design, or result, because if we don't, we just get the same output as everyone else.
- Agent reviews, linters, and verifiers are useful but not sufficient. For now, we still review at least some subset of the outputs. What subset depends on the stakes.
- What shipped, who's using it, and how it's helping them matters FAR MORE than lines of code, prs merged, tokens spent, or the number of agents running in parallel.
- Unreviewed output isn't always necessarily bad. One-shot prompts and vibe coding are high leverage tools. "Slop" is bad when it undermines our outcome -- a useful product, a maintainable system, or saving ourselves and our teammates time and energy.
- When a mistake shows up twice, we scale ourselves and our team by improving the system and its guardrails. Anything a linter, type checker, or automated check can catch, we let it catch.
And finally:
- None of us has this figured out. We're each relearning what we will and won't accept, and there's no single right answer.
Again, the world moves especially fast these days, but the goal of these resolutions is to embrace our important human role in harnessing AI safely, responsibly, in a way that creates value for everyone, and that will be relevant for years to come.
We look forward to working with the community to evolve these beliefs as the world changes. Comments and feedback on this document are welcome. What is slop? Why does slop happen? Why is slop worth fighting? How do we fight slop?
