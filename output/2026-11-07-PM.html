I love open-source software and open protocols, but my family and friends primarily communicate through SMS for exactly the reasons in https://xkcd.com/2365/
Whenever XMPP or Matrix threads pop up on HN, several commenters casually mention their "friends and family XMPP (or Matrix) server."
How did you convince friends and family to move away from SMS/WhatsApp and replace it with a niche messaging app?
My family and friends aren't hardcore nerdy, but they're more tech-savvy than average. Their needs in messaging are mainly pragmatic: they want ease of use, reliability, and fun features. They don't care that much about privacy, decentralization, or extensibility (though I care about those things a lot).
I've been experimenting with self-hosting XMPP and Matrix servers and clients in the past week, and the UX is even worse than I expected. It seems like all the clients either imitate Slack, imitate Discord, or imitate hot garbage. I haven't seen any that try to be a simple, friendly replacement for SMS. I'm a software developer who uses computers all day, and I find the UX for most of the clients incredibly hard to use.
The only angle I could see to pitch XMPP/Matrix to people for non-philosophical reasons are that there's a more consistent experience across iOS/Android. The biggest shortcoming I think is visible to regular users is that there's feature degradation and missing information when iOS users have SMS conversations with Android users, especially in group chats.
For people who have successfully won family and friends to XMPP or Matrix, what was your pitch? What do you recommend others do to make XMPP or Matrix an attractive experience for a regular person?

6. Tesla to wind down Cybertruck production
FULL TEXT
1083 points · 1939 comments · PLenz
nbcnews.com
Best #2 · Top #7
Original article · HN discussion
Article
Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production at its Texas gigafactory around mid-2027, CEO Elon Musk said on Friday, after years of persistently weak sales for a futuristic model once touted as a rival to legacy pickup trucks that ultimately only sold in just a handful of markets because of its heavy, angular design.
Musk said in an X post the production wind-down would enable the automaker to accommodate increased output of its newly launched Cybercab model, which the billionaire has made central to Tesla's robotaxi ambitions.
While it was not immediately clear whether Cybertruck production would be moved to another facility or phased out entirely, the automaker's Austin plant is the only known Tesla facility equipped to manufacture the model's specialized components.
In response to a user on X, Musk called the Cybertruck the "best vehicle Tesla has ever made" and the wind-down the "end of an era."
Tesla did not immediately respond to a Reuters request for comment sent outside regular business hours.
The US EV maker only began delivering the Cybertruck to customers in late 2023. According to Kelley Blue Book, the company only sold 20,237 Cybertrucks in the US last year, down 48% from 38,965 in 2024. Before the pickup truck launched, Musk had predicted annual sales of 250,000 units.
"It doesn't make sense to keep it going when it's not meeting its targets," said former Aston Martin CEO Andy Palmer. "Every rational auto guy would say the same."
"It will no doubt only be a rounding error on Tesla's balance sheet, but the statistics speak for themselves."
Right from its unveiling in 2019, the Cybertruck was a controversial choice for Musk. Its angular design was mocked by pickup truck purists and safety experts predicted it would be hard to gain approval for sale in key markets like Europe.
The unveiling event itself was marred when Musk tried to prove that its windows were shatterproof by having metal balls thrown at them - only to leave massive cracks in two of them.
During Cybertruck's official launch in 2023, Musk touted it as a futuristic competitor to pickup trucks from General Motors and Ford, saying "finally, the future will look like the future."
But the vehicle has struggled to meet those expectations.
Automotive analyst Automotive analyst Felipe Munoz said that although the Cybertruck has been called a failure because of its poor sales, electric pick-ups from Ford and others have also flopped in the US market.
"All of them lost the competition because electric pick ups aren't popular in the US," Munoz said.
But Cybertruck's sharp-edged stainless-steel design limited its prospects in markets like Europe where EVs are more popular. Regulatory approval was impossible for Europe because of rules there concerning pedestrian impact.
"Anywhere where you have crash regulation that takes into account pedestrian impact, that Cybertruck won't go, can't go," former Aston Martin CEO Palmer said.
The Cybercab, which has butterfly doors and no steering wheel, pedals or mirrors, is one of Musk's key bets in his push to turn Tesla into an AI and robotics company rather than just an automaker.
Musk has said he sees it as Tesla's main autonomous model supplying a global network of robotaxis.
After years of delays to Musk's self-driving targets, Tesla began offering paid rides in a small fleet of Cybercabs in Austin in September. However, the automaker still has to answer the US auto safety agency's questions on whether it has properly self-certified Cybercab to demonstrate compliance with federal motor vehicle safety standards.
"Cybertruck was an incredible technology development platform and lives on through Cybercab," Cybertruck lead engineer Wes Morrill said on X.

Article
I was thinking about why this question bothered me so much.
And I think the reason is twofold:
- The speaker is implying that Grok is some sort of “truth Oracle”, rather than a highly trained stochastic parrot.
- The speaker is implying that anyone, or anything is capable of simply delivering The Truth to them in this manner.
Well, Truth doesn’t work that way!
In most cases, it’s not something that’s just given to you “for free”. It’s something you have to work hard for to obtain, because even when it is given to you for free, you still need to verify it for yourself somehow or otherwise it’s hearsay that you’ve chosen to blindly believe.
And when people imply that these autocomplete robots can give them the Truth, it’s not just wrong but dangerous.
I don’t like it when a bunch of fools are running around trying to turn Grok, or any other LLM, into a Ministry of Truth.
Why aren’t these people asking me, or anyone else, whether something is true? Not that I want you to do that, but note how all of a sudden there are thousands of accounts asking Grok whether something is true.
Could this be some sort of psyop? An attempt to condition the herd to replace their own brain with Grok’s? If so, that can certainly be a step on a road to turning the bot that — contrary to xAI’s advertising, doesn’t actually understand a thing — into Judge, Jury, and potentially even executioner.
We can stop this worrying trend with a simple modification to this question. Instead of deifying corporate LLMs, try something like, “Grok, what do you think?”
