Article
Hey HN, I'm Christian and this is a new OSS project I've been noodling on for the last couple of weeks. Hob is a free, MIT-licensed desktop app for Mac, Windows and Linux that works on your files the way the Claude and ChatGPT desktop apps do, except the model runs on your own computer. It uses open-weight models (currently Gemma 4 and gpt-oss). All your chats and files never leave your system. The only things that go online are web search (can be turned off in settings) and any MCP connectors you add yourself.
Main features:
- Automatically finds the best open-weight model for your hardware on first install (Mac/Linux/Windows)
- Writes sandboxed Python code for ad hoc tools (data processing, document conversion, graphs etc.)
- Built-in web search (powered by SearXNG)
- MCP support
- Image recognition
- CRUD to your local filesystem
- Works fully offline with no internet
Before building this I tried every local AI app I could find, but it seemed each one had different problems. The main thing I noticed is that none of them could do what the ChatGPT and Claude apps do with your files, e.g. read and change files in a folder, look at images, run code in a sandbox, draw a chart. The most advanced thing any of them did was paste a plain text file into a small context window, which basically failed as soon as you had a file larger than a few KB. I couldn't find anything that could easily parse images and bigger PDFs.
The OSS solutions that were more advanced all seemed to default to hosted models (either OSS or closed), which defeats the entire point of the privacy that on-device OSS offers. They also seemed to push towards an account/open-core style business.
I build it using Electron bridged with llama.cpp in its own process. On initial load, the system will try and figure out if you have a compatible graphics card and if not default to CPU-only (I ran into a lot of issues of graphics card beeing seemingly compatible but then crashing on first query, there's a whole saga to this lol). Code execution happens in a sandbox that can only write to the chat's folder and has no network unless asked (sandbox-exec on macOS, Landlock on Linux and a low-integrity process on Windows). Images go to a small Qwen3-VL model. Dictation is a local Whisper model.
The hardest part was definitely to get the smaller models to use tools reliably. Ran into a ton of issues of 4B models happily saying "here's your chart" without ever calling the chart tool or get stuck in an infinite call loop. I spent a fair amount of the work building guards vs tweaking prompts (that didn't really work for small models).
I understand the pushback that local models are weaker than frontier cloud models. This is true, but for most tasks for most normal people, I'd argue you rarely need the newest frontier model. From my own evals, systems with 16+ GB of RAM do really well, and if you have a GPU or more RAM, you get very close to what you'll get out of the box with a frontier hosted model.
Excited to hear what you think and see how it runs on other systems!

What it is
The Midnight-Molad Calendar is the liturgical reckoning of the Carpocratian Church of Commonality and Equality—a simplified, purely computational lunisolar calendar that joins three traditions: the Hebrew lunisolar year, the Roman civil day, and the Chinese mean-month rule. Molad (מולד) is the Hebrew word for “birth,” specifically the birth of the new moon. In this calendar, months follow the new moon and days turn over at civil midnight.
The Nativity
The Church holds as a matter of faith that Jesus of Nazareth was a Human Being born the ordinary way on 25 Kislev 3757. Historians typically date his birth between 6 and 4 BCE. Year 1 Anno Nativitatis (AN) begins on 1 Tishri 3757, which is 30 August 5 BCE in the proleptic Gregorian calendar; the earlier months of 5 BCE belong to year 0 AN. Years are numbered astronomically, so the year before 1 AN is 0 AN, and the one before that is −1 AN. In the Common Era, the AN year runs five years ahead of the Gregorian year from 1 Tishri to 31 December, and four years ahead from 1 January until the next 1 Tishri. 25 Kislev of year 1 is Dies Nativitatis (DN) 0: Thursday, 21 November 5 BCE (23 November in the Julian calendar). The classical Hebrew calendar puts 25 Kislev 3757 two days later, on a Saturday.
Comparison to the Roman Civil Calendar
Days turn over at civil midnight rather than sunset, as the Roman civil day did (Varro, quoted in Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 3.2). The date is the local civil date wherever you stand, as with the Gregorian calendar. Only the heavens are placed by a fixed meridian: each mean new moon and each mean equinox is converted to Jerusalem mean time (Universal Time UT plus 2 h 20 m 51 s) and belongs to the civil day on which it falls there. An instant at exactly midnight belongs to the day that begins.
Comparison to the Hebrew Ritual Calendar
Our calendar keeps the skeleton of the historical Hebrew calendar (twelve or thirteen months from Tishri to Elul, with Adar doubled in a leap year). The year begins on 1 Tishri, and each month begins at the midnight that opens the civil day of its own mean new moon, so every month has 29 or 30 days. Nothing is spread across the months to make the year fit. A common year has 354 or 355 days, and a leap year 383 or 384.
Unlike the classical Metonic Hebrew calendar, leap years are determined directly by the spring equinox rather than by the fixed nineteen-year cycle. In each spring, the Nisan lunation is the first lunation whose fifteenth day—the civil day of its mean new moon plus fourteen—falls on or after the civil day of the mean equinox; a tie counts. We use a mean equinox year of 365.242374 days, counted from the mean March equinox of 20 March 2026 at 14:46 UT. The year begins six lunations after the Nisan lunation of the preceding spring, and it is a leap year, with thirteen months, exactly when thirteen lunations rather than twelve separate its beginning from the next. The extra month is Adar I, placed before Adar, which is then called Adar II.
This calendar does not postpone the New Year (Rosh Hashanah) to keep it off Sunday, Wednesday, or Friday, because we do not practice the Sabbath. The New Year falls on whichever weekday its new moon does. Most of the time the result is a New Year and month starts that differ from the synagogue’s by a day or two. In about one year in five, the New Years stand about a month apart.
Comparison to the Han Dynasty Calendars
From antiquity through the Han dynasty, Chinese astronomers began every lunar month on the day of the mean new moon, a method called pingshuo (平朔), and they kept doing so until 619, when the Tang dynasty adopted the true new moon. We follow the older method, so that anyone can compute the calendar without an almanac. Our mean lunation is the modern value, 29.530588861 days, counted from the mean new moon of 6 January 2000 at about 14:21 UT.

Are you tired of your screenshares looking like this?

You need WHIP.
Did you know OBS or FFmpeg can go directly into a video conference? You configure everything, so you control exactly the quality you are going to get. WHIP support means these tools now speak the protocol of video conferencing.
Help! My video conferencing provider doesn't support WHIP. What can I do?
Send them an angry email. Tell them you don't want their crappy proprietary client. You want open standards that let you bring the tools you prefer.
FAQ
What is WHIP?
WHIP is an open standard for sending live video to a service using WebRTC. Your provider gives you a server URL and a stream key, called a bearer token. Before to join a conference call each client had to run custom code. That means FFmpeg/OBS would never be able to join.
Why is my screenshare blurry?
A lack of control. Your browser is picking a bitrate, codec or resolution that doesn't match what you need.
How are OBS and FFmpeg different from browser screensharing?
OBS and FFmpeg let you choose your resolution, frame rate, bitrate, and codec. You can tune those settings for the content you're sharing and the hardware and connection you have.
What about OBS Virtual Camera?
OBS Virtual Camera sends your OBS scene to the conferencing app as a webcam. You can pick the layout of your stream, but the conferencing app still handles encoding.

I have written a lot about reasoning models here over the last two years on Ahead of AI, including a recent article on mechanisms to control reasoning effort levels in contemporary LLMs. Reasoning models are still as relevant as ever. In fact, almost all current-generation LLMs today are reasoning models or offer a reasoning mode.
For those who are interested in how reasoning models work, including inference scaling, reinforcement learning, and distillation, I wrote my recent Build Reasoning Model (From Scratch), which implements these from the ground up in PyTorch, which, in my experience, is still the best way to understand and learn how things really work.
Now, in the last few months, I also sat down and recorded accompanying lectures I wanted to share with you. So, below, you will find eight reasoning model-from-scratch coding videos (11 hours of content).
We start with a pre-trained LLM and implement methods to evaluate and improve it in Python and PyTorch. So, if you enjoyed my Coding LLMs from the Ground Up course last year, this is essentially the sequel (but you can also watch it without the prior course as a prerequisite).

How LLMs, reasoning models, and agents relate and fit together
As a small side note, and since I often get asked how reasoning models relate to today’s AI agents, here’s a short section on this before getting started with the videos.
A reasoning model is essentially an upgrade from a conventional LLM, that is, adding more post-training via reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (and optionally inference scaling techniques) to make it more capable of solving more challenging problems. Classically, reasoning models are developed for math and code, but the capabilities can generalize to other problem domains as well.
A reasoning model then functions as the engine inside an agent harness, where the agent harness is a software or tooling framework around the LLM/reasoning model to tackle even more challenging problems (bigger coding tasks, travel booking, and all other sorts of personal assistant tasks). The task of the harness is sophisticated context management and giving the LLM access to tools.
Figure 1: An engine analogy for conventional LLMs, reasoning models, and agent harnesses. Figure from my Components of a Coding Agent article.
In this analogy depicted in the figure above, the model is the engine, and the agent harness adds the machinery that lets us use it for various things (as an upgrade from a simple chatbot interface). An agent can use either a conventional LLM or a reasoning model, but reasoning models are the more typical choice as they are more capable. (As mentioned above, most current-gen LLMs are reasoning models with variable reasoning effort settings.)
So, the bottom line is that reasoning models, as the “engine,” remain an important part of understanding modern LLM agents.

4. Inference-time scaling: sampling and self-consistency
Now we explore ways to improve answers while keeping the model weights fixed. But before implementing specific inference-scaling techniques, we first implement methods to generate diverse answers from the same prompt (this is standard practice in proprietary LLM chatbots, such as ChatGPT).
Concretely, we implement temperature scaling, multinomial sampling, and top-p filtering to generate diverse responses. Then we combine multiple responses through self-consistency, using majority voting over their final answers.
We also examine the tradeoff between answer accuracy and the extra computation needed to generate more candidates.
Topics covered
- Comparing how additional compute benefits model performance during training and during inference
- A gentle intro to inference-scaling through chain-of-thought prompts
- Implementing temperature scaling and multinomial sampling to generate diverse answers
- Adding top-p filtering to control the quality of the diverse answers
- Combining candidate answers through self-consistency, one of the most popular inference scaling techniques
- Evaluating MATH-500 accuracy against the cost of additional inference compute

Building on the previous video, we implement scoring functions to compare multiple answers generated from the same prompt. We can also use these scoring methods to break ties in self-consistency (covered in the previous lecture).
The scoring methods covered here include computing token and sequence log probabilities (this will also be relevant in the later reinforcement learning chapters). We then implement a self-refinement loop in which the model critiques an answer and attempts a revision.
The evaluation lets us check how useful those additional steps are for our model.

6. Reinforcement learning: implementing GRPO for RLVR
In this lecture, we move on to implementing a training technique called Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). We first implement a simplified version of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), generate groups of responses, score them with our verifier, and use their relative rewards for the model weight updates during training.
Topics covered

Finally, we explore another way to train a small reasoning model, that is, learning from answers generated by a stronger (and bigger) teacher model. We discuss different forms of distillation, prepare reasoning data, and implement the supervised training loop.
