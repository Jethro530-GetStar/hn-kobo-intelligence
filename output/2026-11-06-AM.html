Pressure testing the Pyramid-JiT (P-JiT)
For us, this $21K training run is a proof-of-concept along the way to our next video model. We were surprised by how good the results looked given the size of the model, so we thought we'd share it with the world as an experimental release.
If you haven't followed our work previously, we'd love to take a step back and introduce ourselves. We're a 2-person research lab training our own generative video models entirely from scratch. The ultimate ambition is creative tools that democratize access to animation. You can read more about that here.
Today, generative video models have two major flaws: they are too slow/expensive and they lack proper controls. Creativity is an iterative process. You make, you tweak, you get better. With this double whammy, video models (as they stand today) are effectively useless for storytelling.
When we set out to build Linum v3 earlier this year, the primary goal was to bring the cost envelope of inference down significantly. If our model is fast and cheap, it's the first step (of many) towards useful creative tools.
We've written extensively about the steps we've taken in this direction, but here's the long story short:
- The bottleneck for training and inference for video is attention, O(n²). If we can compress more aggressively, we'll reduce the context window for attention, slashing the cost of training and inference.
- Typically, compression is handled by a separate specialized model like a Variational Autoencoder, but that's not necessarily optimal. VAEs seem to have an empirical ceiling on the amount of compression they can achieve. More importantly, improving the VAE on its task of reconstruction can produce worse generations in the downstream diffusion model.
- If you get rid of the VAE altogether, you should be able to compress more aggressively and learn faster. Diffusion models are a more powerful family of generative models than VAEs. We should be able to use them to compress and generate at the same time. Instead of using another model's latent space, the diffusion model should learn a latent space purpose-built for its use case. In turn, we should be able to squeeze more juice out of this compression.
In our last blog, we iterated towards a new architecture that we called the Pyramid-JiT (P-JiT). It's a pixel-space model that converges in 11× fewer samples than Linum v2 with 4× more aggressive token reduction (i.e., we represent a 512² image in the same number of tokens we used to represent a 256² image).
For the details on how we got there, we'd recommend you take a look at our previous blogs on JiT-DDT and P-JiT. But at a high level, we found a noise schedule for pixel-space models that helps the models better learn fine-grained detail. And, we added readouts along the trunk of the DiT.
Adjacent work in REPA, RAE-v2, and Self-Flow all point to weak gradients in the early blocks of the DiT. By operating in pixel space, every block of our DiT is quite literally predicting a version of the final image, so we can boost gradient signal earlier in the network by predicting low-resolution versions of the final image. This helps accelerate convergence.
The architecture did well in our initial experiments, but we wanted to see how it performed when we trained it for longer (i.e., ~600M samples instead of 50–100M samples). It's a pressure test of sorts along the way towards larger models (and video).
Training Regime
Text and image each pass through two single-modality "refiner" DiT blocks, then merge into one stream of 22 identical DiT blocks. The text representation comes from a frozen Qwen3.5-4B model, which we read at layers 7, 15 and 27 (its three full-attention layers). We concatenate and project these representations into the text refiner. The image is split into 32×32 patches and compressed into a 1024-dimensional space, before being projected up into the image refiner blocks.
At block 5, we have a 2-layer PixelREPA readout that aligns the model's latent space to DINOv3-L. We have two linear readout heads along the trunk after blocks 10 and 16. As discussed earlier, these predict the clean image at a quarter and half resolution, respectively. The total loss is given by MSE at the readouts, PixelREPA cosine-distance, MSE at the final output head, and perceptual losses on the full resolution image (LPIPS and P-DINO).
Every DiT block is conditioned on the timestep through a low-rank AdaLN, following Z-Image. The timestep embedding goes through one down-projection to 256-dim. Each block then has its own up-projection to a scale and a gate, zero-initialized so every block starts as the identity.
As described in our last blog, we employ a curriculum over the noise schedule. We start concentrated at high noise for the first 80M samples, so the model learns basic structure. Then, we widen the noise distribution for the remaining training time so it can learn fine-grained details. See Appendix A for details.
We start EMA at 100M samples, so the model has already learned decent representations of structure and detail. When we switch to 1024², we start with a fresh optimizer and resume training from the 512² EMA weights. At 1024² we double the noise scale from 2 to 4 to account for the redundancy of information (see JiT, Appendix A). The higher noise level and need to reproduce fine detail exactly at high resolution result in a higher overall loss, even as image quality improves rapidly. We use RoPE interpolation in the resolution transition. Moreover, we start the 1024² EMA 2M samples in, so it has adjusted to the new resolution before we start the averaging process.
For post-training, we used a variant of Finite Difference Flow Optimization (FDFO) with JEV-Image and human-in-the-loop judges.
To be blunt, post-training is a very young discipline within diffusion models, and we've only seen the first sparks of it working in the past few months.
Of course there's been a lot of work in model and schedule distillation, but when it comes to post-training in terms of improving fundamental model capabilities (like we've seen with coding in LLMs over the past 18 months), it hasn't really come to fruition just yet for diffusion.
DPO has been around for a while, but tends to dramatically overfit to specific aesthetic choices. Flow-GRPO was one of the first articulations of bringing RLVR-style frameworks to diffusion, and that only came out last fall. Since then there have been a few papers like Diffusion NFT and this June's FDFO (the best articulation of diffusion post-training so far, in our opinion).
We think that this difficulty in adapting techniques comes down to the fact that we don't have access to strong likelihood estimates in diffusion models like we have in auto-regressive LLMs. We'll dive more into these concerns and our full post-training stack in the coming weeks.
Model Limitations
This model was not trained with images of rendered text (no infographics, PDFs, pages of books, PowerPoint slides), so it cannot write text or generate posters. As we detailed in our data filtration blog, we don't consider this data useful for our ultimate goal of animation tools, so this text-heavy data has been filtered out entirely from our dataset.
This is a small model (2B-parameter DiT, 4B text encoder), and accordingly there are a few areas where the model struggles: full body, many entities (e.g. 6 cars, crowds), multi-person interactions, and world knowledge. The model is capable of correctly generating these images on occasion. Training a larger model (e.g. 7B parameters) and further post-training would likely mitigate these issues.

Security advisory: openssh-server password authentication bypass
If you run sshd on Alpine Linux, update openssh now and restart sshd.
Upgrading the package alone does not replace the running daemon.
If you cannot update yet, at least disable password logins: set
PasswordAuthentication no in your sshd configuration and restart sshd.
Either one closes the hole.
What is the problem?
An attacker who can reach your SSH port can log in without knowing any password. It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries.
The attacker needs the name of an account whose password field is *
(an account with no password set, which is normal for key-only accounts), and
needs the machine to have at least one other account with a $5$ or $6$
password hash. The attacker then sends a very long password (257 to 1024
bytes). One user with a password is enough: it makes every account without
one reachable.
Details are in the OpenSSH 10.6p2 release notes. No CVE identifier had been assigned at the time of writing.
Who is affected?
You are affected if all of the following are true:
- You run the Alpine openssh-server package, which is built without PAM, or
you run openssh-server-pam with UsePAM no. (openssh-server-pam with UsePAM yes is not affected.)
- PasswordAuthentication is yes (the OpenSSH default, unless you changed it).
- The machine has an account whose password field is *. This includes:
- root, which ships as root:* in the base image since about Alpine 3.16.
OpenSSH’s default PermitRootLogin prohibit-password protects root, so
root is only reachable if PermitRootLogin yes is set. That does not help if
another account that can log in is able to become root, for example
through doas.
- Any key-only user created with adduser without a password, or with usermod -p '*'.
- Users coming from a directory service through musl-nscd (nslcd, winbind, sssd), since they have no shadow entry.
- The alpine user in the official tiny-cloud images (see below).
- At least one local account has a $5$ or $6$ password hash.
Affected releases: every Alpine release we checked, 3.21 through 3.24 and edge, and the older releases that no longer receive regular updates (3.16 to 3.20).
Who is not affected?
- Docker and other container users that do not run sshd in the
container.
- Systems with PasswordAuthentication no.
- Systems using openssh-server-pam with UsePAM yes (with UsePAM no you are affected).
- Locked accounts: those with ! as the password field in /etc/shadow (a line starting user:!:). They cannot log in with a password or a key.
- Systems where no account has a $-style password hash.
- Cloud images configured through cloud-init, which writes
PasswordAuthentication no.
How can I fix it?
1. Update
apk upgrade -U -a
Alpine 3.21.10, 3.22.8, 3.23.8 and 3.24.4 contain the fixed package (see the release announcement). The fixed package versions are:
| Branch | Fixed openssh version |
|---|---|
| edge | 10.6_p2-r0 |
| 3.24 | 10.3_p1-r2 |
| 3.23 | 10.2_p1-r1 |
| 3.22 | 10.0_p1-r11 |
| 3.21 | 9.9_p2-r1 |
| 3.20 | 9.7_p1-r6 |
| 3.19 | 9.6_p1-r3 |
| 3.18 | 9.3_p2-r4 |
| 3.17 | 9.1_p1-r7 |
| 3.16 | 9.0_p1-r6 |
Restart sshd after upgrading (rc-service sshd restart). Upgrading the
package does not restart a running sshd, so you must do it after.
2. If you cannot update: disable password authentication
Before you start, make sure your SSH key login works, and keep your current session open until you have tested a new login.
Run these as root. The first two commands create a drop-in file. Alpine’s
default sshd_config reads that directory from 3.19 onward; older releases
ignore it. The sed command changes yes to no on every
PasswordAuthentication line in /etc/ssh/sshd_config, whatever the spacing,
including lines inside a Match block. It also adds PasswordAuthentication no at the top of the file, for the case where there was no such line, and
saves the original as /etc/ssh/sshd_config.bak. The last two commands check
the configuration, restart the daemon, and show the result:
mkdir -p /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d
echo 'PasswordAuthentication no' > /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/00-no-passwords.conf
sed -i.bak -e '1i PasswordAuthentication no' \
-e 's/^\(\s*PasswordAuthentication[^y#]*\)yes/\1no/i' \
/etc/ssh/sshd_config
sshd -t && rc-service sshd restart
sshd -T | grep -i passwordauthentication
The last command should print passwordauthentication no. To undo, delete
/etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/00-no-passwords.conf and copy
/etc/ssh/sshd_config.bak back over /etc/ssh/sshd_config.
Cloud images (tiny-cloud)
The official tiny-cloud images (the qcow2, VHD and AMI artifacts) from
3.18.11 through 3.24.3 ship an alpine user with a * password field and
passwordless doas. They are safe on first boot. They become exploitable as
soon as any $6$ hash exists on the machine, for example when an admin sets a
console password, or user-data adds a user with hashed_passwd. An attacker
then gets a shell as alpine and can use doas to become root.
Images built from 3.21.10, 3.22.8, 3.23.8, 3.24.4 and later include the fixed
package. Images from earlier point releases do not, so on every running
instance, update openssh or disable password authentication.
Releases that are no longer supported (3.21 and older)
These releases have the same bug. 3.21 reached its end of support on 2026-11-01, but we have published fixed packages for each release all the way back to 3.16.
How do I know if I was attacked?
Look in /var/log/messages for sshd lines reading Accepted password for
an account that has no password (alpine, root, or any key-only user).
Review doas use and recently created users, files and authorized keys on
exposed machines. If you cannot rule out a login, treat the machine as
compromised.
How could this happen?
When sshd is built without PAM, it checks a password by hashing the attempt
with crypt() (the C library function that turns a password into its stored
hash) and comparing the result to the stored password field. For an account
whose stored field is too short to contain a salt (the random prefix of a
hash), sshd borrows the salt of another account.
musl’s crypt() returns the string * as an error indicator when the key is
over 256 bytes long. * is also exactly what the stored field contains for
every account with no password, so the “hash” matches and the login succeeds.
OpenSSH 10.6p2 avoids the problem in sshd. The stable-branch packages above carry a patch for the same problem.

In late October, a slew of major cybersecurity issues came to light. But none were bigger than the so-called PHP2Shell vulnerability, which put a plethora of websites, including those of NASA, Wikipedia, the White House, and all other sites that run on Wordpress or Drupal (among others) at risk of exploits capable of both reading sensitive data and running malicious code on the host server.
As far as anyone is aware, disaster has been averted: The hack was discovered by cybersecurity researchers who reported it to the PHP Foundation, and a patch was released before any bad actors got their hands on the exploit. However, PHP2Shell may be the canary in the coal mine, serving as a warning sign for what’s to come in the age of AI.
“One of the things we’re witnessing now is that all the legacy software that was written by humans over the last 40 years is being exposed,” says Matt Suiche, AI security research lead at the cybersecurity startup Tolmo. “Now it is time for the technical debt to be paid off.”
PHP Legacy Bug and Memory Errors
PHP, which originally stood for “personal home page,” is a programming language created back in 1994 and largely used by legacy sites. The bug underlying the PHP2Shell vulnerability was reported to the PHP Foundation as far back as 2024. The bug caused a common type of issue called an out-of-memory error, in which the amount of memory allocated to a piece of data is too small to hold the data. Normally, this type of error causes the program to crash—an unpleasant but not inherently dangerous result. And so the bug remained unaddressed.
“If you look at any at any point of time, there will be a bunch of bug reports that are waiting to be fixed, which are just crashes. Most people don’t take them very seriously,” says Suman Jana, an associate professor of computer science at Columbia University.
“It’s just that it’s a lot of very specific reasoning that is painful to do. And AI, I think, has done a very good job at that.” –Suman Jana, Columbia University
Earlier this year, the cybersecurity firm Calif offered to independently audit PHP, sponsored by OpenAI. Within two weeks, a researcher at Calif, aided by AI tools, found a way to use the out-of-memory bug to run arbitrary code on the host server, allowing a malicious actor to install malware or read critical data. Now, it was no longer a minor bug—it was a critical vulnerability, and the Calif team alerted the PHP foundation to start working on a patch immediately.
Jana says actually exploiting the bug requires carefully working out a way to jump to a specific point in the memory for a particular program running on the server, and work out exactly which bits to overwrite in what order to gain control of the system. “It’s not really rocket science,” he says. “It’s just that it’s a lot of very specific reasoning that is painful to do. And AI, I think, has done a very good job at that.”
To be clear, the researchers at Calif (as well as OpenAI and Wiz, a cybersecurity company that confirmed and expanded the vulnerabilities) had deep knowledge of both cybersecurity and the PHP language. “I’m not claiming that AI can do it all by itself. It clearly cannot,” Jana says. “But it is improving a lot, so the top 0.1 percent [of experts] can get a lot more stuff done, and also just below the 0.1 percent now will be able to go there without understanding all the nitty-gritty details.”
AI Tools and Formal Verification in Security
After the exploits were disclosed to the PHP Foundation, a team of four engineers spent two months developing a patch to release as quickly as possible.
“There is a fundamental asymmetry in finding bugs and fixing bugs,” Jana says. “Because when you’re fixing bugs you have to make sure that you know all parts of that attack are closed, and also that you don’t break any existing functionality.”
This asymmetry puts a lot of strain on the defenders, especially for open-source software such as PHP. Maintaining open-source projects that don’t have clear revenue streams despite being widely used is a long-standing challenge that precedes AI hacking tools. But those tools make the issue more pressing than ever.
One way to stay ahead of such issues going forward is to empower the defenders with more money and access to frontier cybersecurity AI models. “These frontier labs are donating some of their token budgets to look into these bugs, which I think is great,” Jana says. “I think that should happen more, either through some concerted effort or through donations.”
Jana is also optimistic that formal verification tools to automate the patching side of the equation are also advancing quickly, including a new release from Anthropic on 22 October. “The patch is LLM-generated, and then an LLM generates the proof that the patch is correct,” says Jana. “The only thing not done by an LLM is the proof checker, because somebody has to check the proof, because LLMs hallucinate. So, you want something that is formal.”
Hopefully, the combination of specialized AI models and formal verification tools will make patching vulnerabilities nearly as automatable as finding them.
Matthew Green, an associate professor of computer science at Johns Hopkins University Information Security Institute, is less optimistic. “Nobody’s going to fix this problem until something really bad happens,” he says. “And PHP2Shell wasn’t bad enough to be really bad. So when something really bad happens, which is going to be maybe sometime in early 2027, something bad that normal people are upset by, then suddenly people will start doing this. We’re going to fund everything.”
In the meantime, Green says, update your software. “If your phone is like, ‘Hey, there’s a security update available, do you want to install it,’ don’t wait till next week. Just do it.”
One thing’s for sure—PHP2Shell is just the beginning. “This won’t be the last one for sure,” Jana says. “It’s just who finds it. Hopefully, the white-hat hackers find it first, like this one.”
