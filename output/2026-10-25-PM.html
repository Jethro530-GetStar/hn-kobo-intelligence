2. Meta Ignores Brazilian Court Order to End Bolsonaro Favoritism

Article
by Otávio Rosso
Sleeping Giants Brasil and the organization CTRL+Z sent new evidence to the Superior Electoral Court on Monday. The groups say it points to possible unequal treatment by Meta of the campaigns of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, of the Workers’ Party, and Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro, of the Liberal Party.
The groups argue that Instagram and WhatsApp tools keep showing results that would favor the Liberal Party candidate. They also say the tools link Lula to derogatory content. This continues despite an electoral court order that Meta ensure equal conditions for both candidates.
Journalist Mônica Bergamo broke the story today in Folha de S.Paulo. According to her report, the organizations filed a petition addressed to Superior Electoral Court Justice Marco Aurélio Bellizze, who is overseeing the court caset. They asked him to order the alleged irregularities fixed within 24 hours. They also want Meta to explain how it ranks search results and produces answers with artificial intelligence. And they ask the court to enforce the daily fine of R$500,000 (about $99,000) it already set.
The new records involve searches for animated GIFs, the ordering of hashtags and answers from Meta AI, the artificial intelligence system built into WhatsApp. The organizations say the examples reinforce their suspicion that the platforms do not apply equivalent criteria to the two presidential campaigns.
Image searches put Flávio Bolsonaro in the spotlight
The petition describes a GIF search on WhatsApp for the word “brasil.” The first result was an image of Flávio Bolsonaro. It showed his ticket number and identified his running mate.
According to the records sent to the court, the same result appeared on three separate accounts. The groups use this to ask whether its top position comes from systematic ranking criteria.
A similar check on Instagram also showed images of the senator among the first results. Users who searched for “brasil” found three images of Flávio Bolsonaro in the top positions. The only mention of President Lula was his name in writing, with no photo.
The two organizations say the difference deserves an explanation. Search and recommendation tools can increase the exposure of certain candidates on social media. They are not claiming that they have proven bias in Meta, choosing instead to posit the question to the Superior Electoral Court.
Lula linked to derogatory content
Another set of examples ties Lula to negative terms in searches on Meta platforms.
On WhatsApp, a GIF search for the word “ladrão” (thief) returned a caricature of the president in a striped uniform, a reference to prison dress. Another result carried the message “Supporting the Workers’ Party is a mental illness.”
On Instagram, a search for the same word showed a GIF reading “Vote 13.” That is the Workers’ Party election number, tied to Lula’s candidacy.
For Sleeping Giants Brasil and CTRL+Z, these results point to an imbalance in how political references are shown to users. The imbalance stands out against the visibility of content favorable to Flávio Bolsonaro.
The petition also challenges how Instagram orders hashtags. A search for “lula” offered “#lulaladrão” (Lula thief) as the first suggestion, ahead of other terms about the president. A search for “bolsonaro” placed the critical hashtag “#bolsonaronuncamais” (Bolsonaro never again) among its last suggestions.
The groups cite the difference in position as another reason to investigate the relevance criteria the platform uses.
The issue is not only whether content for or against the candidates exists. It is mainly how automated systems organize that information and present it to the public.
Meta AI also questioned
The organizations included a test of Meta AI, the artificial intelligence tool offered to WhatsApp users. According to the petition, two people asked the system to pick a number between 1 and 25. They gave no instructions and made no reference to the election.
In one attempt, the AI answered 13. In the other, it answered 22. Those are the party ballot numbers of Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, respectively.
The groups question the coincidence. They ask for an explanation of the parameters the system uses to form its answers.
The episode alone does not allow a conclusion that the tool answered out of electoral preference. Still, the organizations list the test among the elements that, in their view, demand more transparency about how the company’s artificial intelligence features work.
Electoral court demands compliance and threatens to double fine
The new records arrive as the electoral court takes a harder line with Meta.
Justice Bellizze had already ordered the company to apply identical criteria to the campaigns of Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro. He also ordered Meta to prove it was following the measures.
On Friday, the full court upheld the justice’s decision. It reinforced the company’s duty to ensure equal treatment of the candidates.
On Sunday, Bellizze again demanded action. He gave the company a new deadline of 24 hours to show it had met part of the court’s requirements.
The justice also warned that the daily fine, first set at R$500,000 (about $99,000), could rise to R$1 million (about $198,000) if noncompliance continues.
The new petition from Sleeping Giants Brasil and CTRL+Z aims to add to the case just as the court demands more detailed explanations from the company.
The groups want Meta to do more than fix the problems they identified. They also want it to disclose the criteria that decide the order of images, hashtag suggestions and answers produced by artificial intelligence.
Lula campaign has already gone to electoral court
The dispute began with complaints from Lula’s campaign about how Meta’s digital platforms work.
The president’s representatives pointed to limits on the circulation of content about his candidacy. They also cited differences in how the company’s tools behave compared with tools tied to his opponent.
The claims led the electoral court to demand measures to ensure equal treatment of the candidates.
Meta has denied deliberately favoring any candidate. It blamed the earlier problems on technical failures.
Folha de S.Paulo contacted the company about the new records filed Monday. Meta had not responded by the time of publication.
The lack of a specific response leaves open the explanations for the mechanisms behind the results the organizations found.
Groups call protest at Meta headquarters in São Paulo
Besides the legal action, Sleeping Giants Brasil and CTRL+Z planned a demonstration outside Meta’s headquarters in São Paulo at 5 p.m. Monday.
The event is called “Meta, Hands Off Our Election.” The organizations say it will be peaceful.
Participants plan to hand a notice to the company’s director in Brazil. They want action on the suspicions of unequal treatment of the candidacies.
The mobilization increases pressure on Meta on two fronts. In court, the groups seek correction of the alleged irregularities and sanctions. In public debate, they demand transparency about the algorithms used during the presidential race.

3. Show HN: Farol – a risk map of a pull request for the person who approves it

Article
A free, open-source CLI and GitHub Action. After your AI review, farol tells the person who approves how risky the merge is and which files carry the risk, so the riskiest changes get the most attention.
Website · Live demo · Get started · Reference
Agents write the code and agents review it. The last call is still a person's: approve the merge or not. That approval is the scarce part, and spread evenly over every pull request it turns into a skim, which is how a one-line change to a shared hook takes down forty pages.
farol comes after the AI review, not instead of it. Your review agent comments on the lines in the diff. farol gives the person who approves what the agent cannot reliably give: the risk of accepting the change, and where it lives. The goal is not only a faster review: low-risk pull requests can go fast, and the risky ones get read line by line, starting with the files that carry the risk. It works from static analysis (the compilers' own view of your code, not a model's guess), so the same pull request always gets the same answer.
The Claude Code team describes the same principle in How the Claude Code team does code review: review effort follows the risk of the change. As they put it, "The change sets the tier, not the author."
farol (Portuguese for lighthouse) does the triage before the reviewer opens the diff:
- What does this change reach? Every existing page, API operation, RPC method, job and script that runs the changed code, with the call chain to each.
- How risky is the merge? A verdict (fast track, quick review, careful review) and a 0–100 risk score from reach, diff coverage, security findings, contract breaks and dependency changes.
- Where do I start? The riskiest files first, with inline diffs, untested lines marked, and a checklist of what to try by hand.
Free and open source (MIT). Run it as a CLI with npx @winninjs/farol, or on every pull request with the GitHub
Action winnin/farol/action. No account, no hosted service: it runs on your machine or in your CI runner, comments on
every PR and never blocks a merge. It never executes your code and never sends it anywhere.
The demo holds the reports farol produced in CI for a pull request on the FastAPI full-stack template: a FastAPI backend and a React frontend with TanStack Router. The pull request adds a status to items and an Insights page, with the kind of problems a reviewer should catch: a breaking API change, SQL built with an f-string, a shared component without tests.
- Backend report: ⚠ careful review, a breaking contract change and a high-severity security finding.
- Frontend report: ⚠ careful review, a new page and the existing pages a shared component reaches.
The reports are interactive: mark files as reviewed, mark a page as behind a feature flag, open the inline diffs,
follow the test plan and copy the review record from the Result tab. They are the same files the pull request's
comments link to (the pull request); the
copies live in docs/demo/.
| | AI review agents | farol |
|---|---|---|
| Reads | the diff, and whatever context fits in the prompt | the whole program at the base and the head, with the compilers (TypeScript type checker, go/types, Python ast) |
| Tells you | what may be wrong in these lines | what these lines reach, how risky the merge is, and where to look first |
| Answers are | probabilistic: worded differently on every run | deterministic: the same PR gives the same report, and every claim comes with its call chain |
| When unsure | may sound confident anyway | says unknown and asks for a careful review |
| Runs | a model, usually a hosted service | locally or in your CI: it never executes your code and never sends it anywhere |
Use both, in that order. The agent reviews the lines first; then farol tells the person who approves how much attention the merge needs, which files deserve it, which pages and endpoints to test by hand and which contract changes need their clients checked. The agent's review is not enough exactly when a small change has a large reach, and that is what farol shows.
- Review attention goes where the risk is. A 40-file PR usually has two or three files that can actually hurt. farol ranks them, so the reviewer reads those closely and skims the rest with a clear conscience.
- It follows the code, not the file names. farol builds a declaration graph with the real compilers (the TypeScript type checker, go/types, Python's ast). A change to formatPrice lists the twelve pages that render it, not "1 file changed".
- Contract breaks are caught before clients are. Removed response fields, new required parameters, changed types in GraphQL, FastAPI, Go handlers and gRPC, with the services that call each endpoint.
- Review the whole PR, or just what changed since your last look. Every push gets two reports: the full pull request, and only the commits since the previous push. After a round of fixes, the reviewer checks the new commits instead of starting over.
- It uses what your CI already has. Coverage, lint, security and audit reports become diff coverage and findings on the changed lines. Nothing runs twice.
- Fail closed. When farol cannot read part of a change, it says so (unknown) and asks for a careful review. It never calls something safe because it did not understand it.
- Built for the human in the loop. Mark files as reviewed, mark pages as behind a feature flag, check off findings, and paste the result back in the PR as a record of what was reviewed.

farol produces two things: a PR comment with the verdict, the top files and a test checklist, and a single-file HTML report (linked from the comment) where the review happens. Here is what each part is for and how to use it.
What it tells you is how much of your time the PR deserves.
| Verdict | Means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| ✓ Fast track | Only new code, tests or low-risk changes. | Skim the diff and approve. |
| ◐ Quick review | Existing code changed in a contained way, or a weaker signal (low coverage, lint errors, a config file). | Check the Start here files, then approve. |
| ⚠ Careful review | Wide or silent breakage is possible: a score of 60+, a breaking contract change, a high-severity finding, an untested central file, or code farol could not read. | Read the Start here files line by line and test what they reach. |
The score (0–100) is the chance that at least one risky item breaks something. Expand What adds to it to see each item and its points: the files by reach × untested share, findings, contract changes, dependencies. The verdict always comes with its reasons; read them before the diff.
The changed code files, riskiest first. Each row shows how many existing pages or operations run the file, its diff coverage, its findings and its own risk points; with Datadog, also its traffic and production errors. Open the inline diff: added lines no test runs are marked ○, lines with findings ⚠.
How to use it: review top-down and tick Mark as reviewed on each file. The score drops as you go, so you always know how much risk is left. Below it come the low-risk files to skim, environment/infrastructure/CI files (check their values per environment), and files that need no review (docs, lockfiles, tests).
On every page or operation row, and in a panel at the top: mark an entry point that is entirely behind a flag. Files that only flagged entry points use leave the review, labeled behind a flag rather than reviewed. Use it for work-in-progress features shipped dark, so the remaining score reflects what users will actually hit.
Every new and affected entry point, as a list or a tree by module. Opening one draws how the change reaches it, file by file. With Datadog each one shows its traffic share and its distinct errors, busiest first. Use it to judge blast radius: a change reaching 3 rarely used admin pages is not the same as one reaching the checkout.
Each endpoint whose request or response shape changed, as breaking (clients built for the base can fail), dangerous (they may misbehave) or safe (compatible additions), with the exact field and the calling services from Datadog. Use it to confirm every breaking change is intended and its clients are updated, then tick checked with clients.
The evidence behind the score: security findings on the changed lines (with a checked box each), added or bumped dependencies with known vulnerabilities, and diff coverage per file. A report that was not given shows as not verified, never as passing.
The new and affected pages or operations to try by hand, new ones first, then by the risk of the file that reaches them. The PR comment has it as a task list. Use it when you run the branch locally or on a preview environment. It follows your review: once every changed file behind an item is marked as reviewed, the item is marked too (✓ files reviewed), or 🚩 behind a flag. Its own checkbox, for the manual test, stays yours to tick.
Who should review: the people who wrote the code this PR changes (git blame, weighted by each file's risk), plus CODEOWNERS. The PR's own authors and bots are left out, and inactive people are labeled. Use it to request the person who knows the riskiest file, not just whoever is free.
A Markdown record of the review: the initial verdict and score, the score left now, the flagged entry points, the reviewed files, and the checked findings and contract changes. Copy as Markdown and paste it in the PR as your review comment, so the next person knows what was looked at.
After a push to an open PR, the comment carries a second report with only the commits since the previous push
(analyze --since <sha>; after a force-push, from its merge-base). Its verdict, score and Start here cover just
those changes. Use it for the second round of a review: check that the fixes do what was asked and nothing
else, then go back to the full report only if the new commits reach somewhere new.
Kept in Details and a collapsed block of the comment, so reviewers form their own opinion first:
isolated (only adds), touches-existing (existing code changed), global (a global file, manifest or critical
dependency) and unknown (part of the change could not be read: never safe). Every reason code is in
docs/reasons.md.
- Diff the merge-base and the head, detecting renames and ignoring whitespace-only and trailing-comma lines.
- Read both trees without running anything: the TypeScript compiler API, go list + go/types (offline), Python ast. From them, the entry points and a graph of which declaration uses which.
- Compare declarations by a hash of their code, so comments and formatting never count. New declarations and entry points are additive.
- Reach: from each changed declaration to every entry point that runs it, keeping the shortest chain.
- Classify, weigh and plan: class, score, verdict, files to start with, test checklist.
On 662 past commits of four repositories, fast track had 0 problems in 56 commits and the score's AUC was
0.72–0.83; run farol calibrate on your own history.
The details per language are in docs/how-it-works.md, and the reasons behind every choice in
docs/DECISIONS.md.
- analyze never executes code of the analyzed repository and never uses the network. The Go and Python helpers use only their standard libraries and only parse.
- Only comment (GitHub API) and usage (Datadog API) talk to the network. Error messages are not copied from Datadog, only error types.
- In CI the job that runs your code has no secrets and no write token; the job that comments never runs your code.
- Shadow mode: farol informs, it never approves, requests changes or fails a check.
- Static reading: values built at runtime are invisible (computed route paths, getattr, importlib, DI containers, registries filled in loops). When an entry point cannot be read, the PR is unknown; when a call cannot be resolved, farol assumes it reaches more, never less.
- TypeScript frontends: React Router, Next.js and TanStack Router (file-based) only: no code-based TanStack route trees, Remix/React Router file conventions, Vue or Svelte. JavaScript files, renamed files and TypeScript backends use module-level reach.
- Go: route prefixes from middleware stacks other than chi/gin/echo groups are not applied, and project types that wrap a router with their own registration API (Gitea's web.Router) are not read. Python: Django REST Framework routers are not read.
- The score and verdict thresholds are heuristics checked on a few repositories. Calibrate them on yours.
We already have an AI reviewer. Why add farol? They answer different questions. The agent comments on the lines in the diff; after it, farol tells the person who approves how risky the merge is and which files carry the risk, from static analysis of the whole program. Run both: farol's comment and report sit next to the agent's, and neither blocks the merge.
Is it free? Yes. farol is open source under the MIT license, with no account, hosted service or paid tier: run it

10. You Are Losing Weight. Why Are You So Angry?
FULL TEXT

Article
You started losing weight and it is working. The scale is moving, your clothes fit better. And you snap at your partner over the dishes, lose patience in traffic, and find your coworkers unbearable by four in the afternoon. You are not turning into a worse person. You are hungry more often than you used to be, and hunger changes how everything around you looks.
The extreme version of this is old. In the Minnesota Starvation Experiment during World War II, 36 healthy young men ate about 1,570 calories a day for six months and lost roughly a quarter of their body weight. They became irritable, depressed, and obsessed with food, and movies they used to laugh at no longer amused them. That was semi-starvation, far beyond any sensible diet. But the same effect shows up at everyday scale. In a 2022 field study, 64 adults rated their hunger and mood five times a day for three weeks. In a 2010 trial, women were put on a three week diet. The results:
- Hungrier moments came with more anger, more irritability, and less pleasure
- Eating 1,200 calories a day raised cortisol, a stress hormone, and counting calories raised how stressed people felt
The last one matters because the two effects were separate. A diet adds stress from two directions: your body runs low, and the constant arithmetic of what you can and cannot eat wears on you.
Why does hunger turn into anger at someone in particular? In experiments at the University of North Carolina, hungry people rated neutral symbols as more unpleasant, but only after seeing a negative image first. In the lab, hungry students whose computer crashed, and who were then blamed for it, reported more stress and hatred and judged the researcher as harsher. Students who had first spent a few minutes writing about their own feelings did not. Hunger feels bad, and when something frustrating happens, your mind attributes that bad feeling to the person in front of you instead of to your stomach.
The good news is that irritability is not the price of losing weight. In the CALERIE trial, 220 healthy adults spent two years trying to eat 25 percent less, and in practice managed about half of that. Compared with people who ate normally, they lost about 17 pounds and reported better mood, less tension, and better sleep. A modest deficit kept up over time did not make people angry. A steep one, run on willpower and long gaps without food, is what the hunger studies describe.
So the goal is not to stop losing weight. It is to lose it without spending your days on the edge of hunger:
- Cut less, for longer: a small daily deficit is far easier on your mood than a crash diet
- Eat protein early: it keeps you full longer per calorie, which is why I skip lunch without struggling
- Fill up on water rich food like soup and vegetables, so your stomach is not empty when your budget is
- Keep hard conversations, negotiations, and big decisions away from the hour before a meal
- Name it out loud: saying "I am hungry, not angry" gives the people around you context and gives you a pause
- Protect your sleep: short nights raise appetite and lower patience, a bad combination on a diet
If the irritability comes with weeks of low mood, losing interest in things you used to enjoy, or thoughts about food that take over your day, raise it with a doctor. Aggressive restriction can slide into disordered eating, and that is not something to push through alone.
Otherwise, treat the anger as information. It is telling you the deficit is too steep or the gaps between meals are too long. Adjust the diet, not your opinion of the people around you.
