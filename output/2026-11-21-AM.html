introducing dettato
we're introducing Dettato, a SOTA audio to MIDI transcription model that significantly beats MuScriptor 1.3B - the next best open model - and scores a bit higher than mirelo's closed source model 'Mirelo A2M v1.1 Pro' on their published benchmarks. all at just 20m parameters - roughly 65x smaller than muscriptor. disclaimer with the benchmarks: it's difficult to gauge the true accuracy of the model for your use-case without trying it yourself. one thing my model performs poor on for instance, is splitting instruments correctly. In addition, this just spits out performance MIDI with a beat aligned. If you're looking for something that you can actually play(e.g sheet music), i'd reccommend songscription or klangio - both are excellent tools by great companies that push the envelope of what's possible in this field forward.
the driving goal of kobimusic: to help musicians, not replace them. a part of that goal means following copyright law. we trained dettato on a mix of synthetic instrument recordings and CC solo instruments mixed in using the URMP dataset. the rest of the instruments morph together ~20 soundfonts, using the 'soundfont babies' technique i published. no copyrighted recordings were used.
I began working on dettato about 10 months ago, but scrapped what I had in about April. The reason why was that I treated AMT as a data problem - but in reality, the bottleneck is architecture. There are a lot of parellels that you can draw for OMR (first model i made) and AMT. both traditionally were tackled early on using algorithms. later, in both fields they plugged in giant LLM components. These models would naturally become overfit to synthetic data, since globally the problems of AMT and OMR become different than if you treat it locally. But, for both you can reframe it as a local problem. For OMR, using a detector model. For AMT, a pitch-lane model.
overall, i hope both open and closed weight music retrieval companies can learn from this. The constraint of not using copyrighted recordings doesn't hold you back, but actually encourages innovation, and it's likely you'll end up with a better model in the end.

Title:Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights
View PDF HTML (experimental)
Abstract:A language model is trained once and frozen. Making it better takes another training run, or a prompt, a harness or extra computation at inference that is gone once the task ends. We show a third way: to our knowledge for the first time, a language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training. We call this self-rewriting: the model is its own optimizer. In earlier work, a frontier model edited a much smaller one; here an open 27B model, given its weights, its graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its own failure and rewrites the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw. It keeps getting better: it tests its edits on practice problems and submits only what works, each edit improving on the last, and over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rises. It repairs a damaged copy of itself, like a broken robot that fixes itself without being rebuilt: the copy solves almost nothing, yet with no retraining and without ever reading the original weights, the model brings it back to over 80% of the original accuracy on image tasks it never saw. And the ability emerges with scale: in one model family, the smaller models make no edit or break themselves, and only the largest improves and repairs itself. Unlike test-time scaling, self-rewriting writes its gain into the weights, at no cost per answer; unlike training, it runs only forward passes, as serving does. The wall between training and inference is a limit of our tools, not a property of intelligence. We believe self-rewriting is a necessary step toward recursive self-improvement: a model that, like a human, does not separate training from inference could keep upgrading itself for life, with no larger model, no harness or prompt engineering, and no pre- or post-training.

Article
I want to show you HippoMaps. It is a fully featured map and navigation app built on OpenStreetMap with a custom Overpass API engine for really fast search of POIs, areas and ways.
My aim with HippoMaps is to get to a level where I don't need Google Maps anymore. The biggest hurdle IMHO with OSM based apps is the lack of nice and fast search (where GMaps excels at). To get there I started working on a deterministic (as in no LLMs used) parser of queries that translates them to the Overpass QL.
Search queries are split into tokens and the algorithm tries to guess "what" you want to search and "where". The former comes from a word list (known brands, sports, OSM tags) and the latter from the geocoder service (Photon).
You can get quite creative like "cafes in Zurich by the Limmat", "peaks over 4000 m in Valais", "pharmacies open now in Bern". It's good for discovering hiking trails, e.g. "hiking trails from Arth-Goldau to Rigi Kulm". It knows how to find stuff between two places, next to a place, near a place and so on. You can stack those as well like "bridges over the danube in serbia 50 km from novi sad".
Except the search it has almost all of the features you would expect from a map like turn-by-turn navigation (Valhalla) with voice (Piper), public transport navigation in Europe (MOTIS), live traffic (only a few countries so far).
The app is GPL-3.0: https://github.com/cdutsov/hippomaps
I'm looking forward to feedback on searches that work or don't work for you + overall comments.

10. I'll never buy Framework hardware again

TL;DR:
We bought a 3000 EUR Framework Laptop 16 for one of our developers. It was broken out of the box. Three months, around thirty support emails, two replacement parts and a trip to their repair center later, Framework concluded “No Fault Found” and blamed the keyboard layout of an OS that wasn’t even installed when the problem first showed up. I’m done with them.
Some context first#
I have been a Framework fan for a while. At my company, developers pick the machine of their choice and several work on Framework laptops or desktops, we have about quite a few of them around, and I have been recommending them to pretty much anyone who asked me what laptop to buy so far. Repairable hardware, Linux-friendly, a company that seemed to care about the people using its products: it was an easy recommendation to make.
So when Q joined the team and needed a workstation, we ordered a Framework Laptop 16, around 3000 EUR once configured.
Three months later, I’m pissed off at Framework and have banned future purchases from them at my company. Not because the machine arrived broken, this happens and I’ve been around hardware long enough to know it, but because of how unprofessional and useless the support has been from the first email to the last.
OH and feel free to share, friends don’t let friends enter hell.
Day one: broken out of the box#
The laptop arrives early August. Q assembles it, powers it on and is greeted by a “missing input module” screen, before reaching the BIOS, before any operating system is even installed. Keep this detail in mind, we’ll get back to it.
The keyboard isn’t detected when centered, and when moved to the left it shows up as… a numpad. In that state, nothing works: not the keyboard, not the touchpad, not the numpad. There’s a BIOS option, “Force Power for Input Modules”, that bypasses module detection. With it enabled, everything works. Without it, the machine has no keyboard and no touchpad. It is recommended NOT to turn it on permanently as it might be harmful to the hardware, as stated on their own website.
I open a ticket on August 13th.
August: the script loop hell#
What follows is two weeks of support reading from a script, one step per email, one email per day or two:
- is it out of the box? any damage on the packaging? which OS, which kernel, which BIOS version?
- do a mainboard reset by pressing the chassis switch ten times
- reseat the midplate
- try Force Power On (we had already told them it was the only thing that worked)
- send photos of the keyboard, the midplate, the connector, the whole laptop from six angles, and a video
- reset the BIOS to defaults
- reinstall the BIOS and the drivers bundle (a Windows drivers bundle, for a problem that happens before any OS boots, on a machine that doesn’t run Windows…)
- try different input module combinations
We did all of it. We sent the photos and the video in a shared drive. Each reply came from a different agent who clearly hadn’t read the thread, and asked for something we had already provided.
On August 24th I lost patience and wrote that this was a 3000 EUR laptop that didn’t work, that my co-worker had been without a usable machine of his own for over a week, and asked them to either send replacement parts or take it back and refund us. I even went all Karen and asked to involve a manager.
Their answer: please send a clear photo of the midplate and the underside of the keyboard. The same photos, again. No manager involved to break the script because no flying fucks were given.
We did their diagnosis for them#
Q then replied from his own address, which apparently is a problem, because support spent the next ten days asking him to “verify the shipping address” of the order, which he did, several times.
Meanwhile, tired of being asked to repeat steps,
he updated the keyboard firmware himself (only possible with Force Power On, since the keyboard is otherwise not detected),
installed Framework’s own framework_tool,
and ran a proper isolation:
$ sudo framework_tool --inputdeck
The result was unambiguous:
the keyboard module reports the ID of a numpad, KeyboardB.
The firmware therefore sees two numpads and no keyboard,
considers the input deck incomplete,
and never enables the power rail for the input modules,
which is why everything is dead and not just the keyboard.
He moved the keyboard alone across slots: it misidentifies itself in every position. He moved the numpad alone into the same slot: it identifies correctly. He removed everything: the baseline is clean. The wrong ID follows the keyboard. The keyboard is faulty.
He sent all of this to support on August 28th with a full report and a one-line reproduction command.
September: they replace the wrong part#
On September 7th, support concludes… that the midplate needs to be replaced. Not the keyboard, the only part that a reproducible diagnostic, run with their own tool, had just pointed at.
The midplate arrives a week later. The problem is, of course, still there because… GOTO previous paragraph. Q asks them whether they’re sure it’s not the keyboard, given that he proved it with the diagnosis.
Their answer ?
Back to the queue. Back to photos: the packaging box from different angles including the interior, the midplate front and back, the keyboard top and bottom, close-ups of the sockets. Photos they already had.
At this point, Q is no longer a developer but has become a profesionnal photographer.
October: a new keyboard, a new bug#
On October 8th, two months after the laptop arrived, Framework finally agrees to replace the keyboard. What they should have done on the first week…
The new keyboard is detected, the original bug is gone,
but there is a new one:
pressing C sends a c followed by a Delete escape sequence.
$ showkey -a
c 99 0143 0x63
^[[3~ 27 0033 0x1b
91 0133 0x5b
51 0063 0x33
126 0176 0x7e
An external keyboard on the same machine produces a single c, as it should.
FU-CKIN’ HELL it never ends…
You can’t use a keyboard where typing a word containing a c deletes the next character.
You certainly can’t type a disk encryption passphrase with it.
Q is smart so he knows the drill and sends the showkey output, photos, and a video to avoid wasting time on a useless round-trip,
but he understimated them:
support asks him whether the issue happens with an external keyboard (he had already said it didn’t),
then asks him to boot a live USB to “isolate software issues” (yes, the famous ones that would trigger on just their keyboard).
He does, tries both the US and French layouts, same result as anyone not brain-dead would expect.
The ticket is escalated, with the usual “escalations may take 5 to 7 working days”.
November: “No Fault Found”#
End of October, Framework decides to have the laptop sent to their repair center for a “full review and assessment”, with a reminder that we may be charged if they find customer-induced damage.
We ship it.
This morning, November 20th, the verdict arrives:
Our repair center has completed their diagnostics, tested two keyboards and found that they were working perfectly. They have determined No Fault Found and that the issue was likely caused by installing the wrong keyboard layout in the operating system.
Let me be very clear about why this is nonsense:
- Keyboard 1 failed before any operating system was installed on the machine. The “missing input module” screen shows up before the BIOS. There was no keyboard layout to get wrong. However, the BIOS workaround they asked us to enable was still on when the laptop was shipped, and with it on, the problem is hidden. If you don’t turn it off, you won’t see the fault.
- Keyboard 2 fails in GRUB, before the kernel is even loaded, and on a live USB with both QWERTY and AZERTY layouts, which, by the way, put the C key in the same place. A keyboard layout maps a key to a character; it does not make a key emit two scancodes. And if you test by typing a sentence and looking at the result, a spurious Delete at the end of a line deletes nothing and you’ll never notice it. You have to actually look at what the key sends, which is exactly what we showed them.
So either the repair center tested with the workaround on and never pressed C in the middle of a word, or they didn’t test at all. In both cases, “the user picked the wrong keyboard layout” is not a diagnosis, it’s a way to close a ticket.
What it cost us#
Let’s count:
- three months and one week since the laptop arrived,
- around thirty emails from support, handled by more than a dozen different agents,
- two replacement parts, one of which was the wrong one,
- a round trip to a repair center,
- hours of an engineer’s time spent producing photos, videos, reports and diagnostics that the support team then didn’t read,
- a developer who had to work for weeks with external devices plugged into a laptop that couldn’t be used as a laptop.
And at the end, the laptop comes back in the exact state it left, with a note implying we don’t know how to configure a keyboard layout.
The problem isn’t the hardware#
Hardware fails. A bad keyboard module out of the box is annoying, not scandalous. I’ve had DOA hardware from every vendor I’ve ever bought from.
What’s scandalous is the process:
- nobody owns the ticket, every reply comes from someone new who restarts from a script;
- information we provide is not read, so it gets requested again, and again;
- a clear, reproducible diagnosis made with their own tool is ignored in favor of replacing an unrelated part;
- basic customer identity handling is broken enough that a colleague replying on the thread triggers ten days of address verification;
- and when it finally reaches people who could actually look at the hardware, the conclusion contradicts facts that were in the ticket from day one.
What’s scandalous is the amount of not giving a damn, not escalating, not caring at all about having a customer in a shit situation for more than three months when they could simply replace the broken part and be done. Like they can’t afford a spare keyboard and make sure that it works ?
So, what now?#
We’re not going to spend another three months trying to get Framework to acknowledge a fault we’ve demonstrated a dozen times when they obviously don’t care. We’ll recycle the laptop into a desktop when it comes back from the repair center and will get Q a new laptop from another vendor that’s more serious and professional.
As for me: buying new Framework hardware is now off the table. The machines we have will live their lives, but the next ones will come from somewhere else.
I liked the idea of Framework a lot. Repairability is worthless if the company behind it can’t tell a broken keyboard from a keyboard layout nor… repair, and the support I’ve experienced is the opposite of what I’d expect from a company selling premium-priced hardware to professionals.
If you’re considering a Framework for your company, I hope it works out of the box for you. Because if it doesn’t, you now know what’s waiting for you.
