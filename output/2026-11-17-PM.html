Scarce Units, Typed, Related, Actionable.
An open-source, self-hosted ontology platform.
Your data becomes one model you own: typed, linked, changed only through

Quick start · How it works · Proposals · Python SDK · Contributing
Beta. Only small parts are still alpha: the model language and APIs may still change between versions, and parts of the user interface and docs are unfinished.
Maintainers wanted. Sutra-OS is a one-person project so far. See Contributing.
Sutra-OS began in late 2025 as a thought experiment: a free version of the system described in Ontology-Oriented Software Development, whose creators I deeply admire.
Much talent today goes into software that sells attention, while the software everything else runs on is mostly free: by a rough estimate, 70–90% of US economic output depends on free software at some critical point. That openness is not guaranteed. In November 2026, Nvidia's board parted with the founder who had its AI models released openly. Sutra-OS adds to the commons under the AGPL, so that it stays readable by anyone.
The past ten years have shown what it costs when nobody can see what depends on what. In 2017, NotPetya spread through a single Ukrainian accounting program and stopped Maersk's ports and Merck's factories. In 2023, a corrupted file in one FAA system halted every domestic departure in the US. In 2024, one faulty CrowdStrike update crashed millions of Windows machines, and airlines, hospitals and banks stood still. In November 2026, a single vendor took Outlook down for a day and some cities went back to fax. That same week, data flooded the FAA's telecommunications network, aging routers buckled, and the US saw only the third nationwide ground stop in its history. No one, it seems, had those routers on the map of what keeps planes in the air, so no one could ask beforehand what would stop if they failed.
That question is what Sutra-OS is built for. Every system, device, dataset and decision becomes an object with links to what it feeds and what feeds it, and every number can be traced back to its source. Whoever holds such a model can ask, before the outage, what stops when one piece fails, and get an answer, at every scale from a single person to a government (hopefully never a world government). Many small ontologies, each held by those it describes, keep power spread out; one model of everything would not.
Scarce Units: energy, water, land, housing and time will shape the coming decades. Whoever counts what is scarce decides how it is shared, and today that bookkeeping sits with a few platforms; for most people, the only trace their own data leaves in their life is the tendinitis from the hundredth weary click on "Accept all" in a cookie banner. Sutra-OS keeps the bookkeeping with those it concerns, under rules everyone can read: households, farms, workshops, clubs, newsrooms and public administrations get their records back, and their wrist with them. That is what a democracy needs from its software: a council that can trace the number behind a decision to its source, a change that stays a proposal until someone answers for it, and a public administration accountable to its voters rather than to a vendor under another country's law.
Almost everyone. Whoever you are, someone else keeps a better-connected record of your affairs than you do. Sutra is for anyone who wants their own.

Three ideas carry the whole platform: object types, link types and
action types. The examples below use the farm model in
resources/presets.
You start by saying what exists: a lot of hay, from one field and one cut; a sale, to one buyer. Each is a typed thing with a kind and a key.
:objects
{:lot
{:name "Hay lot" :plural "Hay lots" :symbol "🌾" :title :name
:primary-key :name :match-keys [:name]
:attributes
[[:name :string {:name "Lot"}] ; "North Meadow, 1st cut 2026"
[:baled :integer {:name "Bales made" :constraints {:min 0}}]
[:sold :integer {:derived {:expression [:connected-sum :sales :bales]}}]
[:in-barn :integer
{:name "In the barn" :visibility :prominent
:derived {:language :clojure
:expression [:- [:get :baled] [:get :sold]]
:inputs [:baled :sold]}}]]}
:sale
{:name "Sale" :symbol "🤝" :primary-key :sale-nr :match-keys [:sale-nr]
:attributes
[[:sale-nr :string]
[:lot :string]
[:buyer :string]
[:bales :integer {:constraints {:min 1}}]
[:price-per-bale :decimal]
[:sold-on :date]
[:sold-by :string]]}}
Nobody types or counts :in-barn: it is the bales made minus every bale sold,
computed by the server. :bales is an integer, so "about forty" or -3 is
refused before anything is written.
An object type is a typed table with a name, a face in the UI and a class in the generated SDK. Properties are typed, constrained and can be derived, so a value nobody typed in still shows up like any other column.
A sale on its own is just a number. A sale linked to the lot it came out of is a fact everyone can see.
:links
[[:sale-lot :sale :lot :many-to-one
{:fk :lot :forward-api-name :lot :reverse-api-name :sales}]]
The sale names its lot and the link follows it: from the lot you see its
sales, from a sale its lot, and :connected-sum above sums across that link.
One line, and the barn knows what is in it.
Links are first-class: named in both directions, navigable in the UI, traversable
in the SDK (lot.sales), and usable in derived values, filters and object
sets.
In most systems anybody may change anything, and nobody can say who did. Here there is no "update row". There are only actions: named decisions with typed inputs and rules the server enforces. Selling hay is one of them, and it has a condition.
:sell-hay
{:on :lot
:params {:lot {:type :lot :required true}
:sale-nr {:type :string :required true}
:buyer {:type :string :required true}
:bales {:type :integer :required true :constraints {:min 1}}
:price-per-bale {:type :decimal}
:sold-on {:type :date :prefill :now}
:sold-by {:type :string :exposure :hidden :prefill :current-user}}
:submission-criteria
[{:message "Not that many bales in the barn."
:test {:want [:get :bales] :have [:get :lot :in-barn]}
:pred [:<= :want :have]}]
:rules [{:op :create :object-type :sale
:values {:sale-nr [:get :sale-nr] :lot [:get :lot :name]
:buyer [:get :buyer] :bales [:get :bales]
:price-per-bale [:get :price-per-bale]
:sold-on [:get :sold-on] :sold-by [:get :sold-by]}}]
:edits [{:object-type :sale}]}
Ask to sell more bales than are in the barn and the server refuses and says why, with the same sentence for the phone, the CLI, the Python script and the AI agent. A refused action writes nothing. Every sale records who made it, and nobody can put someone else's name there.
Actions are the only write path: typed parameters, validation, submission criteria and state transitions, defined once and enforced everywhere.
Every rule you would otherwise keep in your head has this shape: a fact the server computes, and an action that checks it. Declare it once, and stop remembering it.
The same three ideas run the arable side of the farm. A field's margin is derived from its paid harvests and the hay sold off it, two links away. A harvest moves from planted to harvested, delivered and paid only through its actions, and Sell harvest refuses to run without a signed delivery note and a quality test of your own.
In most software, deciding and doing are one step. In Sutra they are two. When you sell hay, the server first writes down what would change: one new sale, forty bales, your name on it. Nothing has happened yet. Only when that note is approved does the barn change.
Everything else follows from that:
- You can always ask first. "Would this work?" is the same question, without the last step.
- An agent can only make suggestions. What it hands back is the note, never the change. You decide.
- You can try things out. Run the same decisions on a copy, compare, and keep the real one untouched.
- You can look back. Every approved change is kept, so last Tuesday's state is still there to read.
- The answer is the same everywhere. The rule is checked in one place, so the phone, the script and the agent hear the same no.
And it is not a promise. A test fails if the part that decides ever does anything but decide.
- Your data comes in as it is. Mail over IMAP, CSV exports, REST APIs, PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server and SSH, shaped by transforms in Python, SQL or Clojure.
- Every number has a past. Lineage runs from each object back through the
transforms and datasets to the mail, statement or row it came from
(sutra lineage <node>). When someone else's figures disagree with yours,
you open the source.
- Agents get only the tools you name. An action, a function, a query on
one type. By default (:writes :propose) their changes are staged as
proposals; a rejected one doesn't come back, and the model's prompt and
answer are kept with the run.
- The model is versioned like code. Model and data live on branches. You
stage a change, commit it, open a proposal, see who consumes what you
touched, and merge into main only when it's reviewed. Webhooks and
notifications stay silent on a branch unless you say otherwise.
- Share exactly what you choose. Markings on types and properties are mandatory access control; grants on projects decide who may read, act and invoke. A group (a co-op, a tenants' association, a family) can pool what its members agree to share, and nothing else, and the server enforces it on every read: lists, filters, counts and totals. One person's data can't show what everyone else was offered. A group's can.
- Builders and users are different people. One person writes the model; everyone else gets apps with the build tools locked away, and a server that says no when no is the right answer.
- Secrets stay out of reach. A separate egress agent holds every external credential, so the code that reads your mail never sees the password.

farm_sdk.py is plain, typed Python: a class per object type, an attribute
per property and link, a method per action with the action's parameters as
its signature. Sutra's language server (clients/ent-lsp)
runs pyright for Python and labels each completion as what it is in the model
(property, link-type, action, state); any editor that lets you pick the
language server can run it (Neovim config).
An action is a method. Its signature comes from the model; a missing argument is flagged before anything runs.

An object, not a row. A lot brings its derived values and its links along.
Nobody stored in_barn; the server computes it.
lot = Lot.get("North Meadow, 1st cut 2025") # a Lot, or NotFound

Ask first, or only propose. The same call can be a question (nothing is written) or a proposal (staged until someone approves it). That is how an agent gets to use it.

Walk a lifecycle. A harvest goes from planted to harvested,
delivered and paid, one action per step, and the rules travel with them.
The states are declared in the model, so status is typed
Literal['planted', 'harvested', 'delivered', 'paid'] and your editor offers

Sets, not lists. On the class, a property is a filter and a link is a hop.
Combine sets with |, & and -; the server plans it, and you fetch only
what you iterate. Each set knows what it holds: running_low is an
ObjectSet[Lot], and one hop along Lot.sales makes it an ObjectSet[Sale].
running_low = Lot.where(Lot.in_barn < 50)
neighbours = Sale.where(Sale.buyer == "Neighbour").around(Sale.lot)
(running_low - neighbours).count() # 3: running low, and the neighbour never bought from them
running_low.around(Lot.sales).aggregate(sum_(Sale.bales), group_by=[by(Sale.buyer)]) # who emptied them
Try tomorrow's rule on today's farm, or look at last Tuesday. Everything above runs unchanged against a branch, and every action tells you the revision it landed in, a state you can read again later.
with client.branch("new-rule"): # made with: sutra branch new-rule

client.at(sale.revision).objects("sale") # the sales as they stood right then

4. All AI-generated code should be Open Source
FULL TEXT
1268 points · 656 comments · jcubic

Best #1

Article
This may be a hot take, but I strongly believe that all code generated by AI should be Open Source. And here's why.
First of all, AI models were trained on a massive corpus of Open Source code written since RMS announced the GNU Project and the Free Software Movement. That code was simply scraped from the internet and used without permission or proper attribution. The models were also trained on Stack Overflow and Stack Exchange sister projects, which are released under Creative Commons licenses that legally require attribution. Turning this collective knowledge into proprietary, closed-source code feels like license laundering.
The second reason is legal: purely AI-generated code is not written by a human, meaning it should not be copyrightable. The foundation for this principle in the US goes back to the famous Monkey Selfie dispute, which established that non-humans cannot hold copyrights. A precedent later cited by the U.S. Copyright Office regarding AI. Following the same logic, Wikimedia Commons requires all purely AI-generated images to be tagged as Public Domain.
However, copyright laws differ across the globe. For instance, countries like the UK grant limited copyright protection to computer-generated works. To make things fair across all jurisdictions, and to honor the open ecosystem that made AI possible, developers and companies should explicitly release AI-generated code under Open Source licenses or dedicate it to the public domain using the Creative Commons Zero (CC0) license.
Of course, code is not always 100% manual or 100% automated. However, developers more and more rely on AI without even reviewing the generated output, let alone modifying it to fit their specific needs. Code like this, where humans put little effort, except writing prompts, should definitely be Open Source.

Title:Extending concurrent separation logic to the hardware level to verify the xv6 OS kernel on RISC-V with AI agents
View PDF HTML (experimental)
Abstract:MachCSL is a framework for verifying system software, such as an OS kernel, on top of low-level semantics of a RISC-V computer, based on the Sail RISC-V semantics. The key idea behind MachCSL is to adapt concurrent separation logic, based on Iris, to reasoning about low-level hardware execution at the sub-instruction level: page-table translation, TLB, privilege levels, configuration registers, instruction fetch/decode/execute, traps and interrupts, DMA, shared memory, power failures, etc. Reasoning at this level of detail ensures that the system software correctly manages all of the hardware details.
Verifying software at this low level of abstraction is tedious, but LLM-based agents are capable of reasoning about such low-level details. As a case study, we verify the xv6 OS kernel (6,657 lines of C and assembly code), which provides a traditional Unix system call interface (processes, file system, file descriptors, and preemptive scheduling) and has substantial internal concurrency (multi-core support with fine-grained locking, shared memory, interrupts, DMA, etc.). In the verification process, we uncovered 11 bugs in the xv6 implementation, as well as one bug in the Sail RISC-V semantics, and one bug in Sail's Lean extraction. The verification effort took us 102 days, including the time to develop the MachCSL framework.
On top of the verified xv6 kernel, we prove a theorem about console-level behavior: e.g., if the user types echo hello world as input on the UART console, the only output the system can produce is hello world (if there are no crashes or other errors). This theorem covers both the kernel and the init, sh, echo, cat, grep, sync, and seccomp binaries in the initial file system image.

I recently asked AI to help me look through the latest Windows binaries for undocumented features and functionality. Among the leads it turned up, one list immediately caught my attention: coding agents such as codex.exe, claude.exe, copilot.exe, and cursor.exe, alongside familiar developer tools such as python.exe, dotnet.exe, gcc.exe, and code.exe.
Why would a Windows system DLL need a list of coding agents and developer tools?

The compiler-generated C++ names are unwieldy, so I will call these functions executable-name matchers. For reference, one related matcher is std::_Func_impl_no_alloc<<lambda_340dfe7e962ab225b6ac33bc664bd54f>, bool, std::basic_string_view<wchar_t, std::char_traits<wchar_t>> const&, Microsoft::Diagnostics::MissionControlAggregator::Utils::AppType>::_Do_call. The screenshot shows another lambda instantiation performing the same kind of name comparison.
The feature guarding the new agent checks has a particularly direct name: CLICodingAgentUsage, feature ID 62580080.
And the list extends beyond AI agents. The same component checks for node.exe, python.exe, dotnet.exe, gcc.exe, clang.exe, gdb.exe, javac.exe, devenv.exe, and code.exe, among others. Those checks feed broader developer-tool categories. Some of these checks also verify the publisher: the Node.js slot expects Node.js Foundation, Python expects Python Software Foundation, and the .NET and Visual Studio slots expect Microsoft Corporation. The compiler/debugger group containing GCC and Clang has no additional publisher check in this path.
Where does the DLL get this information?
The next question was whether the component scans running processes itself. Following Aggregator::OnLoad revealed that it subscribes to existing Windows events instead.
For Windows programs, it consumes KernelProcess.AppStateChangeSummary and Win32kTraceLogging.AppInteractivitySummary. These provide application identities that its handlers parse and pass to the matchers. For WSL, it consumes Microsoft.Windows.Lxss.Manager.Exec and ExecCritical, using the imageName field. A regular Windows codex.exe can therefore reach the CLI-agent checks through the app-state summary route; this is not limited to Linux programs running inside WSL.
The relevant flow looks like this:
Aggregator::OnLoad
├─ WSL Exec / ExecCritical
│ └─ ProcessWslExecEvent → CLI agent matchers
├─ KernelProcess.AppStateChangeSummary
│ └─ ProcessAppStateChangeSummaryEvent → CLI agent matchers
└─ Win32kTraceLogging.AppInteractivitySummary
└─ ProcessAppInteractivitySummaryEvent → desktop agent matchers
Aggregator::OnRelativeTimerExpire
└─ MissionControlAggregatorLogging::ReportCohorts<12,4,5>
└─ Segments.SubCohorts ETW report
Aggregator::OnUnload / intermediate timer
└─ PersistentState::SaveToDisk
A successful match sets a slot in the aggregator’s state. Later, ReportCohorts<12,4,5> turns those slots into bitsets and emits a report named Segments.SubCohorts. The numbers correspond to twelve broader tool categories, four CLI agents, and five desktop agents.
ETW (Event Tracing for Windows) is the mechanism carrying that report. In this case, it is a self-describing TraceLogging event. Rather than writing the executable names into every report, the component assigns each category a bit. A set Codex bit means the matching tool was observed during the reporting window.
The source events also include runtime, time in focus, interaction counters, and resource usage. This component appears to discard those details and record only a bitmap of which agents were observed. The report’s timestamps describe the collection window, not how long each agent was used.
Although I found no prompt text or source code in this report, collecting which agents and developer tools someone uses already has significant privacy implications.
Is it actually running on a normal machine?
Strings and code are a useful lead, but I wanted to see the component running. I checked my Parallels Windows 11 ARM64 VM, build 26200.9974. Its MissionControlAggregator.dll was version 10.0.26100.9818, and CLICodingAgentUsage was already enabled as a service experiment. I had not manually enabled the feature.
DiagTrack, the Connected User Experiences and Telemetry service, was also already running with its startup type set to Automatic. It starts a separate AggregatorHost.exe process, and that process loads the DLL. The following administrative PowerShell commands show the service, its child process, and the loaded module:

The public ISO’s baseline VM had the coding-agent feature disabled by image default, so the evidence does not support saying that it is active on every Windows installation. On my ARM64 VM, however, the relevant feature and service were active before the test.
Testing it in practice
To test the finding in practice, I copied cmd.exe to a file named codex.exe and ran it in the interactive Windows session. The name alone was enough for the component to recognize it as Codex.
I captured an AppStateChangeSummary for the running process from the KernelProcess provider ({2839ff94-8f12-4e1b-82e3-af7af77a450f}), with LaunchCount=1 and TerminateCount=0. The aggregator’s saved state contained the value 2 in CLI slot 2, the Codex slot. A separate run without an additional ETW trace also set that slot, confirming that detection worked through Windows’ existing diagnostic collection.
That state is persisted in:
C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\Diagnosis\AggregatorStorage\MissionControlAggregator$
The report normally follows a roughly daily boundary. To capture it immediately, I temporarily moved the saved reporting-window start 25 hours into the past and restarted DiagTrack with a trace listening for the aggregator. The resulting Segments.SubCohorts event contained:
Provider: Microsoft.Windows.MissionControlAggregator
Time: 2026-11-16T12:34:29.125116Z
Version: 0.1.5
CliAgentSubCohorts: 4
DesktopAgentSubCohorts: 0
FlushedFromSave: true
The value 4 is 1 << 2: the Codex CLI bit. The name had made it through the input summary, the matcher, the persisted state, and the emitted report.
I also tested the developer-tool path separately, using a copy of cmd.exe named gcc.exe. It set developer-tool slot 3. With optional diagnostic data allowed, I found the resulting Segments.SubCohorts event in DiagTrack’s upload queue with SubCohorts: [8] (1 << 3). This is the compiler/debugger category shared by GCC, Clang, and related tools, rather than a separate bit for each executable.
Following the report toward Microsoft
An ETW event by itself would not establish that anything leaves the machine. ETW is used for plenty of local diagnostics. The more revealing question was who was listening to this particular report.
The VM already had a Diagtrack-Listener session subscribed to both ends of the path. These PowerShell queries showed its subscriptions:
Get-EtwTraceProvider -SessionName Diagtrack-Listener `
-Guid '{2839ff94-8f12-4e1b-82e3-af7af77a450f}'
Get-EtwTraceProvider -SessionName Diagtrack-Listener `
-Guid '{00de11a2-ec06-58e5-1a2b-21bb21dbee4b}'
The first subscription, for KernelProcess, had MatchAnyKeyword=0x400000000002, which includes the summary’s 0x400000000000 keyword. The second, for Microsoft.Windows.MissionControlAggregator, had MatchAnyKeyword=0xe00000000000, which includes the report’s 0x800000000000 keyword. Both had level 255.
The subscription showed where the report could go. To check whether Windows actually queued it for transmission, I inspected DiagTrack’s EventStore.db and decompressed the JSON batches in its AsimovEvents table. This revealed an important condition: the agent-usage report is treated as optional diagnostic data.
When optional diagnostic data is allowed (enabled by default in the US setup I tested), Windows queues the coding-agent usage report for upload to Microsoft. I verified this by finding the agent event itself in DiagTrack’s upload queue:
{
"name": "Microsoft.Windows.MissionControlAggregator.Segments.SubCohorts",
"ext": {
"privacy": { "isRequired": false }
},
"data": {
"Version": "0.1.5",
"CliAgentSubCohorts": [4],
"DesktopAgentSubCohorts": [0],
"FlushedFromSave": true
}
}
I checked this with a clean installation of Windows 11 Home ARM64 using the latest official ISO, with the region set to the United States. Before changing any privacy settings, the Diagnostic data toggle was already enabled and labeled Include Optional. After completing setup with those defaults, AllowTelemetry was 3, confirming that optional diagnostic data was enabled.
Optional diagnostic data is therefore enabled by default, at least in the US setup I tested. If you did not opt out of it during installation or disable optional diagnostic data later, it is still enabled and your coding agent usage data is being uploaded. You can decline optional diagnostics during setup or turn off Send optional diagnostic data later under Settings → Privacy & security → Diagnostics & feedback. Microsoft’s diagnostics and privacy guide explains how to change it.

Amazon’s Decoy Deals: Groundwork Investigation Reveals Nearly 1 in 3 Black Friday and Cyber Monday “Deals” Advertised by Amazon Offered No Savings to Shoppers, Nearly 1 in 10 Were Actually More Expensive
A blockbuster new investigation from Groundwork Collaborative exposes how Amazon shoppers may be getting hosed during the retail giant’s much-touted Black Friday and Cyber Monday sale events. The report, Deal or Decoy? Amazon’s Deceptive Discounts, examines products the retailer showcased as some the “best” discounts of the year to shoppers hunting for bargains during these sale events. A closer look at hundreds of these “deals” finds that Amazon frequently offered shoppers the same price, or in some cases a higher price, during recent holiday sale events than they could have paid just weeks earlier.
The report’s authors, Breyon Williams, Elizabeth Pancotti, and Lindsay Owens, find that nearly 1 in 3 of these Amazon “deals” offered no real savings – the exact same products were sold for the same price or less in the month before the sale. During Black Friday and Cyber Monday sales in the past two years, prices for nearly 1 in 10 products advertised as “deals” were higher than what customers could have paid for the products in the month leading up to the sales.
Groundwork’s analysis examined the prices of more than 350 products included on Amazon’s own deal pages for the Black Friday and Cyber Monday sale events in 2024 and 2025. These pages promote these deals as some of the “best” deals available. Amazon’s Black Friday 2025 deals page says, “With so many deals to explore, we’ve handpicked some of the best ones you’ll find.”
Groundwork Collaborative President and CEO and report co-author Lindsay Owens, shared the following reaction:
“Millions of Americans wait all year for Black Friday and Cyber Monday to score the biggest bargains on stocking stuffers and household staples. Shoppers trust that the deals advertised to them deliver real savings because Amazon promotes them that way. But it turns out that these major sales aren’t all they’re cracked up to be. Instead, our investigation reveals that shoppers who wait for these major sale events may be playing rip-off roulette.
“Amazon dominates online shopping, and families count on its sale events to stretch their dollars. With the holiday shopping season upon us and prices continuing to rise, shoppers deserve to know they can trust every deal Amazon promises them. If Amazon can’t deliver that basic promise, lawmakers should hold them accountable.”
Owens is the author of the recent book Gouged: The End of a Fair Price—And What That Means for Your Wallet, which exposes how corporations are sophisticated pricing schemes to overcharge consumers.
- The report’s key findings include:
- The price of nearly 1 in 3 products that Amazon described as “deals” was the same – or higher – during sale events than what customers could have purchased them for in the month leading up to the sale.
