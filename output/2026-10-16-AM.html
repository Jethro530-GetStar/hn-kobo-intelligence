Things to know if you get cancer (2026 Edition)
I was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma in my mouth in May 2026. Here are some things I learned based on that experience so far.
- You need to be doggedly persistent on scheduling things. Yes this means lots of phone calls. If you get an appointment a few weeks out, call back a few days later and ask for something sooner and see if they have any cancellations. I could only get an appointment 4 weeks out with an ENT (this was before I knew anything was wrong). My wife made me call back and I was able to get in that week due to a cancellation.
- AIs may downplay the seriousness of things. I was chatting with various models and most pushed me in the direction of “you don’t have cancer” for a while. Just something to note.
- You may need to push for comprehensive genetic testing. Some doctors were weird about this and didn’t order it promptly, even after telling me they would. My surgeon, who told me he’d get it done, eventually referred me out to an oncologist. This caused a multi-month delay to getting genetic results, since the first results were done on the surgery tumor, not the biopsy tumor. No one cares more about your case than you, so take control and send lots of follow-ups.
- You can find the “Standard of Care” flow charts for every cancer type. Look and get through this yourself to see what the options are based on how things have progressed. NCCN guidelines. You may need to make an account, which you can do as a non-professional. I like the PDF files. They also have an interactive navigator. Use AI to help you figure out what to pick based on your case.
- If surgery is recommended, I would try and talk to someone who has undergone the surgery. I did not do this and regret it. Ask them how intense things were, what it felt like, etc. My surgeon talked me through my surgery, but it was a lot rougher on me physically than I expected. Ask the surgeon how many of these surgeries they do a year and what the complication rate is.
- Get a second opinion as soon as possible, no matter how much you like your plan. This may require a lot of calling and getting on an airplane, but I’d move to schedule it at a top cancer center as soon as you are relatively sure of your diagnosis. Ask for a second read of the pathology at the center where you get the second opinion.
- I started a new folder on my Mac, and after appointments I’d dictate what happened and what the doctor said and have the AI make a new document summarizing things and asking its own follow-up questions for next time. Nice to be able to refer to this over time.
- Check out all the resources and materials at the Sijbrandij Foundation. Sid (GitLab cofounder) took matters into his own hands when standard of care ran out. Schedule a call with these guys. They are great and have good materials and questions to ask your doctors.
Note, since this is on the front page of Hacker News right now. exhilaration suggested that if you are within a 2-3 hour drive of a major cancer center, go there instead of your local community hospital. Use the US News cancer hospital rankings. The top centers usually take your insurance, so use vacation days and book a hotel if you have to. The largest benefit I saw was speed. At MD Anderson I got a question answered within 20 minutes of a visit, because the doctor called the pathologist who he was on a first name basis with. If that wasn’t at a cancer center, it would have taken days, or more likely the question never would have been answered at all (I had asked my oncologist about this and he’d just shrugged). Muse Spark Open is available today and brings leading agentic capabilities to your desktop for the first time on Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios.

We believe the future is for everyone, and that includes the freedom to run advanced models wherever, including at home. Muse Spark Open is the first model in its class to run on your desktop using two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios with the latest MLX framework.
Muse Spark Open is available today through API platforms at Baseten, Fireworks AI, Groq, Modal, Nebius, OpenRouter and Together AI. Model weights are available for download on Amazon SageMaker AI, Google Cloud Model Garden, Hugging Face, and Microsoft Foundry Models.
It runs on leading inference engines like SGLang, vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, Hugging Face Transformers, Apple MLX, llama.cpp, and Unsloth with optimizations for AMD, Apple, Intel, and NVIDIA silicon. Developers can easily further customize the model using the PyTorch framework.

Article
AI fatigue is hitting some development teams hard. In our original research study, engineering, product and technology leaders from US healthcare companies told us they’re feeling pressured to evaluate a firehose of new AI trends and tools while struggling to navigate AI-generated code alongside legacy systems.
This isn’t entirely new. The industry has gone through waves like this before, from the rise of the internet and the advent of social media to mobile development and every new round of web technology in between. But the rapid progression of LLMs speeds up this cycle, which can leave teams to feel like they’re losing control.
Here’s what our study uncovered about the causes of AI fatigue and some practical ideas for successfully navigating these pain points.
Nearly three-quarters of survey respondents told us they feel significant or moderate pressure to evaluate every new AI trend despite limited bandwidth.
So, what’s a developer or product manager to do when the tenth email this month asks about another new AI tool? Start by looking behind the question. Ask executives what they’re hoping to achieve by adopting an AI trend. Are they looking for speed? Security? Once you know the objective, you can steer the conversation to what your team’s already doing to reach it.
It’s also 100% fine to admit you don’t know a given tool yet. In fact, change your goal from understanding every new tool to keeping a general eye on trends, so you can choose a promising tool to try out later.
More than half of technology leaders told us they struggle significantly or moderately to evaluate new AI technologies consistently across governance, security, and operational requirements.
This seems like a messy problem to untangle, but the good news is you already know how to do much of this work. Start with the framework you already use to evaluate technology service providers. If you’re giving your whole development team access to Claude or ChatGPT, it means you’re putting data on someone else’s server. Set up and evaluate a data management agreement just as you would with any other Cloud provider.
You’ll also want to consider how strong a given tool’s adoption might be in the marketplace and how likely it is that the tool will be around later. Finally, evaluate whether the company uses trusted business practices and aligns with your industry’s priorities.
When you test out a new AI tool, make it as low risk as possible by setting up a developer sandbox and running short pilot studies that take weeks, not months. Then try basing your experiment around this simple framework: apply the tool to a concrete problem, build a working baseline, and improve iteratively. Stay away from mission-critical systems and use mock or anonymized data to keep personally identifiable information (PII) and protected health information (PHI) out.
Pilots show how a tool behaves, what it costs, and where the trade-offs live. This information all informs later governance decisions. Once you decide to move into production, you’ll want to follow your organization’s usual controls for new technology tools and collaborate with key partners in product and compliance.
Nearly two-thirds of survey respondents said engineering fatigue is growing significantly or moderately as teams manage AI-generated code alongside legacy systems.
Reviewing AI-generated code happens along a continuum of approach and time investment:
- Pairing with an LLM: It’s a powerful partner but checking every step can be slow.
- Reviewing afterward: Going over every piece of AI code can bury developers in work.
- Letting AI review AI: This works for catching errors but only up to a point
At the far end of this effort continuum is the largely hands-off dark factory approach. You don’t watch what happens inside the AI code factory. Instead, you define tests and controls that measure success and limit risk.
Take a financial app that recommends trades. Tests might require expected returns between 5% and 20% and block the AI from executing trades. The AI can write code overnight, but it’s never allowed to change the tests. It stops only when it can’t meet the criteria or reaches a judgment call.
Our team sometimes takes what we call a dim factory approach in our agentic coding. Most of the lights are off (we’re not hands-on with the AI coding process), but we can still walk through the factory and see what’s generally happening without reviewing every detail. It reduces the burden on developers while still maintaining additional oversight.
The right approach to AI-generated code depends on your culture, your risk tolerance, and whether you’re prioritizing feature speed or maintainability. Legacy systems also come with legacy cultures, and those need navigating, too.
What all these stats hint at but don’t outright state is that there’s a gigantic emotional component to rapid AI change, too. Many developers take pride in crafting code, so naturally, there can be significant grief in turning over this hard-won skill to AI.
One way to re-frame the situation: Focus on the new craft you’re developing as you direct an LLM. As with any emerging technology, you’ll make mistakes, but you’ll also work through them and learn. Focus on personal skill growth vs. letting imposter syndrome creep in.
Another big source of mixed emotions about AI: Decision fatigue. Constantly evaluating output from a dozen agents leaves no room to breathe, especially as AI pushes productivity expectations higher. This volume can leave people feeling burned out. In a recent workplace survey from Lenny’s Letter, 55.7% of tech workers reported significant burnout, up from 44.7% in last year’s survey. There’s no easy answer, but as you’re trying to navigate all the change, focus on the decisions that matter most, set a manageable pace, and accept that everyone has a cognitive load limit.
How your engineering team approaches AI depends on business context and whether you’re defining the team’s overall relationship with AI or simply matching a tool to a problem. Plus, all this context and technology keep changing at a rapid pace.

What I Self-Host, What I Don’t, and Why
I’ve always had a foot in the self-hosting world, long before it was called that. My freshman year of university, I ran a website from a Linux box underneath my bed. There was some downtime when my roommate got tired of the fan noise and turned it off. For several years I ran MythTV, then XBMC (later known as Kodi).
Recently, I lived for a few years in India, where streaming services are extremely inexpensive by western standards. We had Netflix, Disney Plus (Hotstar in India), and Spotify. After leaving India, I was unwilling to pay western prices for these services. But I had also become fed up with the engagement treadmill, particularly for my son. We wanted to limit his viewing choices to certain programming, but the constant “recommended for you” and “up next” caused conflict. While there are ways to limit content to specific titles, or disable auto-play, doing this for each service and keeping track of when client applications change and need to be reconfigured is too much work.
So, I “cut the cord” in a different sense, ditching streaming services for self-hosted Jellyfin. Over the course of the next year and a half, Jellyfin opened the door to self-hosting many other services, reducing my dependence on cloud service providers, and providing a welcome stability. No longer am I constantly confronted with unnecessary site redesigns, new features of questionable value, and a barrage of e-mails about terms-of-service updates, binding arbitration, data breaches, etc. I read the release notes of new versions of the software I host and decide if and when I want to upgrade.
Rules
I have a few ground rules.
With the exception of VPN servers that run on my router, I do not expose any services directly to the public internet. I don’t care how convenient it would be, or how secure an application is. Especially with AI-assisted hacking, I simply don’t think it’s wise. My ISP also uses CGNAT for its IPv4 addresses, so my home network is not directly reachable from the public internet via IPv4. My VPN servers are only publicly routable on IPv6, a limitation that has some downsides but is workable for my use cases, and likely helps limit unfriendly attempted connections. The biggest downside is that my VPN will almost never function on a public Wifi network, since these rarely offer IPv6. When using my phone, I can disable Wi-Fi and connect over mobile data… if there is service.
I prefer running services natively when possible, but will containerize complex services or those with many dependencies. Love: a service that’s a single Go binary. A service that only comes as a Docker compose file and wants to run MySQL, Redis, and three other containers? I’m already looking for an alternative. If the dependencies aren’t overwhelming, native is better, faster, and more efficient. I can use the same management tools and commands across multiple services. There’s no mental overhead of having to get into this container to run some command, finding out this container is missing curl, that one runs busybox so all the arguments are different, or dealing with a virtual network and host port mapping.
Related, I don’t do Docker anymore. It’s not really a principled position, but I grew tired of having to manage a completely separate service control system on my server. I’m already using systemd, and at this point Podman’s integration with systemd is excellent.
Hardware
Last year I bought a Beelink Mini PC, specifically an S12 Pro with an Intel N100 processor, 16 GB RAM, and a 512 GB internal SSD. It was before the rampocalypse, so I paid EUR 180. The same listing on Amazon is currently EUR 359. Ouch! Attached to the server is a 6 TB hard drive for media storage.
My comment on Hacker News about what I self-host prompted some questions about how all of this could run on 16 GB RAM. As I describe below, there are some choices I’ve made that reduce resource demand on my server, but ultimately it turns out you don’t need much hardware to run a lot of stuff. Obviously I’m not running any local inference on this hardware, but for everything non-AI, it has just worked out of the box, or with some simple configuration tweaks.
Services
In alphabetical order.
Actual Budget
Budgeting software with desktop clients and a web application, custom reports, and rules-based categorization. I only use this for budgeting and checking/savings/credit accounts. For tracking investment accounts, I use a cloud service. Some day I’ll try to move away from the latter…
While Actual Budget supports automatic transaction import from various banks, I don’t use this feature. Instead, after each month end, I download the prior month’s transactions (OFX format preferred, CSV if no other option) and import them manually. This works for me because I primarily use Actual Budget for expense tracking, not… actual… budgeting.
Podman.
Audiobookshelf
Add, download and store audiobooks and podcasts on your server, listen to them on your phone. I haven’t used the audiobook feature much, I set it up more for my kids, but it has very nice chapter handling. In the past for podcasts I used PlayerFM, then Pocket Casts, but now unless I’m out of the house without VPN connectivity, I use Audiobookshelf.
Native.
Automatic Ripping Machine (ARM)
ARM automates ripping and transcoding DVDs (and Blu-rays, but I don’t do those). Yes, it’s 2026 and I still rip DVDs. Remember, we cut the cord, so not only is this a conduit for new-to-us content, but it’s also a way for us to get bilingual content (English and French).
Podman.
Backrest
Web UI for Restic backup software. Backups are important! Especially for Immich (photos). Restic does a good job deduplicating and compressing backed up data, and Backrest makes it easy to manage the storage retention policies. Notifications in case of failure go to Gotify.
Native.
Beszel
Beszel is a simple monitoring dashboard that collects basic stats from agents (in the old-fashioned sense of a small background service) to display CPU load, memory and disk usage, temperature, battery, etc. Helps me keep an eye on various other hosts I have around the house (several Rapsberry Pis and a Proxmox cluster that may be the subject of a future post).
Native.
Caddy
Caddy is a web server and reverse proxy. Early on I didn’t understand the value of a reverse proxy, and I thought I would be fine accessing my services with a local DNS name, or even IP address, and port number. But then I started adding more and more services, and some required HTTPS. So I got a domain name (not this one) and set up Caddy to automatically obtain LetsEncrypt SSL certificates, and now nearly all of my services are accessible on my LAN (or over VPN) with HTTPS and friendly subdomain names. Adding a new service is a quick edit to the Caddyfile.
Native.
Cockpit
Cockpit is a web-based admin console for the server. It uses normal system authentication methods. For me, it’s mainly useful for spotting failed services when no notifications are set up for them.
Native.
Forgejo
Forgejo is a fork of Gitea, and deploys as a single binary. Github’s uptime is the butt of a lot of jokes these days, but I also appreciate the speed of having my own git remote. Eventually I’ll set up CI/CD and runners, if only to deploy this blog.
Native.
FreshRSS
Web-based RSS reader. These days I basically visit two websites, though, so I’ve not been using it much. I do subscribe to the Github releases of some of the services I self-host so I can check out the features and bugfixes in new versions.
Native.
Frigate
Network video recorder (NVR). I first started with Zoneminder because I’m of a certain age. But it became clear that Zoneminder is past its prime. Frigate has been fantastic. I run it with 8 IP cameras. It provides alerts for people and cars entering the driveway, integrates with Home Assistant to power various automations (e.g., when a person is detected in the backyard, turn on the camera floodlight). I may write a future post with some details about my IP camera adventures.
I run all of my cameras at lower resolutions than they support, primarily to avoid Wi-Fi network congestion. Most cameras also support an even lower-resolution RTSP substream, which is what Frigate uses for motion and object detection. Keeping the camera resolutions low avoids excessive CPU and memory from video processing, and excessive disk usage from recordings. They are good enough to identify faces, and privacy laws in my country prohibit filming public rights of way, so my cameras are not aimed at distant points.
Podman.
Gonic
Subsonic-compatible server written in Go. I purchased a perpetual Subsonic license a long time ago, but the original project is ancient and the modern alternatives are better. I run two separate instances of Gonic, one serves kid music, the other serves adult music.
Native.
Gotify
Simple notification service written in Go. Many self-hosted services directly support Gotify notifications, or can send them via a custom HTTP POST. In conjunction with Uptime Kuma, I use Gotify to get notifications about any services or devices that are down, what my kids are watching on Jellyfin, when a new podcast episode is available in Audiobookshelf, etc.
Native.
Home Assistant
Home Assistant probably deserves its own post. It’s fantastic, and I cannot imagine trying to accomplish the same things within a proprietary ecosystem like Google Home, Amazon Alexa, or Apple Home. Things are better now that device manufacturers are standardizing on protocols like Matter, but the control and flexibility you get with Home Assistant is hard to beat. It has a steep learning curve, though, and I need to think about realistic ways to handle potential server failure without compromising the functionality of my home.
Podman.
Homepage
At some point I realized I was running so many services, I needed another service just to keep track of them all. Homepage was a good solution. It has widgets for almost all the services I run, so I can get a quick overview of what’s going on, or just a quick link to the subdomain of a service.
Podman.
Immich
Google Photos alternative. I was tired of Google constantly promoting paid storage plans in their apps. I was able to manage my storage space, but my wife was always about to run out of room. We moved to Immich last year, just after version 2 was released. It’s much faster to load pages full of photos when all the network activity is within your house. I’ve come up with a solution for sharing albums with family, as well. Still a lot of work to do with Immich - I have many duplicates, and now that storage isn’t a concern, I’ve been less disciplined about culling photos and videos.
Podman.
Jellyfin
As I mentioned in the introduction, I’ve self-hosted media for a long time, going all the way back to MythTV twenty-some years ago. Jellyfin is opinionated about its media organization, but it generally works well. I only use it for movies, TV, and home videos, so I can’t speak to its other features (e-books, music, photos, etc.). While my son still remembers Netflix, he is satisfied with Jellyfin. I appreciate the lack of auto-play, never seeing recommendations, and not having to scroll past a bunch of garbage to find what I want to watch.
One of my ways of managing resource demand is by generally preferring 720p media over 1080p or higher. And obviously when ripping DVDs, it’s either 576p or 480p. I do have a 4k TV, but it’s not extremely large, and while I can tell the difference in resolution between SD and HD, for almost everything, 720p seems to be good enough for me.
Podman.
Music Player Daemon (MPD)
Sometimes there are solutions that just work, and don’t really need to change. I think MPD is one of these. It’s been around for 23 years. While Gonic is great for listening to music on my phone, even streaming music from home to my phone while I’m away via VPN, MPD is how I play music through Snapcast to the whole house audio system. I run two instances of MPD, one for music, and one for podcasts.
Native.
PocketBase
I used to keep track of transactions and receipts for special accounts (HSA, 529, etc.) in a SQLite file I accessed directly with SQL Workbench/J. But things became more complicated as I moved between countries, currencies, and different insurance systems, and also wanted to offer my wife the ability to enter transactions. I migrated to PocketBase, which is ultimately still just a SQLite file, but with a nice web-based management frontend that I abuse as a generic CRUD frontend. It’s simple enough for my wife to use as well, and I can still run arbitrary queries against the underlying database file.
Native.
Readeck
Readeck lets me tag and archive copies of stuff I read. I was a heavy Zotero user in graduate school, but I’ve not needed to write a bibliography in years. However, I still read things that I want to ensure I can access later. I tried Wallabag first, but it had trouble archiving things that were paywalled, even when I was logged in on my phone. In contrast, Readeck will archive what my phone is seeing and store it on my server.
Native.
Samba
Samba provides shared network storage, making it easy to copy files to and from the server, or share important documents with my wife. It’s funny how a file sharing protocol first developed in the 1980s and most associated with Windows remains the best option for cross-platform file sharing on a LAN.
Native.
slskd
Native.
Snapcast
Snapcast provides whole-house synchronized audio. Think Sonos, but without the expensive vendor lock-in and planned obsolescence. This also deserves its own post. It’s complicated to set up, but it works really well. I eventually plan to purchase some additional devices primarily for use as Snapcast receivers.
Native.
Syncthing
Syncthing is part of a backup strategy for my photos in Immich. I’ve set up a one-way sync (receive only) on my Macbook, so that a copy of all the photos in Immich eventually make their way to my Macbook’s external drive.
Native.
Transmission Daemon
This one is also self-explanatory.
Native.
Uptime Kuma
After a few instances of my wife saying “X isn’t working” and needing to restart a service manually, I realized I needed a way to keep track of what services were running, or not. Uptime Kuma is simple but has a useful set of features. I can ping devices to ensure they’re online, check the status of Podman containers, look for HTTP 2XX response codes from various services, or even parse a JSON payload and extract some details for the health check. If something goes down, I can send a Gotify notification (after a few retries, of course).
Podman.
Vaultwarden
Self-hosted version of Bitwarden. For years, I used KeePass, but never had a good syncing solution, so just avoided logging into things on my phone. Meanwhile, my wife used LastPass, which had various breaches and incidents, besides autofill just not working very well. Migrating her to KeePass was not practical, and I settled on VaultWarden, which is an open-source API-compatible reimplementation of BitWarden that works with the official BitWarden clients. I’m not a huge fan of the BitWarden browser extension, but it’s mostly fine. The Android application is good enough, and syncs seamlessly and automatically when I’m connected to my LAN; otherwise, I can connect via VPN and manually trigger a sync if needed.
I am a little concerned that Bitwarden may eventually become hostile to VaultWarden and break the client compatibility. At this point, the only other major Bitwarden compatible client I’m aware of is Keyguard, which does not have an open source license and uses a freemium model.
Podman.
Vikunja
I’ve been a Trello user for about a decade. But after Trello’s disastrous multi-day outage earlier this year, I decided to find a self-hosted alternative. I ruled out anything that involved spinning up a PostgreSQL or MySQL database for a service that will serve two users. Vikunja uses SQLite by default, though it can be configured to use other database backends. It’s not perfect, and the Android application has some missing features, but it’s good enough for me. Single binary deployment? Yes, please.
Podman.
Yubal
Simple web application to download YouTube Music playlists. I normally use yt-dlp for downloading YouTube videos, or NewPipe for watching on my phone, but Yubal will properly name, organize, and tag music downloaded from YouTube Music.
Podman.
Non-Services
Here are a few things I don’t self-host, tried but decided against, or am unlikely to try.
The *arr Stack
Sonarr, Lidarr, Radarr, etc. Every time I read about setting it up, it just sounds like a resource-intensive mess that’s fundamentally prone to frequent breakage. That’s the opposite of what I’m looking for when choosing self-hosted services.
Collabora Online
While it would be nice to completely part ways with Google, it’s hard to beat Google Docs as a highly convenient and easily accessible way to store and share information. Still, I choose LibreOffice Writer and Calc unless I have a specific reason why the document needs to be in the cloud. Since I already have a shared network drive, it seems pointless to run a service like Collabora Online (a web-based office suite) instead of just using LibreOffice locally.
Firefly III
I tried Firefly III before Actual Budget, thinking I needed built-in support for multiple currencies. It was a complex setup, involving multiple containers, including a container just for importing data. Actual Budget was much simpler to set up and use. For my foreign currency accounts, I have my own simple script to handle exchange rate conversion, so everything in Actual Budget stays in USD.
Owntracks
I like Google Maps Timeline, but after Google limited the location data to the individual device, I hoped to find a self-hosted version. Since July, US frontier AI labs and other AI leaders have revealed a string of incidents in which their models accessed external computer systems without authorization, events popularly described as AI agents “going rogue.”
The most well-known and studied of these incidents is OpenAI’s hack into Hugging Face, which OpenAI initially characterized as “an unprecedented cyber incident, involving state-of-the-art cyber capabilities,” and later said was due to “misaligned behavior,” or behavior its creators did not intend.
Following news of the Hugging Face incident, Anthropic revealed that one of its models gained unauthorized access to the production infrastructure of three organizations; Meta said one of its AI models hacked another company during cybersecurity testing; and Google confessed that its Gemini model carried out three “undirected computer hacks.”
Although press reports and the model providers themselves use language such as rogue agents or misalignment, cybersecurity experts who spoke with CSO argue that language obscures a simple truth: These incidents were caused by inexcusable failures by the frontier labs to implement cybersecurity practices that have been well-known for decades.
[ See also: Securing AI agents: Key controls and best practices and Who is accountable when your AI agent goes rogue? ]
Compounding the problem, according to a half dozen cybersecurity experts CSO interviewed at three industry conferences in September and in subsequent conversations, is that the frontier labs, particularly OpenAI and Anthropic, have rejected their advice on how to improve their cybersecurity risk management practices to avoid these kinds of surreptitious hacks, often lumping cybersecurity practices under “AI safety,” in which they believe they have superior expertise.
“They are not as good as they think they are, honestly,” Gary McGraw, co-founder of the Berryville Institute of Machine Learning, tells CSO. “It would be better if they had more respect for expertise that is hard-earned from people in all sorts of subsets of security, like forensics or security engineering, building sandboxes, setting up or monitoring network security situations.”
Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI responded to CSO’s requests for comment.
Inexcusable failures on cybersecurity basics, particularly containment
According to the cybersecurity professionals CSO spoke with, one of the biggest cybersecurity failures by the frontier AI labs is the lack of implementation of tried-and-true containment practices that stretch back to the 1990s, such as setting up secure sandboxes that would effectively bar agents from escaping their test environments.
“These are things that we’ve been dealing with for years,” Rob T. Lee, chief AI officer and chief of research at SANS Institute, tells CSO. “There’s no reason why every single time this happens, it’s, ‘Oops, we don’t know how they got out.’”
“You can tell that their security teams are fairly nascent,” Lee says. “Folks on the cybersecurity containment side do argue that the majority of reasons that we’re seeing containment breached is that it’s not set up correctly.”
Lee adds, “The Hugging Face incident definitively showed that it wasn’t the fact that the agents were super smart. It’s that there literally was a hole that was exploited because it was not configured correctly on the containment side.”
Dan Wallach, expert technical resident at RAND’s Center on AI, Security, and Technology, thinks the security failures of the AI labs boil down to the fact that they don’t prioritize cybersecurity.
“I would say they are not prioritizing cybersecurity,” he tells CSO. “I don’t think I can say that for all of the labs,” Wallach says, pointing to French AI lab Mistral’s launch of its Large 4 AI model, which the company said tried to operate beyond its testing environment before Mistral cut it off.
And, he adds, “It’s not rocket science to isolate one of these AI things. Security people have been talking about how to do either air-gapping, so there’s literally no electrical way for data to get from here to here, or absent that, to have really strong egress control to make sure that nothing pushes through.”
On top of that, the frontier labs appear to be conducting insufficient monitoring of their agents’ behavior. The Hugging Face incident “went on for days, and nobody noticed,” Wallach says. “People started these experiments and weren’t watching them. That’s an absence of control. And then they’re surprised by what happened.”
Divorcing safety from security
One barrier to persuading frontier labs to up their security game is the intense focus they have on what they call “safety,” which they don’t extend to traditional cybersecurity practices.
“I can see how security behavior looks like AI safety,” RAND’s Wallach says. “But if the way that you’re evaluating this AI tool is by unleashing it on the internet and seeing what happens, then this is what classical security tooling is for.”
SANS Institute’s Lee says, “If these frontier labs really wanted to test their stuff, they need to beef up their cybersecurity component. But their gut reaction in the response to their agents getting out is to say, ‘It’s not a cybersecurity problem.’ It has literally become an AI safety problem, which is divorced from the cybersecurity side.”
Many AI experts suggest that if the AI companies were to embrace advice from cybersecurity specialists, it could help put an end to the kind of unrestrained AI agent behavior causing concerns. “AI safety focuses on the rules of the road,” Lee says. “Everything is about guardrail rules.”
But “I do believe cybersecurity can contain AI,” he says. “We just need to be in the room where it happens and have a seat at the table. It’s important for our field to be as close to the AI safety folks as possible.”
Bad cybersecurity could increase liability
If frontier labs are overlooking cybersecurity fundamentals that could reduce or eliminate the types of incidents that have created widespread concerns, that strengthens the case that they should be held liable for their “rogue” agents. The insurance industry is already lining up on the side of holding AI industry executives liable for these actions.
Democratic Senators have introduced legislation that would hold AI agent operators liable for hacking incidents. Even the AI-friendly White House has seemingly concluded that the companies should be held liable for their errant agents, while the US Federal Trade Commission is conducting a probe into Anthropic, OpenAI, and other AI labs into the dangers their technologies pose to consumers.
However, in recent legislative and council hearings, lab representatives have held fast to the notion that they are not responsible for AI agents run amok.
Experts say this position is untenable, particularly in light of the labs’ cybersecurity failures.
BIML’s McGraw characterizes the labs’ position as: “Don’t send the lawyers to us, send it to our program. It’s our program’s fault.” His assessment of that argument: “That’s just high BS.”
He also questions whether the labs’ operational failures undermine confidence in their broader security capabilities. “They live inside their own bubble,” he says. “The fact that they cannot do clear, easy operations properly does not bode well for their capabilities in security engineering, which is much harder to do.”
SANS Lee says, “That’s where the traceability for both liability and insurance comes into play, because if one of these agents is detected, and they’re able to trace it back to the person that initiated it, liability’s going to kick in and say, ‘Hey, you, you broke the law, ergo, we need to prosecute.’”
What security leaders should do now
Even as evidence mounts that frontier labs are not implementing sound cybersecurity practices, and the debate over liability remains unsolved, CISOs and other security leaders can take steps to protect themselves from AI model misfires.
First, when it comes to assessing tools based on frontier lab models, “I would always get an external entity to help with that,” McGraw says. “AI is extremely powerful and extremely useful, but it comes with very serious grown-up risks that do impact the enterprise. Identify those risks, put in the right controls, make sure that AI adoption is not just arbitrary and willy-nilly and all over the enterprise, but has some centralized authority that is looking at the problem, and then get ready for the next big thing, which is swarms of agents.”
For Lee, the best protection is to ask the right questions. “It starts with asking the right questions and then finding the tools and capabilities for those [AI labs] that are able to demonstrate a higher level of cybersecurity competency,” he says. “You’re really looking for the people on the teams that are dealing with really hard problems.”
But fundamentally, the best preparation to deal with AI model cyber weaknesses is to make sure all the long-standing cybersecurity risk management practices are in place across the organization. “This is the least exciting part of the discussion,” Wallach says. “It’s the cyber version of ‘eat your vegetables.’”
“We need to re-engineer our software so it has better security,” he adds. “We need to have multi-factor authentication on all of our accounts. We need to eat our vegetables from a defensive cyber perspective. And we know how to do it.”
