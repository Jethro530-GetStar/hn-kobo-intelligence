Those I trusted to build out a greenfield product had their positions eliminated. The status quo was more important to maintain than resolving our operational issues with the next generation of our infrastructure.
That first meeting crushed my soul. I heard my ears ring more violently than ever. The color drained from the world. I could barely walk straight.
And, the next day, I went to a house party with two of my reports present knowing that both of them would lose their job next week with zero notice.
I know first hand what it is like to know and later execute on a layoff.
Finally, it was my time. I've had grief several times expecting it to come earlier. When it finally came, I took it well for a few hours.
Then my heart went to over 100 bpm as the fear and doubt for my future set in. And it didn't ease off for over a week.
Do you know how hard it is to sleep when all you can hear is your own heartbeat? Do you know how hard it is to function with four effective hours of sleep when you offer your body ten? It is rough.
Several in my social circles are having difficulty too. Six or Seven in a DevOps interest group have been laid off or have received a layoff notice in the last month. Work at one of FAANG of all places and oops, you're not important now! Here's 6 months of severance, we're so nice. "AI took my job" is far more fun to say than "AI contributed to my role being eliminated."
The frontier models are not good enough to replace an experienced engineer. They still are not, even though the marketing by the US frontier labs (and their 'I quit because I'm scared' employees) scream of what's to come.
These models lack the taste that makes for effective product decision making. It is cheaper than ever for a product manager to say "Someone complains about this on reddit, let's implement it", while the tasteful leader says 'No, that's not what our product is. Making that means we commit to it for a long time and our customers really want {something else we already do, but not perfectly} instead.'
These models lack the foresight to predict consequences weeks, months, years down the line on decisions and commitments — in operating costs, complexity, accessibility, and affordability to the very customers the product is for. All those secondary things really matter in making a product successful and worth buying. The slop services out there omit these secondary things all the time and you can taste it.
And yet, models are evidently able to give Shopify the confidence to yeet react-native out into the ocean and reach back to native iOS and android apps, entirely machine-coded while overseen by otherwise experienced and tasteful software developers.
And, I agree with Shopify. Models are getting good enough where they can be driven step by step, with a carefully crafted harness, to reproduce and port the behaviors they've A/B tested into oblivion as humans. They can push a rock up a hill. Then up the next hill, and so on — when the technique can be measured and check-pointed.

I sometimes run across comments where someone says they want an open-source router. I wonder if this is a viable idea for someone to start as a small company. What makes me hesitant is that, first, it looks like this already exists in several wireless and non-wireless routers (like the GL.iNet Flint or OpenWrt One), and second, I assume hackers would just make their own.
I have been flashing DD-WRT to my routers for many years. My current setup is an Archer C9 that connects via cable to an Archer C8, which then wirelessly bridges to an R7000. All of them run DD-WRT. If I had "fun money," I would buy a low-power SFF PC with dual NICs, run OPNsense or OpenWrt, and use the wireless routers strictly as access points. Since I don't have that much to spend, I look for older DD-WRT friendly routers, usually under $30, on Craigslist or Facebook Marketplace, and flash them myself.
I see that Ubiquiti and MikroTik make quality routers with proprietary OSes for around $65 new. Protectli and Netgate offer pfSense routers starting at $259.00. The GL.iNet Flint 2 is $169.00, and the OpenWrt One is $89.
According to ResellBot (because eBay now requires a login to see sold listings with prices), the sold "DD-WRT Routers" generally go for under $160. These include the WRT3200ACM, Netgear AC1750, Linksys EA9300, and Linksys R7350.
When I asked an AI how I would go about judging interest, building, raising funds, and manufacturing, it seems doable but expensive (said $125,000 minimum in total made up of $30,000 for regulatory approval, engineering and protoyping $10,000+) and time-consuming just to get to a "golden prototype" for demos.
So if I were a "hacker" and wanted an open-source router, depending on my funds, I would either:
1. Flash DD-WRT to an existing router (used or new).
2. Buy one with it already installed, like the OpenWrt One or a Protectli device.
3. Get an SFF PC and build my own.
I remember when some comments about DropBox said it was a waste because any hacker could make that. I also know DropBox was marketed to the average person and businesses. I don't really see how a hacker friendly router could take off like that unless hackers are choosing the hardware and setting up the networks for average people. And I assume that most people setting up the networks want different qualities like brand name recognition, ease and speed of purchasing equipment (Walmart or BestBuy), and the ability to point a customer or family member to the number of solid reviews and articles saying it a good router to qualify their choices.
So, do you actually want a router that you can buy with DD-WRT pre-installed?
3. Show HN: PS2 games statically recompiled to WebAssembly

Article
PlayStation 2 games running in the browser without an emulator. The game's Emotion Engine machine code is translated ahead of time into C++, compiled to WebAssembly, and linked against a reimplementation of the PS2's operating system and hardware that draws with WebGPU.
Watch the full recording (1:24)
This is the sibling of psp-web-recomp and follows the same method. The first title brought up this way is Gran Turismo 4, chosen because it is one of the most demanding games on the system: it leans on both vector units, on exact floating point behaviour for its replays and license tests, and on graphics tricks that read rendered images back as palettes. It plays from boot through the opening movie, the menus and races, with music and sound effects, and saves to a memory card kept in the browser. A race runs at full speed, 50 frames per second for this PAL release, in Chrome on a laptop's integrated GPU at twice the PS2's resolution, which is how the recording above was made.
God of War II followed through the same scripts. It needed no game-specific code, but it found two bugs that Gran Turismo 4 had never triggered, one in the recompiler and one in the vector unit timing, and a few missing pieces of the PS2's input/output processor. It now plays from the menus through its cutscenes into combat, and holds 60 frames per second in heavy combat at the PS2's resolution in Chrome. At twice the resolution it runs at 48 to 60, limited by the laptop's integrated GPU.
No game data is included here. You bring a disc image of a game you own, and the scripts in this repository turn it into a web page on your machine.
Recompilation. eerecomp reads the game's executable, follows its control flow to find the functions and emits C++ for them in units of 16 KiB of guest code. The output keeps the R5900's quirks: 128-bit registers and the multimedia instructions that work on them, branch delay slots, the PS2's floating point unit, which has no infinities or denormals and rounds differently from IEEE, and vector unit 0 used as a coprocessor. Jumps between units become tail calls, and a thread switch unwinds the host stack and resumes at the guest address, so the generated code never needs a stack per guest thread.
A small PS2 kernel. The EE side of the operating system is answered by high-level emulation: threads, semaphores, interrupt handlers for the DMA controller and the vertical blank, alarms, and the SIF channel that connects the main CPU to the I/O processor. The I/O processor runs the game's own IRX modules, the sound driver, the disc streaming code and the controller and memory card drivers that ship on the disc, on an interpreter of its R3000 CPU, while the IOP kernel and the libraries those modules call are reimplemented in C++. The disc is read over HTTP Range requests, so only the executable is downloaded up front.
Vector units. Most of a PS2 game's 3D work happens in microprograms the game uploads to vector unit 1. They run with bit-exact PS2 float math and with the pipeline delays of the MAC, status and clip flags modelled, because games schedule their code around them. Microprograms seen during a test run are compiled ahead of time into SIMD C++; any the page meets for the first time are translated into a WebAssembly module on the spot, which takes a few milliseconds per program.
Graphics. The Graphics Synthesizer's 4 MB of video memory lives on the GPU, in its native swizzled layouts, and drawing is done by WebGPU compute shaders: triangles are binned into tiles and rasterized with the GS's own rules for texturing, palettes, alpha blending, depth and destination alpha tests. Because everything stays in GS memory, effects that render into a texture or reinterpret a framebuffer as a palette work the way they do on the console. The internal resolution can be one, two or three times the native one. A software rasterizer of the same GS remains as the reference the GPU output is compared against.
Threads. The emulation runs in a worker, vector unit 1 has a worker of its own the way it runs in parallel on the console, and the GS commands go through a ring buffer to the page thread, which owns the WebGPU device.
Sound and movies. The SPU2 is reimplemented with its 48 voices of ADPCM, ADSR envelopes and the streaming DMA games use for music, and its output goes to an AudioWorklet through shared memory. Movies are decoded by an implementation of the IPU, the PS2's MPEG-2 decoder.
The first race ran at 3 frames per second natively, with a software rasterizer and an interpreter for vector unit 1. Moving the GS to WebGPU compute shaders and compiling the vector unit programs brought it to about 70 natively, and most of the remaining work was about where the browser spent its time rather than the emulation.
The first version of the GPU renderer submitted around 650 compute dispatches per frame, because every texture upload and palette load split the batch of triangles around it. Hoisting uploads ahead of the triangles that do not depend on them, with the same output, brought that to about 80.
Gran Turismo 4 uploads the same 3 MB of textures every frame of a race. GS memory is swapped between frames, so the uploads cannot simply be skipped, but a 16 MB cache of recently uploaded contents on the GPU lets most of them be served without crossing from WebAssembly to the GPU again, which cut the data sent per frame by about 60 percent.
At three times the native resolution the frame rate dropped to 23. Profiling the shader showed that the per-triangle setup was being loaded after a branch that differed between threads, which made the whole setup divergent, and that uploads split into 1 MB pieces blocked Chrome's page thread. Loading the setup uniformly and writing each frame's data in one piece brought three times the resolution to 48 to 50 frames per second on the same integrated GPU.
The browser's own scheduling mattered too. Nested setTimeout calls are clamped to 4 ms, so the emulation loop now yields through a MessageChannel instead, and moving the emulation into a worker, so that the page thread only feeds the GPU, steadied a race that had wandered between 40 and 50 frames per second at a constant 50.
God of War II went from an ISO to a page through port.sh with only generic changes, and each one was a bug or gap that the first game had simply not exercised.
It started with silence. The game's sound driver drives its tick from one of the I/O processor's hardware timers, which nothing had implemented, so the driver never mixed a sample. The intro after the difficulty menu was a mess of huge flat polygons, and when the gameplay was about to begin the game crashed after its render list ran past the end of memory and overwrote its own vertical blank handlers. Following a corrupted linked list back with hardware watchpoints led to the recompiler: when a branch was the last instruction of a 16 KiB unit, the path that does not take the branch continued in the next unit at the delay slot instead of after it, running that instruction twice, or running the instruction a branch-likely should have skipped. That fixed the crash and the game reached combat, still with giant polygons over parts of the screen. Those came from triangles that should have been culled. God of War II's microcode reads the clip flags three instructions after the CLIP that sets them, and the vector unit model made them visible only after four, which Gran Turismo 4, whose microcode always waits longer, could not have noticed.
Along the way it also needed a boot path like the BIOS gives, a module loaded from memory instead of the disc, and the scratchpad DMA's destination chain mode.
You need git, CMake, Ninja, a C++20 compiler (clang is preferred and picked up automatically), Python 3, and 7-Zip if your disc image is in a .7z. Everything has been run on Linux on x86-64. The first run installs the Emscripten SDK into tools/emsdk, or uses the one EMSDK points to, and downloads wgpu-native for the headless test runner. Expect about 300 MB of disk space per game for the generated code and the builds; the disc image itself is linked, not copied.
git clone https://github.com/snuri00/ps2-web-recomp.git
cd ps2-web-recomp
scripts/port.sh mygame "My Game.iso"
python3 scripts/serve.py build/web-mygame games/mygame/disc.iso # http://localhost:8620/
port.sh takes a disc image (an ISO, or a ZIP or 7z archive holding one) and does the whole job. It reads the boot program named in the disc's SYSTEM.CNF, translates its machine code to C++, and then runs the game headless a few times. Static analysis cannot see every function a game reaches through pointers, so each run writes down the addresses it could not find, the script adds them as entry points and translates again, until a run gets through without new ones. The same runs record the vector unit microprograms the game uploads, which are compiled ahead of time as well. Last it builds the page into build/web-mygame. God of War II goes from ISO to page in three and a half minutes on an 8-core laptop.
The exploring runs press start and then cross every five seconds, which gets most games through their title screens, and put an empty memory card in slot 1 the way the page does, so they follow the path a player takes. --press 900:cross replaces that with your own inputs and --frames sets how long each run lasts. Frames from the last run land in games/mygame/shots, which is the quickest way to see how far a new game gets. scripts/build_web.sh mygame rebuilds only the page.
Set your expectations accordingly: two games have been brought up so far. Another game will most likely stop at a system call or an IOP library function nobody implemented yet, which the runner logs as [sys] unhandled syscall or [iop] missing <library> #<n>, or use a GS feature the renderer does not handle yet.
The page needs WebGPU for the GPU renderer. Chrome and Edge have it on Windows and macOS. On Linux it is off by default in Chrome and Chromium: turn on chrome://flags/#enable-unsafe-webgpu and chrome://flags/#enable-vulkan and restart the browser, as the page tells you when it finds no usable adapter. Without WebGPU the software renderer takes over, which is fine for menus and far too slow for 3D. On laptops with two GPUs, a canvas drawn on the discrete GPU sometimes cannot be shown on the screen; the page tries both adapters and falls back to copying frames through a 2D canvas.
The keyboard maps the face buttons to Z (cross), X (circle), A (square) and S (triangle), the shoulders to Q, W, 1 and 2, start and select to Enter and Backspace, the D-pad to the arrow keys and the left stick to I, J, K and L. Gamepads work through the browser's gamepad API, phones get on-screen controls, and F toggles fullscreen. The resolution selector reloads the page at the new size.
The page puts a memory card in slot 1 and keeps it in the browser's storage for the address you play from, so progress survives a reload. Games format and use it as they would a real card. Export card downloads it as a .ps2 file in the layout PCSX2 uses, and Import card puts such a file back in the slot, so a card can travel between browsers or come from PCSX2. ?nocard starts with the slot empty.
The page uses SharedArrayBuffer for its threads, so it has to be served cross-origin isolated, with Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp. scripts/serve.py sends both headers and supports the Range requests the disc streaming relies on. Anyone who can reach the server can load the game, so keep it to yourself.

9. Postgres isn't slow, your storage is
FULL TEXT

Article
At POSETTE 2026, Sai Srirampur presented "Postgres Isn’t Slow. Your Storage Is." a talk examining how storage affects PostgreSQL performance at scale. This post recaps the benchmark, what the results revealed, and the architecture proposed for running PostgreSQL on local NVMe in production. You can watch the full talk in the video linked below.
The talk starts with several symptoms teams running Postgres at scale may recognize: ingestion slows down, P95 read latency becomes unpredictable, autovacuum falls behind, checkpoints compete with your actual workload for resources, or logical replication accumulates lag. These problems do not always share the same cause, but they can have a common one that is easy to overlook: storage performance.
To understand why, Sai ran a benchmark comparing the same 3.3-billion-row PostgreSQL workload on baseline gp3 EBS and local NVMe. We will walk through the setup and what Sai discovered when analyzing the results, then close with the production patterns proposed for running Postgres on local NVMe.
Five scaling symptoms
There are five common issues that often appear once a PostgreSQL workload reaches hundreds of gigabytes or terabytes and is running with real concurrency and throughput.
- Slow ingestion. The first one is ingestion pipelines heavy on UPDATEs and UPSERTs. Jobs that once finished in seconds can stretch into minutes as the volume of data underneath them grows.
- Inconsistent read latency. Next one appears in workloads where most queries remain fast but a few become dramatically slower. P95 latency moves from milliseconds to seconds, dashboards time out, and the uneven slowdown makes the cause harder to isolate.
- VACUUM falling behind. A third issue is autovacuum falling behind as UPDATE and UPSERT throughput grows, with table bloat accumulating from gigabytes into terabytes.
- Slower checkpoints. Checkpoints can also take longer and compete with the client workload for I/O and CPU, turning an operation that used to be invisible into a recurring latency spike.
- Logical replication lag. The last symptom appears when PostgreSQL data is streaming to systems such as Kafka or ClickHouse. WAL decoding can be slow and a growing replication slot can compound the problem.
How do these symptoms connect to storage
As the working set outgrows the memory available to PostgreSQL and the operating-system page cache, more data read require physical I/O. On slower storage, the higher cost and queuing of those reads can increase both average and tail latency.
Client queries and background maintenance also use the same storage budget. Heap and index writes, WAL synchronization, checkpoints, VACUUM, and logical-decoding spills compete for bandwidth, IOPS, and queue capacity.
Once storage reaches one of those limits, operations begin delaying one another. That is how slow storage can be the root cause of several apparently unrelated PostgreSQL problems.
What changes with local NVMe
The case for local NVMe is not that it eliminates I/O, but that it can sharply reduce its cost. The comparison contrasts millisecond-scale operations on network-attached storage with operations that may complete in tens of microseconds on local NVMe. Cache misses, WAL flushes, and maintenance I/O still happen, but they simply don’t impact the overall system as much.
The benchmark: Local NVMe vs. EBS
To better understand why this is the case, Sai set up a simple experiment to compare a similar workload on PostgreSQL running on EBS and local NVMe.
Setup
To isolate storage as the variable, the benchmark used eight identical PostgreSQL clusters: four using instance-store NVMe and four using baseline gp3 EBS provisioned at 3,000 IOPS. Each cluster ran on an m6id.4xlarge instance with 16 vCPUs, 64 GiB of RAM, and 16 GiB of shared_buffers. We used multiple instances for each storage type to normalize variability across benchmark runs.
The setup used a 482 GiB pgbench_accounts heap containing 3.3 billion rows. Each cluster then ran the same highly concurrent random-UPDATE workload for five minutes, with 64 clients and 16 worker threads:
\set aid random(1, 3300000000)
UPDATE pgbench_accounts SET abalance = abalance + 1 WHERE aid = :aid;
In short, the only difference between the two groups was the storage class.
What the benchmark showed
The benchmark produced a median 16,030 TPS for the NVMe clusters, compared with 1,734 TPS on EBS—a 9.24× difference in this test. Median transaction latency fell from 36.9 ms to 4.0 ms.
For different operations, NVMe completed a VACUUM over 10 GB of bloat in 366 seconds, compared with 964 seconds on EBS, and drained a 10 GB logical-replication backlog in 139 seconds instead of 238 seconds.
One qualification matters: these results apply to this workload and these storage configurations. The EBS side used baseline gp3 provisioned at 3,000 IOPS, different EBS setup would most likely produce different results.
Explaining the gap
The throughput gap becomes easier to understand by looking at where the 37 ms of EBS transaction latency went.
In this benchmark, page reads accounted for about 19 ms on EBS, compared with 0.3 ms on NVMe. WAL fsync at commit added another 11 ms on EBS versus 1.5 ms on NVMe, while lock and scheduler overhead contributed about 5 ms versus 0.2 ms. CPU work itself was roughly the same—about 2 ms in both cases.
In other words, most of the additional EBS latency came from waiting on storage, not from PostgreSQL doing more CPU work.
A mid-run pg_stat_activity snapshot showed the same pattern from another angle. Of the 64 backends, 29 on EBS were waiting on IO:DataFileRead, compared with nine on NVMe—roughly 45% versus 14%. This was a point-in-time snapshot rather than an average across the run, but it illustrates how many more EBS sessions were stalled on reads at that moment.
The CPU profile then adds a counterintuitive observation. Over a profiling window of roughly 240 seconds, the NVMe host accumulated 2,253 CPU-seconds—about 9.4 cores busy—while the EBS host accumulated only 251 CPU-seconds, or about one core busy. Some PostgreSQL functions appeared proportionally hotter on EBS, but the absolute CPU time showed that EBS processes were spending far more time off-CPU waiting for storage.
The NVMe host kept more of its CPU capacity busy doing useful work. That helps explain the much higher throughput: cache misses, WAL flushes, and maintenance I/O still occurred, but they completed faster and left PostgreSQL spending less time blocked on storage.
The durability question
After the performance results, the talk turns to the obvious objection: instance-store NVMe is tied to the lifetime of a machine, so the local data disappears with the node. The operational question is whether PostgreSQL can combine NVMe latency with production-grade availability and recovery.
The short answer is yes, given durability is provided at the cluster level rather than by any individual Postgres node.
Running NVMe-backed Postgres on production
Running PostgreSQL on local NVMe in production means designing around storage that is fast but tied to the lifetime of a machine. Losing a node means losing its local copy of the data; instance-store volumes cannot be snapshotted through the EBS API; and available capacity depends on the selected instance type.
The production architecture presented in the talk addresses this through three main requirements: replicate the database so the service can survive node loss, maintain backups and recovery data outside the individual machines, and plan capacity around the NVMe available on each instance type. Together, these patterns make it possible to run NVMe-backed PostgreSQL confidently in production.
High availability with two standbys
The first pattern is quorum-based synchronous replication with two standby candidates. With a configuration such as ANY 1 (standby1, standby2), a transaction can commit after either standby durably acknowledges its WAL rather than waiting for the slower of the two. Placing the nodes across availability zones protects against an AZ failure.
Continuous backups to Object Storage for durability
For durability outside the database nodes, open-source tools such as WAL-G can provide physical base backups and continuous WAL archiving. With low-latency WAL shipping, this architecture can target an RPO measured in seconds and support point-in-time recovery within the retention window.
Backups and WAL should also be kept in a separate failure domain, with another regional copy when the recovery plan needs to survive a full regional outage.
Backups as the recovery foundation
Base backups and archived WAL are more than an emergency mechanism: the same recovery history can support point-in-time restores, isolated branches, deployment resizing, read-replica seeding, and disaster recovery.
Backups and replication solve different problems. In the architecture described in the talk, standbys provide rapid failover, while backups protect against corruption, operator error, and cluster-wide failure; both recovery paths need regular testing.
Examples from production
This pattern is not only theoretical, the talk cites public examples from Instacart, which has described PostgreSQL on NVMe for a latency-sensitive search workload, and Datadog, which has discussed local-NVMe PostgreSQL instances for workloads that need them. The recurring pattern is local storage for performance, synchronous replication for availability, and independently stored base backups and WAL archives for recovery.
The main takeaway
The talk closes with a diagnostic suggestion: when PostgreSQL appears to stop scaling, inspect the database and storage together. Slow ingestion, unpredictable read latency, vacuum pressure, disruptive checkpoints, and replication lag can be signals that the storage layer has reached a limit.
The bottom line is deliberately narrower than the title: local NVMe can sharply reduce the I/O penalty for storage-bound workloads, but it comes with a different operating model. The architecture outlined in the talk uses quorum-based replication for availability and independently stored base backups plus archived WAL for recovery.
