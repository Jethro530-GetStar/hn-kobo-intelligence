2. Holy shit holy shit holy shit holy shit: The magnitude of what’s now happening
FULL TEXT

Article
I feel like my previous posts (and post titles), and podcasts, and talks, have failed to do full justice to the magnitude of what’s now happening, so I’m trying again.
Last night, we had friends and colleagues over for dinner. We were still trying to process OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s proofs of VP ≠ VNP—hot on the heels, obviously, of the Alpöge/Anthropic proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, which already feels like last week’s news. But then before dessert was served, we saw that OpenAI claims to prove that ω, the exponent of matrix multiplication, is exactly 9/4—a lower bound (!!), matching the upper bound that OpenAI proved in the great Mathocalypse drop of October 6.
There are two separate shocks here. The first shock is that we now, finally, know the right answer to this central mystery of algorithms going back to the late 1960s, when Volker Strassen first showed that ω<3 (he got ω≤log27) … and that the true answer is actually a rational number (!). But the even bigger shock is that the AI was able to prove any lower bound of the form ω>2. I never expected this challenge to be all that much easier than P ≠ NP.
Over the decades, I often heard colleagues express extreme confidence that ω=2, and I expressed skepticism whenever this came up—not because I had any strong argument for ω>2, but just because I saw that there was no strong argument for ω=2 either. So, I’m glad to have been vindicated about that!
But wait, there was more. During dessert, we saw another OpenAI release proving that the true number of linear gates needed to compute the Fourier Transform is N (log N)1/2+o(1). The upper bound here improves, not only over the O(N log N) that stood since the 1960s (i.e., the Fast Fourier Transform or FFT), but also over the O(N log0.9999999999999 N) (give or take some 9’s) that OpenAI published back in the October 6 Mathocalypse. Again, though, the real shock here is that there’s also a matching lower bound (!!). All these questions from the ancient days are now just getting … straight-up answered, rather than slowly illuminated with a drip-drip-drip of clues.
To give an idea of how much things have changed, the other day Yupan Liu put a preprint on the arXiv proving that QIP(2) = PSPACE—that is, quantum interactive protocols with only two messages have the full power of those with three, or any polynomial number. As recently as 2025, this would’ve considered been one of the top complexity results of the year, and the fact that Yupan had used AI to help achieve it would’ve been strange and wonderful. These days, I had to remind myself it had even happened, and the use of AI was so standard that it didn’t even raise an eyebrow.
It’s now obvious that we’ll be getting, not one-off solutions to this or that complexity question, but a whole new continent of mathematical truth, one that we’d previously only glimpsed through fog. Picture van Leeuwenhoek first training a microscope on pondwater, or Galileo first training his telescope on the stars. That might give you some idea of how our resolution into the complexity-theoretic universe, and the whole universe of mathematical truth beyond it, has now improved.
All through last night, as OpenAI and Anthropic uploaded more files to their GitHub repositories—because that’s what they’re doing now, they don’t even bother to issue press releases anymore, they just upload papers—my guests and I sat at the dinner table with phones out and a laptop, wondering which shoe was going to drop next. It was as though the clouds had parted above Mount Sinai, and we were waiting for the next sentence uttered by the Holy One Himself.
I now feel in my bones, for the first time ever, that a proof of P ≠ NP is not far.
For all I care, despise the AI companies for bulldozing our garden. Or love them for opening a new vista, or anything in between. Just please don’t deny what’s happening. Now that the companies have repeatedly shown that they can back up their claims with Lean certificates, don’t be a raging doofus who seizes on some triviality (e.g., a minor discrepancy between the Lean and informal proofs, or unused sections of the Lean proof) to pretend that dozens of great open problems of math and theoretical computer science are not, in fact, getting solved. Because they are getting solved. It might take us years to understand the solutions, but my working assumption by this point is that any proof that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic is correct barring information to the contrary.
I can’t possibly put the matter better than one of my friends on Facebook:
After years of crackpot “solutions” claiming Millennium Problems are solved, now that we have actual solutions, we have crackpot “refutations” claiming Millennium Problems are not solved. It is really poetic, in a way.
Along the way to so many lower bounds that we wondered whether ever we’d live to see, there will also, of course, be further shocking upper bounds: that is, ultimate barriers to lower bounds, also known as “algorithms.” And yes, some of those algorithms will break currently deployed cryptosystems.
Because people have been hounding me about it: yes, I’m virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells in that area. However, until I either see details or hear directly from someone at a lab, I feel much, much less confident about the specific nature of those bombshells. I do not wish to be responsible for spreading unverified rumors. And I worry that exact statements of what was done may have been mangled in a game of Telephone.
I also, crucially, don’t want the cryptography part of the story to overshadow the intellectual enormity of what’s happening. If, in these days of long-prophesied revelation and wonders and terrors, your main worry is your Bitcoin wallet, then go somewhere else for rumors about whether or not ECDSA is broken or whatever. I don’t have what you’re looking for here.
Let me be clear: for those of my readers who celebrate, I believe that tomorrow would be a superb Thanksgiving to spend some quality time with your kids and parents and siblings and old friends.
Right now—this past week—is very much like I would’ve always imagined the beginning of an AI singularity to look and feel like. I just hope we’ll get lucky, and it will “merely” be a singularity for our little corner of the world that involves hard math problems.
At some point at dinner last night, Dana had a sneezing fit. So I said to her, I hope it isn’t a swarm of nanobots that’s come to infect us! But if it is, then I hope it’s the friendly kind, the kind that will usher us all into an AI utopia.
Now, of course, another morning has dawned, and we’re still here. May we find a world where we and our children will be happy to live at the bottom of this rollercoaster. May we work to make it so.
Updates (Nov. 26):
(1) Many commenters thought it was inappropriate and unnecessary of me to warn people to stop being “raging doofuses” who, fingers in their ears and seizing on the flimsiest of pretexts, just flat-out denied the reality of AI solving our longstanding open math problems. Surely no one was doing that?! And then, of course, tons of other people did exactly that. Gary Marcus, to his eternal shame (because he knows better), signal-boosted to his 260,000 Twitter followers Jenny Nielsen’s crackpot claim that the existence of unused sections of Alpöge’s Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis somehow invalidated the proof—while, in the spirit of Tucker Carlson, never quite endorsing the crackpot claim. Meanwhile, in my moderation queue, I have someone angrily accuse me of “sucking off the AI companies,” patronizingly explaining to me that, if I myself say it will take years for experts fully to understand these proofs, then obviously it must take years to verify them as well! The entire concept of Lean verification just sails entirely over his head. So I say one more time: either give evidence of bugs in Lean that would actually matter here, for example by showing how the “con-leche” verifier can accept a proof of 0=1. Or else stop being a raging doofus.
(2) Many other people said some version of, “so computers are solving a bunch of super-hard math problems. Whoop-de-doo. Either explain to me why this matters for my everyday life, or shut up and go away.”
Very well then: there are probably no direct implications of a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis or of VP≠VNP for your everyday life. The vast new ideas that AI introduced to prove such theorems might well have indirect implications, eventually leading to new fields of mathematics with scientific and technological applications, but right now we can only speculate.
In the meantime, however, the fact that AI now exists that’s capable of such things has potentially staggering implications for everyday life, which could be “merely” a transformation of the world’s economy, or could range all the way up (down?) to the destruction of all life on earth in a matter of years in a cataclysmic technological singularity. Opinions differ. But if you want to be part of the serious conversation on such matters, you’ll first need to set aside your “ooga booga, me only care about Riemann proof if it help me make sharper spear” caveman act.
(3) Finally, moving on to better questions, someone asked why I titled a blog post “HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT” only now, and not earlier. Of course, I could’ve used that title at any time since May, if not before! In my mind, though, even after the AI Mathocalypse began in earnest, there remained two interesting hypotheses: (a) that AI was picking off problems “in the convex hull of existing techniques” (like, I dunno, just the friggin’ Riemann Hypothesis) while P≠NP remained serenely untouched and would still take a human conceptual leap, or (b) that AI had really, actually climbing the mountain that leads to P≠NP. Even after VP≠VNP, you could wonder if that was just a bizarre one-off. But with the determination of the matrix multiplication exponent and all the other breakthroughs of the past couple days, option (a) has become much less tenable. Yes, I do now think that AI is climbing the mountain that leads to P≠NP.

Ask HN: Why is pagination gone on "Ask HN", and showing only 19 items? - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50280719 - Oct 2026 (3 comments)
I believe I found the problem. The pool of stories for https://news.ycombinator.com/ask has traditionally been drawn from the ranked stories list from which we generate the frontpage. That list is restricted to the top 1500 ranked submissions, and it seems the % of submissions which are text posts (i.e. no linked URL) had declined enough to starve the Ask HN feed. The solution is to compute a separate list of ranked Ask HNs.

What happened
On November 19, 2026, 1Password began investigating indications that an attacker gained access to an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The actor used the account to access source code and push malicious code to backend services, which downloaded administrative data pertaining to our customers. This data included customer names and email addresses, in addition to non-secret internal metadata about the account. At no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker.
What information was affected
The attacker downloaded account records for a subset of our users. These records include:
- Names and email addresses
- Account and user identifiers, and account creation and last sign-in times
- Public keys (which are public by design, this has no impact to user security)
- Secure Remote Password protocol parameters (which cannot be used to access your account)
- Tokens present in certain emailed links for invitations, provisioning and account recovery
- Other non-secret account metadata relative to our internal architecture (e.g. version numeric identifiers, file numeric identifiers)
The attacker also copied source code from private repositories and gained access to our CI/CD system. Our source code is not customer data, and contains no user secrets. CI/CD contains infrastructure related secrets in order for our product to work. We have triggered a rotation of all credentials which may have been exposed, and there is no impact to 1Password’s services, availability, or product security.
When we send an email to a customer to accept a provisioning invite to an account, or to accept recovery of their account, it contains a token. A provisioning invite is specifically used for our SCIM Bridge or Hosted Provisioning. During this incident there were a small number of users in this state, who have been specifically notified. This token is used to complete the invitation or recovery process, but only when combined with an SSO sign-in or a six digit code sent to the user's registered email address. The token is not able to get access to the account by itself. In order to reduce the ability for a malicious third party to successfully guess the code, we’ve expanded the code from six numeric digits to ten alphanumeric digits. This makes brute force guessing practically impossible.
What was not affected
- Your vaults and the items in them
- The keys that protect your vaults
- 1Password does not store your account password or secret key
- Account recovery keys
- 1Password apps and browser extensions
What we’ve done
- Disabled the affected GitHub account, revoked the stolen token, and removed the access the attacker created.
- Rotated or revoked all internal credentials which may have been exposed.
- Lengthened the codes used in invitation and recovery emails from six digits to ten characters as a precautionary measure.
- Tightened internal GitHub, CI/CD, and endpoint controls, and added new detections for this kind of attack.
- Validated no internal evidence of user account takeovers or compromise using any exposed account provisioning tokens.
- Brought in independent cybersecurity experts to validate our containment approaches and remediation efforts.
What you can do
- Be cautious about phishing attempts in the form of unexpected emails, texts or calls that mention 1Password, invitations, or account recovery attempts.
- 1Password will never contact you to ask for your account password or Secret Key. Only enter them in the official 1Password app, or on our official website, and only when you’ve opened the app or typed the address yourself. Your Secret Key is only needed the first time you sign in on a new device or browser.
- Keep your Secret Key and account password safe.
- If you see something suspicious, tell us at support@1password.com.
Our commitment to you
You trust us with some of the most sensitive information you have. 1Password is designed so that a breach of our systems can’t expose your vault contents. Your vault secrets are encrypted with keys that only you hold, so we can’t access them, and neither can an attacker who gets into our servers.
We are committed to transparency in the interests of our customers' security and the security of the broader industry, which is why we have published and maintained our Security Whitepaper since 2015. We’ll keep this blog post current, and we’ll update it if we find anything that changes what we’ve said here.

Article
Shows running iOS simulators in the Finder sidebar so you can drag files in and out of them.
SimDrop ← Finder sidebar › Locations
└── iPhone Duo/ ← one folder per *booted* simulator
├── Photos and Videos/ ← simulator Photos library
└── Downloads/ ← Files app › On My iPhone › Downloads
Simulators appear when they boot and disappear when they shut down. Everything is also reachable from the terminal:
cp config.json ~/Library/CloudStorage/SimDrop-SimDrop/iPhone\ Duo/Downloads/brew install --cask rxliuli/tap/sim-drop
Or download SimDrop-macos.dmg from
GitHub Releases. Requires macOS 15 or
later. It is Developer ID signed and notarized. SimDrop isn't on the Mac App Store and
can't be: the app and its helper must run unsandboxed to drive simctl and the
simulators' folders.
Open SimDrop once. The window is only a settings page showing whether these two one-time approvals are done:
- Background Service: allow SimDrop in System Settings › General › Login Items.
- Finder Extension: click Enable in the Finder banner (or turn on SimDrop under File Providers in System Settings).
After that it works silently in the background, including after a restart. The window can be closed.
After installing or upgrading, open SimDrop once. Replacing the app makes macOS
drop its Finder extension (Finder then says the application can't be found) and stop the
background helper. launchd refuses to start a helper whose recorded launch constraint
came from the old copy (needs LWCR update, exit 78). Until the new version has been
launched, no part of SimDrop can run to repair this. A Homebrew postflight_steps can't
launch it either, because open fails inside the cask step sandbox. So the cask shows a
caveat instead. Opening the app re-registers the extension and makes sure the helper
answers with this build's version. If needed, it boots the stale launchd job out and
registers the helper again.
Finder moves files when you drag within the same volume and copies only across
volumes. File Provider locations live in ~/Library/CloudStorage on your startup
disk, so dragging a file from the Desktop into SimDrop moves it. iCloud Drive
and Google Drive behave the same way, and File Provider has no API to change this.
- Hold ⌥ Option while dragging to copy instead.
- The other direction moves too: dragging a file out of a simulator's Downloads removes it from the simulator. Photos are read-only, so dragging them out always copies.
Making SimDrop copy by default like a USB drive would require it to be a separate volume (e.g. an FSKit file system), which would be a rewrite.
- In icon view, photos show real previews without downloading them first. The extension provides downscaled thumbnails.
- Dropped images and videos are imported with simctl addmedia, so they show up
in the Photos app. iOS renames them (e.g. IMG_0007.JPG), and Finder updates
the name to match.
- Finder can't reject drops by file type, so anything that isn't an image or video is put in the same simulator's Downloads folder instead. Finder moves it there too.
- When that happens SimDrop posts a notification saying where the file went. (Known issue: clicking the notification doesn't open Finder yet.)
- The list mirrors the Photos library: it is read from Photos.sqlite, so photos
deleted in the simulator (now in "Recently Deleted") disappear from Finder too.
- Deleting a photo in Finder moves it to the simulator's "Recently Deleted" (recoverable there for 30 days), with no confirmation dialog. If the Photos app is open in the simulator, SimDrop relaunches it so the change shows.
- Supports adding, opening, replacing and deleting files and folders.
- Deleting goes to the Mac's Trash, with no confirmation dialog. The file is removed from the simulator and lands in your Trash. Put Back restores it into the simulator.
- Renaming and moving items inside Downloads isn't supported yet, because item identifiers are path-based.
- Changes made inside the simulator appear in Finder within a few seconds.
- iOS simulators only. Real devices aren't supported: iOS doesn't expose the Files app storage over USB, and photos written to a real device's DCIM folder are not imported into its Photos library.
| Target | Sandbox | Role |
|---|---|---|
| SimDrop (app) | no | Settings window. Registers the helper ( SMAppService.agent) and the single File Provider domain. |
| SimDropHelper (launchd agent) | no | Starts at login. Watches ~/Library/Developer/CoreSimulator/Devices with FSEvents and tells Finder to refresh (signalEnumerator). Over XPC it runs xcrun simctl addmedia and moves deleted Downloads files to the Mac's Trash. It must be a plain tool in SimDrop.app/Contents/MacOS. As a separate .app bundle, signalEnumerator fails with "The application cannot be used right now". |
| SimDropFileProvider (extension) | yes | NSFileProviderReplicatedExtension. Reads and writes simulator folders directly through a temporary-exception entitlement, and asks the helper to import photos and to trash files. |
| Shared/ | — | Simulator discovery from device.plist (state == 3 means booted), plus path resolution. |
Paths inside a simulator (~/Library/Developer/CoreSimulator/Devices/<UDID>/):
- Downloads: data/Containers/Shared/AppGroup/<UUID>/File Provider Storage/Downloads,
where the group's metadata plist has MCMMetadataIdentifier = group.com.apple.FileProvider.LocalStorage.
- Photos: data/Media/DCIM/1xxAPPLE/. Library state comes from data/Media/PhotoData/Photos.sqlite (ZASSET, where ZTRASHEDSTATE = 0).
Implementation notes:
- Change sync. The helper calls signalEnumerator(.workingSet). The working-set
enumerator diffs against the last snapshot it handed out (stored in the
extension's container) and reports removed items with didDeleteItems.
Leaving an item out of the working set does not remove it from Finder.
- Deleting photos. The helper edits Photos.sqlite directly. It updates the
same ZASSET columns an in-app delete changes (ZTRASHEDSTATE, ZTRASHEDREASON, ZTRASHEDDATE, ZMODIFICATIONDATE, Z_OPT), found by diffing the table before
and after deleting in the Photos app. The tables have Core Data triggers calling NSCoreDataTriggerUpdateAffectedObjectValue, which only exists inside the Photos
daemon, so the helper registers a no-op function with that name. The Photos app
caches the library and only shows the change after a relaunch. This is unofficial:
a future iOS schema change may break it, and the helper then reports an error
instead of guessing.
- Thumbnails. Photos get previews through NSFileProviderThumbnailing. Finder
only asks File Providers for thumbnails of media types, never for .app, .ipa or .zip (an app-install feature was tried and removed for now). Thumbnails are
cached per content version, so bump Item.contentSchema after changing them.
- Re-imports. After the domain reconnects (e.g. following an upgrade), the system
may call createItem for folders it already had, such as a simulator folder or its
Downloads / Photos and Videos. These are adopted under their real identifiers. A
rejected create stays pending forever, and the system ignores deletions of a pending
item, so a shut-down simulator would never disappear.
- Folder types. Directories are always reported as public.folder, including
bundles like .app. For uploads, the returned type must match the template, or
the system keeps retrying the create and never uploads the children.
- Item versions. Item.metadataSchema is part of every metadata version. Bump
it when capabilities change, or Finder keeps the cached ones (e.g. photos showing
as "locked").
- Trash. The domain keeps the default supportsSyncingTrash = YES, so Finder
reparents trashed items to .trashContainer. modifyItem moves the real file
to the Mac's Trash through the helper, and the next sync reports the item as
deleted. The extension must serve .trashContainer (an empty folder).
Otherwise the domain's .Trash becomes a stale handle and Finder fails with -36.
CI (.github/workflows/release.yml) runs when MARKETING_VERSION in project.yml
changes on main. It generates the project with XcodeGen, then uses
rxliuli/apple-release/dmg to archive,
sign with Developer ID, build the DMG, notarize, staple and publish a GitHub Release.
The release-macOS environment needs these secrets: APPLE_CERTIFICATE_DEVELOPERID_BASE64,
APPLE_CERTIFICATE_DEVELOPERID_PASSWORD, APPLE_API_KEY, APPLE_API_KEY_ID,
APPLE_API_ISSUER, and optionally TAP_GITHUB_TOKEN. When it is set, the release also
bumps Casks/sim-drop.rb in rxliuli/homebrew-tap.
No provisioning profile is involved. The app group is Team-ID-prefixed
(N2X78TUUFG.com.rxliuli.sim-drop, not group.…), which macOS accepts without one.
Requires XcodeGen.
xcodegen generate
xcodebuild -project SimDrop.xcodeproj -scheme SimDrop -derivedDataPath build \
-allowProvisioningUpdates build
# restart the extension and helper so they pick up the new build
pkill -x SimDropFileProvider; launchctl kickstart -k gui/$(id -u)/com.rxliuli.sim-drop.helper
open build/Build/Products/Debug/SimDrop.app
App icon: the source is Design/icon-source.png (a full-bleed 1024 squircle with
transparent corners). Regenerate the icon set with
swift Design/make-icon.swift Design/icon-source.png SimDrop/Assets.xcassets/AppIcon.appiconset.
The script scales the artwork to the 824pt macOS icon grid and adds a drop shadow.
Dev launch flags:
- open SimDrop.app --args -reregisterHelper YES re-registers the launchd agent
after its plist changes.
- open SimDrop.app --args -resetDomain YES removes the domain with all local
state and re-adds it.
Don't remove the helper's launchd job on a plain uninstall (e.g. uninstall launchctl:
in the cask). brew upgrade runs the old version's uninstall step before installing the
new one, so the job disappears while the registration still says "enabled". The cask
only does this on --zap.
Switching between a dev build and an installed release: only one copy should be
registered. launchd records a launch constraint (LWCR) for the helper with the signing
identity of whichever copy registered it. If that copy differs from the one now on disk
(e.g. Apple Development vs Developer ID), launchd refuses to start the helper
(last exit reason = OS_REASON_CODESIGNING). Quit the other copy, then run
launchctl bootout gui/$(id -u)/com.rxliuli.sim-drop.helper and launch the copy you
want with --args -reregisterHelper YES. Duplicate extensions (pluginkit -m -v -D -i com.rxliuli.sim-drop.FileProvider) also hide SimDrop from System Settings › Login
Items & Extensions.

9. CTO of 'RansomwareHelp' Indicted for Wire Fraud

Article
The chief technology officer and co-founder of ransomwarehelp.com, a six year old Florida business that claimed to use “advanced cryptographic tools” to restore data locked by ransomware, has been charged with wire fraud after an FBI sting operation found the company was simply paying the attackers for a decryption key and charging victims a markup for work that was never performed.
The website RansomwareHelp urged victims “Do not pay the ransom!,” claiming its advanced cryptographic tools could restore 100 percent of data, servers, and systems to their original format, and that there was no need to give in to extortion demands.
“Our cryptography experts use proprietary tools and algorithms to achieve 100% data recovery, all backed by a success rate of over 99%,” the website enthused, claiming the company had over 28 years of experience and a team of 80 experts in cryptography, cybersecurity, forensic analysis and reverse engineering. “We never promote, intermediate or pay ransom to threat actors on behalf of our clients.”
But according to an indictment (PDF) returned this week in Rhode Island, the company’s co-founder — 52-year-old Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar of Miami and Medellin, Colombia — routinely paid ransomware groups for a decryption key to unlock victim data, and then charged those victims well above what he’d secretly paid the threat actors [Palacio’s indictment was reported earlier this week by 404 Media, but for some reason that story did not name the defendant’s company].
The indictment says the FBI set up a sting operation in which the feds were posing both as a ransomware victim company and as a fictitious ransomware group. Pretending to be victims of a cyberattack, investigators contacted RansomwareHelp and supplied the company with a copy of a phony ransomware note, saying they did not wish to pay a ransom. RansomwareHelp replied by reiterating its policy against negotiating with or making ransom payments to threat actors.
The government said Palacio soon after contacted the fake ransomware group run by the FBI and requested the lowest possible price for the decryptor. FBI investigators responded with a price of .2 bitcoin, or around $13,000 at the time. Less than 48 hours later, Palacio allegedly told agents acting as the victim company that the diagnostic assessment was successful and that the cost of decrypting the files would be $13,260.
The following day, the government alleges, Palacio messaged the fictitious ransomware group operated by the feds and asked to reduce the ransom demand to $4,000 for the purchase of a decryption tool. The indictment states that the agreed upon price was around $6,000 in bitcoin, and that Palacio charged the FBI’s fake victim firm $16,010, including an initial $2,750 diagnostic fee and the full fee of $13,260.
Paul Ihme is co-founder of the security firm Soteria. Ihme told KrebsOnSecurity that in 2024 he heard from a small accounting firm that had paid RansomwareHelp a diagnostic fee to help recover from a Lockbit ransomware infection that had locked all of their company and client data.
Ihme said RansomwareHelp sent a proposal to the victim with a recovery price of $438,880, which it claimed included “professional configuration of forensic servers,” “expert engineering hours of programming to configure decryption algorithms,” and “eight (6-hour processing cycles) related to the decryption process.”
The proposal stated that the recovery process involved reverse engineering the ransomware encryption using “510 preconfigured forensic servers of the maximum quantitative capacity located in different parts of the world.”
“Our programmers will inject randomly the encryption codes extracted during the forensic and cryptographic analysis using brute force processes,” the proposal claimed.
Ihme said RansomwareHelp insisted that they be allowed unrestricted remote access into the victim’s network to run their diagnostic checks and analyses, using an Anydesk remote desktop software installer. Ihme believes RansomwareHelp insisted on that remote access in part so that the company could delete all copies of the ransomware notes left by the attackers on the victim’s network.
A typical ransomware note is a plain text file that introduces the ransomware group and lays out their demands, and it includes a way to contact them — often an email address or a live chat session on a darknet website. In most cases, the attackers will leave a copy of the ransom note on every system on the victim network that gets locked.
But Ihme said a review of the company’s network at the time found only one copy of the ransom note, which was unearthed from a backup snapshot on one of the infected computers. When they connected to the Lockbit chat server with the provided user ID, they discovered that someone had already been negotiating with the Lockbit extortionists for several days.
“The charitable view would be to say that they were using Anydesk so they could grab the stuff they needed without having to walk the client through downloading and uploading a bunch of stuff,” Ihme said. “A legitimate firm would say, ‘Hey, send us a copy of the ransom note,’ which will have a link or URL to go to and chat with the threat actors. What they were doing is using that access to delete ransomware notes off the systems. The only reason they would do that is to prevent the victim from jumping into that chat itself.”
Indeed, RansomwareHelp pleaded with the Lockbit group throughout the chat not to reveal to the victim that they were negotiating. However, Lockbit quickly grew annoyed with the length of the negotiations, replying that the victims had waited too long and that the ransom demand would increase by $100,000 for every day they dithered further.
In the end, the accounting firm took Ihme’s advice and went with a more established security firm that helped them negotiate a payment to Lockbit.
“The victim told us, ‘If we don’t get this data back, our business is over’,” Ihme recalled. “They ended up paying half of what RansomwareHelp was going to charge them.”
The chats show RansomwareHelp kept pestering Lockbit to negotiate a deal even after it was clear a new negotiator was engaging with the ransomware group on the victim’s behalf.
“I tell you something, that person who is negotiating with you for the other chat is damaging the whole process,” RansomwareHelp wrote to Lockbit. “Please do not change the price. I am doing my best to achieve it.”
Ihme said RansomwareHelp primarily targeted small to medium sized businesses that weren’t particularly tech-savvy, noting that many of the technical claims made on the company’s website and in their proposal are patently farcical. For example, the proposal sent to the accounting firm said it could break six types of encryption, and then proceeded to list a number of irrelevant password hashing algorithms like SHA-256.
“If it were really true these guys could decrypt any ransomware out there, this guy would be a billionaire,” Ihme said. “They were going after people who don’t have any technical knowledge, are desperate, and don’t have anyone advising them about how any of this stuff works.”
The case against Palacio marks the latest in a string of recent prosecutions by the U.S. Department of Justice (DoJ) targeting security firms alleged to have conspired with fraudsters to defraud clients. In early October, the government charged 54-year-old Edward Dubrovsky, an executive at the Canadian cybersecurity firm Cybersteward. The government has sealed the case against Dubrovsky, but he is currently facing cyber extortion and conspiracy charges.
Around the same time as Dubrovsky’s arrest, the DoJ charged MonsterCloud CEO Zohar Pinhasi, 50, with wire fraud and conspiracy for allegedly charging customers to remediate ransomware attacks without paying the hackers. According to charging documents, Pinhasi secretly went behind his customers’ backs and paid hackers anyway, paying ransomware groups $8 million while charging customers $19 million.
In July, Angelo Martino, a 41-year-old ransomware negotiator from Land O’Lakes, Florida, was sentenced to 70 months in prison for conspiring with the Blackcat/ALPHV ransomware group to extort multiple victims. According to the DoJ, Martino was paid by BlackCat attackers to provide confidential information about the negotiating position and strategy of his employer’s clients and enable the ransomware actors to maximize the ransoms paid by the victims.
