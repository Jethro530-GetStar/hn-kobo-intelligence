Warning
recalc is in early beta. It's very, very early in development, so expect bugs, rough edges, and changes between versions that break things. Keep copies of anything you can't afford to lose, and please tell us what breaks.
Spreadsheets are still a great way to organize and analyze data. While it would be possible to just let Claude Code or Codex take control of Excel or Google Sheets, this felt like an opportunity to reimagine the spreadsheet for the agentic AI age from the ground up (though most of it should still feel familiar). This is still very experimental and very much a work in progress.
In its current form, recalc gives you and your agents one shared grid. Every cell can hold a number, a formula, a terminal command, or an AI agent. Agents write their plans and results into cells while you watch. Commands in other cells check their work and turn red when something's wrong. When you want something changed, you edit a cell instead of interrupting the chat. The different Claude Code or Codex instances running in recalc understand how recalc works via an MCP server.
Check an agent's "done". Put a cell that runs your tests next to the cell where the agent says it's finished. The test cell re-runs by itself whenever the code changes. If the agent says done while a test is failing, you see it straight away. You can even stop a Claude Code session from finishing until its row goes green.
Ask a hundred documents the same questions. Put files down the side and questions across the top, then press Run. An agent answers every cell, and each answer links to the exact passage it came from. Click a cell to see the quote highlighted in the document. Mark the answers you've checked. Agents can't overwrite them after that. Export the result to Excel when you're done, or to share it.
Run a few agents at once without losing track. Give each agent its own row and watch them all on one screen. Anything that needs you (a question, an error, a finished answer) lands in one list called Needs You. Or let the Driver, an agent that works the board for you, set things up from a prompt.
Keep your work on your computer. There's no account, no server and no tracking. The only request recalc makes on its own is a once-a-day check for a new version, which you can turn off. A board is just a folder of files. The agents you connect send what they always send to their own providers. For private documents, you can run the agents on a local model.

As has been widely reported, Tesla is about to end Cybertruck production. The Cybertruck has been a shocking failure. That’s pretty wild. A net project loss of around $2 billion. Now, I have to admit that I haven’t fact checked the numbers here, but what we know for certain is that Tesla planned to produce a lot of Cybertrucks per year and ended up producing a small fraction of that target. In fact, the annual sales target for the Cybertruck was higher than 125,000 — it was 250,000, with a goal of getting up to 500,000!
The Cybertruck was hyped up as having more reservations than any vehicle in history and was supposedly going to shatter records and transform the market. I warned people on many occasions that the reservation total was not a real reservation total and was definitely not a reliable indicator of demand because a) a reservation only required a $100 refundable deposit, and b) a ton of people made reservations after Elon Musk said that you could lock in the price of lifetime Full Self Driving when making a reservation (so, you’d only have to pay $6,000 for FSD no matter how much the cost of it went up in the following years). Anyway, the Cybertruck had other issues as well when it finally arrived. It cost about twice as much as it was supposed to cost and it had about half as much range as it was supposed to have. In other words, it was not even close to the vehicle it was supposed to be — its range per dollar cost was about ¼ what it was supposed to be.
The Cybertruck was supposed to be the next big thing after the Tesla Model Y. It was supposed to disrupt the gigantic US pickup truck market. The Model S was a superb and widely acclaimed car, if a bit more expensive than the mass market could afford. The Model S did in fact compete superbly well with the leaders in its segment, the BMW 7 Series and Mercedes S-Class. The Tesla Model X brought a similar offering in a larger package with some fun extra elements (falcon-wing doors, self-presenting doors, a third row). The Tesla Model 3 then completely shook the automotive world as a tremendous market phenomenon and perhaps the biggest product launch in history (in terms of total revenue). The Tesla Model Y was basically the Model 3 in a more popular crossover package and eventually became a much higher selling vehicle. In fact, it became the highest selling vehicle model of any kind in multiple years. Then JB Straubel stepped down as CTO.
Due to their EV passions, JB Straubel and Elon Musk were introduced to each other and had lunch together to talk about electric airplanes (Elon’s dream) and then electric cars (because JB talked more sense into Elon). Then the two of them were introduced to Marc Tarpenning and Martin Eberhard, who had founded Tesla Motors and just barely got started on the idea. After a while, Tarpenning and Eberhard left the company, and Musk and Straubel truly led its development and growth into an automotive superpower (as briefly described above). Since JB Straubel stepped down from his role as CTO of Tesla, however, if one thinks about it, it’s hard to identify any successful product launch from the company or much significant improvement at all.
Straubel left his CTO position in July 2019. The Model Y was unveiled in March 2019 and was about ⅔ the same as the Model 3. It would seem that getting the Model Y designed and prepared for launch was the last big project Straubel worked on. (Note that the Semi was unveiled in November 2017 but was long delayed due to battery supply, according to Elon Musk.) Since the Model Y, what significant product launch have we seen from Tesla? There was the Cybertruck, as we’ve already discussed. That was unveiled in November 2019. When you think about the timing now, did Straubel disagree strongly with Musk on the Cybertruck and decide it was time to move on when Musk pushed forward with his idea for an electric truck? It took another 4+ years for Tesla to start delivering the Cybertruck, in contrast to Model Y deliveries starting in January 2020, less than a year after the model was unveiled. More recently, the Cybercab has been developed, unveiled, and is now in production. A consumer car smaller and cheaper than the Model 3 was never developed and unveiled — just a stripped down, decontented Model 3 — and there basically haven’t been any other product unveilings since Straubel left his role as CTO. Elon Musk has published a couple of somewhat scattered, rambling “master plans,” but we haven’t gotten any significant new products aside from the Cybertruck and the just arriving Cybercab.
Even looking over on the energy storage side of things, the Tesla Megapack was launched in July 2019, the month Straubel stepped down from his role as CTO. Did Straubel just decide he had done enough once he had finished the Megapack launch and gotten the Model Y essentially ready for market? Did he have conflicts with Musk on where to take the company from there?
Yes, there’s been incremental product development and refreshed versions of Tesla cars and energy storage systems. There are still plenty of engineers working on improving things. However, there doesn’t seem to be the same leadership in new product development, and there also isn’t the same practical, moderating element that Straubel seemingly provided to Musk’s more wild, grandiose, sci-fi inspired dreams and ideas.
Perhaps this is all misunderstanding the roles of the two men to some extent. Perhaps things would not be much different if Straubel had stayed in the CTO role and had the same influence he had before leaving. He is still on the board of directors. (What that actually means is unclear, though.) Perhaps Tesla’s success and business would still be primarily based on the Model 3 and Model Y, without new transformative products. Or perhaps we would have had a more normal pickup truck from Tesla, an electric van, better batteries, a better market rollout of the Semi, and other products. Perhaps the Cybertruck, an epic product failure, never would have been shown to the product and hyped up unrealistically with specs and figures disconnected from what was going to be possible. Perhaps we’d have a Model C (affordable, compact Tesla) rather than a Cybercab. Perhaps Tesla would be a very different company.
Who knows? We don’t know the ins and outs of JB Straubel and Elon Musk’s relationship, or how much Straubel actually led Tesla in his CTO role. We don’t know how much Straubel tampered down Musk’s worst instincts or guided product development. We still really don’t even know why Straubel left. According to Tesla, it was so that he could focus more time on Redwood Materials, but why wasn’t that business within the umbrella of Tesla in the first place? Vertical integration has long been a key element of the company. Who knows? There’s a lot we don’t know and never will. However, what we do know is that since JB Straubel stepped down from his role as CTO of Tesla, we haven’t seen a significant, successful new product launch at the company. We’ll have to wait a bit longer to see if the Cybercab is one, and whether that sets a new, positive course for the future of the company or not.

Libghostty 1.0
libghostty-vt 1.0 is here. libghostty is a
portable, high-performance C library for terminal emulation, extracted
directly from the core of Ghostty. The 1.0 release means both the library
itself and the C ABI are now stable.
A little over a year ago, I wrote that libghostty is coming. At the time, the Zig module had just been merged, the C API didn't exist, and nobody but Ghostty was a consumer. Today, the C API is a complete terminal emulator, it runs in production for millions of people across hundreds of products, both free and commercial, and it has a real, enforced ABI stability policy.
This post is about how we got here and what 1.0 actually means.1
Who Is Already Using It
Since starting the Ghostty project in 2022, the most common question I've been asked is "why?". I already answered this in the libghostty is coming post, so feel free to read that if you wish. Today, I'd rather answer this by showing the broad industry impact libghostty has already had. I believe this strongly validates its existence.
A year ago, I used a bug in JediTerm, the terminal emulator in JetBrains IDEs, as an example of why terminal emulation is hard to get right. A year later, the terminal in JetBrains IDEs now runs on libghostty. Every IntelliJ, PyCharm, GoLand, etc. user now has a libghostty-powered terminal. Based on a report by JetBrains, that's over 12.5 million active users.
Neovim has an approved pull request
replacing libvterm with libghostty-vt. Among other things, it closes a
10-year-old issue
about reflowing scrollback on resize. The maintainers are waiting on a
tagged release before merging so that distributions can package the
dependency separately, and the release today should enable that.
KDE developers are experimenting with a Konsole replacement built on it. Vercel's Turborepo uses it to render task output in its TUI. devenv uses it to drive shells. Antithesis used it to fuzz TUI programs and found real bugs in btop and others. Ghostel brings it to Emacs. ghostty-web is the WASM build with an Xterm.js-compatible API. Commercial products like OrbStack embed it. And a whole wave of new terminals and "agentic" coding tools, such as herdr and cmux, have been built on it rather than writing yet another emulator.
There are community bindings for Rust, Go, JavaScript, Swift, Python, .NET, Elixir, Dart, Java, and more, and the awesome-libghostty list tracks well over a hundred projects.
More people today use a terminal powered by libghostty than use Ghostty the application, by a very large multiple. And libghostty is having a much larger impact than being "just" a terminal emulator.2
ABI Stability
1.0 means libghostty-vt follows semantic versioning and has strict
ABI compatibility
promises so that packagers across distributions and operating systems
can be confident packaging libghostty-vt and program developers can be
confident dynamically linking against it.
Beyond providing useful features, I believe the hallmark of a high-quality and enduring C library is ABI stability. So from the start, I took inspiration from C libraries I enjoyed working with and designed the API to have a strong, stable ABI that allows for future additions.
Every object is an opaque handle. The hundreds of values you might read or
write go through a small set of enum-keyed getters and setters, so we
can add new values easily without introducing a new symbol. Programs with
newer headers linked against an older libghostty get GHOSTTY_UNSUPPORTED
results instead of crashes or undefined behavior. The few non-opaque structs
we have carry their size so that fields can be appended later, or contain
reserved padding. Enums are pinned to int on every compiler, as much as
the C standard allows.
Our CI enforces our ABI policy. Every change is checked with libabigail against a committed baseline on Linux, and against golden type manifests on macOS and WebAssembly.
On ELF platforms, every exported symbol is also versioned, so the dynamic loader refuses to load a mismatched library by name rather than failing in some mysterious way later.
Packaging
In addition to ABI stability, the 1.0 release brings along the proper
build tooling, tarballs, dependency licensing manifests, documentation,
and more to enable every major operating system and distribution to
package libghostty-vt through standard official tooling.
Libghostty has its own release cycle, separate from Ghostty the application. And it has its own tarballs with only the files and dependencies libghostty needs, resulting in smaller sizes and simpler licensing verification and compatibility.
Each release publishes a signed source tarball along with a companion
dependencies tarball containing every Zig package in Zig's own cache
layout, so a distribution can build the library completely offline. The
build installs the shared and static libraries with a proper SONAME,
headers, pkg-config files, and a CMake package config, and it honors
--prefix, DESTDIR, and lib64. Debug info is kept by default so
distros can split it out. All of this is documented in
PACKAGING-LIBGHOSTTY.md,
which ships with the tarball.
Dependencies and Licensing
libghostty-vt is MIT licensed. At runtime it needs nothing beyond
libc, and it can be built fully freestanding if you don't want that
either.3
The library compiles in a small number of permissively licensed dependencies: simdutf and Google Highway for SIMD, a compact Unicode table for grapheme data, and Wuffs for image decoding in the Kitty graphics protocol. Both simdutf and Highway are built without libc++, so there is no C++ standard library dependency.
There is no copyleft code anywhere in the dependency tree. Every dependency is listed in a machine-readable manifest with its version, SPDX license, and hash, specifically so distros can fill in their bundled-library metadata more easily.
If a distro would rather dynamically link the system simdutf or Highway, there's a build flag for that.
Portability
libghostty-vt is highly portable. The table below lists all the platforms
and architectures that we build and test (full unit tests plus running all
examples) in CI:
| Platform | Architectures |
|---|---|
| Linux (glibc, musl) | x86_64, aarch64 |
| macOS 13+ | x86_64, aarch64 |
| iOS 13+ | aarch64 |
| FreeBSD | x86_64, aarch64 |
| Windows | x86_64 |
| Android | aarch64, x86_64, arm |
| WebAssembly | wasm32 (freestanding) |
There is a second set of platforms we build in CI but do not run tests for. These systems all have known users; we just haven't set up the machinery for automated testing:
| Platform | Architectures |
|---|---|
| Bare metal | riscv32, thumb |
| WebAssembly (WASI) | wasm32 |
| Windows | arm64 |
| illumos | x86_64 |
| 32-bit x86 | x86 |
Each of the tested platforms gets the artifacts it expects:
- Linux and FreeBSD get a shared library with a proper SONAME and versioned symbols, a static library, headers, pkg-config files, and a CMake package config.
- macOS and iOS get a dylib with an @rpath install name and
compatibility version, plus an XCFramework covering macOS, iOS, and
the iOS simulator that drops straight into Xcode or Swift Package
Manager.
- Windows gets a DLL with a .pdb, an import library, and a static
library. The library is built with the GNU ABI, but MSVC programs
link against it through the import library and CI verifies this.
- Android gets a shared library built against the NDK with 16 KB page size support, as required by Android 15+.
- WebAssembly ships in two flavors, one optimized for speed and one
for size, and the small one is under a megabyte with every feature
enabled. If you only need a read-only viewer, subsystems like input
encoding, search, selection, and Kitty graphics can be compiled out
with a build flag, which roughly halves the module. Every function
stays exported and simply returns GHOSTTY_UNSUPPORTED so your code
doesn't have to change.4
Portability isn't just about the ability to build and run. It's also about resource usage and being able to work in a wide variety of resource environments.
For resource usage, the library creates no threads, allocates only through
an allocator you can customize, and uses minimal compute and memory.
I've run 10,000 active terminals, each with a live shell producing output,
on a Linux server with 4 cores and 8 GB of RAM. In this test, I had to use
dash because bash used far more RAM than libghostty-vt and wouldn't fit
into the 8 GB budget.
What's Next
Libghostty 1.0 is a major milestone.
You can now create a terminal in ~1,500 lines of C that has more features and better performance than just about any terminal that existed in 2025, and most today in 2026.5 And thanks to the rich ecosystem of language bindings, you can do this in every major programming language. You get this for free. For almost no effort. You can just do it.
libghostty-vt is a generalized library, so we will focus on stability
while implementing as many terminal protocols as exist
(giving options to enable/disable them, of course) so that embedders
have the luxury of choice.
Every bug any libghostty-vt embedder reports makes everyone else in
the ecosystem better. Every feature we ship in libghostty-vt becomes
available to everyone quickly and safely. Terminals can move faster with
less fragmentation.
Thank you to everyone who contributed to the library, wrote bindings, filed bugs, and shipped something on top of it while the API was still experimental. As with all open source, this was the work of a large community.
Docs, examples, and downloads are at lib.ghostty.org.
