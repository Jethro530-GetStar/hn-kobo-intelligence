Things to know if you get cancer (2026 Edition)
I was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma in my mouth in May 2026. Here are some things I learned based on that experience so far.
- You need to be doggedly persistent on scheduling things. Yes this means lots of phone calls. If you get an appointment a few weeks out, call back a few days later and ask for something sooner and see if they have any cancellations. I could only get an appointment 4 weeks out with an ENT (this was before I knew anything was wrong). My wife made me call back and I was able to get in that week due to a cancellation.
- AIs may downplay the seriousness of things. I was chatting with various models and most pushed me in the direction of “you don’t have cancer” for a while. Just something to note.
- You may need to push for comprehensive genetic testing. Some doctors were weird about this and didn’t order it promptly, even after telling me they would. My surgeon, who told me he’d get it done, eventually referred me out to an oncologist. This caused a multi-month delay to getting genetic results, since the first results were done on the surgery tumor, not the biopsy tumor. No one cares more about your case than you, so take control and send lots of follow-ups.
- You can find the “Standard of Care” flow charts for every cancer type. Look and get through this yourself to see what the options are based on how things have progressed. NCCN guidelines. You may need to make an account, which you can do as a non-professional. I like the PDF files. They also have an interactive navigator. Use AI to help you figure out what to pick based on your case.
- If surgery is recommended, I would try and talk to someone who has undergone the surgery. I did not do this and regret it. Ask them how intense things were, what it felt like, etc. My surgeon talked me through my surgery, but it was a lot rougher on me physically than I expected. Ask the surgeon how many of these surgeries they do a year and what the complication rate is.
- Get a second opinion as soon as possible, no matter how much you like your plan. This may require a lot of calling and getting on an airplane, but I’d move to schedule it at a top cancer center as soon as you are relatively sure of your diagnosis. Ask for a second read of the pathology at the center where you get the second opinion.
- I started a new folder on my Mac, and after appointments I’d dictate what happened and what the doctor said and have the AI make a new document summarizing things and asking its own follow-up questions for next time. Nice to be able to refer to this over time.
- Check out all the resources and materials at the Sijbrandij Foundation. Sid (GitLab cofounder) took matters into his own hands when standard of care ran out. Schedule a call with these guys. They are great and have good materials and questions to ask your doctors.
Note, since this is on the front page of Hacker News right now. exhilaration suggested that if you are within a 2-3 hour drive of a major cancer center, go there instead of your local community hospital. Use the US News cancer hospital rankings. The top centers usually take your insurance, so use vacation days and book a hotel if you have to. The largest benefit I saw was speed. At MD Anderson I got a question answered within 20 minutes of a visit, because the doctor called the pathologist who he was on a first name basis with. If that wasn’t at a cancer center, it would have taken days, or more likely the question never would have been answered at all (I had asked my oncologist about this and he’d just shrugged). You can find today’s release in https://rsync.libreboot.org/stable/26.10/ or you can use one of the mirrors.
Libreboot is free, open source boot firmware replacing proprietary BIOS/UEFI firmware on supported Intel/AMD and ARM64 hardware. It initialises the hardware inside your computer such as the CPU or memory controller, configuring the machine in such a way as to enable use of an operating system like Linux or BSD at boot - and now Microsoft Windows is also compatible because Libreboot has added full UEFI support via MrChromebox EDK2 payload. Libreboot offers greater security and customisation compared to proprietary firmware; its goal, plainly, is to rid the world of proprietary software and to do that, we therefore try to provide firmware that is extremely easy for non-technical users to understand.
Today’s release changelog is relative to the previous Libreboot 26.01 release.
This is a very special release, with several major design changes made regardless of hardware changes, so users should read this changelog very carefully - and read it twice.
First thing’s first. (and yes, you read the previous paragraph correctly)
A strongly anti-AI policy was added to a new file: AGENTS.md in lbmk. This is the most important change in the release. Now everything else:
WARNING: MAJOR design changes were made to this release, in ways that may affect lots of existing setups. We now use EDK2 as the default payload, with GRUB/SeaBIOS provided in flash as EFI builds for backward compatibility. Everything works, but there are some caveats so please read this release changelog very carefully.

The full list of boards is quite long, so it’s shown at the end of this article. All 233 of them!
MrChromebox supports nearly every Chromebook, and this release imports all boards from that project. This includes everything up to the most recent Intel and AMD hardware.
Imported all 233 currently supported Chromebooks from the MrChromebox coreboot distro. Baytrail (Kip) tested - others need testing. Source-only in this release (must build from source, no binaries provided).
This was done with a utility I wrote at util/mrimport/ which generates configs for lbmk, including revisions and submodules. I made further tweaks manually; further updates upstream can be synced in Libreboot by diffing again the output of mrimport between revisions, adapted into Libreboot irrespective of the latter’s modifications which are numerous - and will be covered next.

Basically, these boards actually work for the first time.
EDK2 is Intel’s reference implementation of the UEFI specification, for booting. It’s typically used by vendors, in proprietary forks of Intel’s BSD-licensed code, but Intel’s reference code is Free Software. We use their reference code, and full source code is provided in this Libreboot release. EDK is short for: EFI Development Kit.
More specifically:
We use a fork of EDK2 provided by MrChromebox, which specifically offers use of EDK2 as a generic coreboot payload. This is a complete, industry-standard UEFI implementation capable of booting any compliant operating system.
All. Nearly all boards use it in Libreboot now, even older ones e.g. ThinkPad X200. Tested on all platforms. Older, 32-bit-only mainboards still use the setup from the previous release, while virtually all 64-bit-compatible hardware now use EDK2. EFI builds of GRUB and SeaBIOS are provided in flash, accessible from the EDK2 setup menu (press ESC at boot time when prompted), for backward compatibility with existing Linux/BSD installations in Libreboot.
EDK2 support was integrated as a prerequisite for Chromebooks, but then it was extended for use on all previously existing supported mainboards in Libreboot. It even runs well on really old machines e.g. ThinkPad T500.
All Libreboot boards now use an open source UEFI implementation by default, where feasible. All of them, and it’s by default - the bare metal GRUB and SeaBIOS coreboot payloads have been removed, replaced by EFI versions that are backward compatible.
We previously did not provide UEFI at all, except for U-Boot on a few select mainboards. We now provide full UEFI, with all four freedoms intact, on nearly every mainboard that Libreboot supports.
All of the following operating systems were tested and confirmed working, on various Libreboot machines using EDK2:
Modern computers and operating systems are all UEFI, and this new setup can boot basically all of it reliably. More testing will be required, but this is battle-tested code and it has been used extensively in coreboot by many users, for many years. Libreboot finally has it as well.
This change alone is part of a much wider effort to modernise Libreboot, and I’m only getting started. The next Libreboot releases will draw heavily on this, as EDK2 is pretty much the expected payload on a lot of modern coreboot setups these days. Integration of EDK2 removes all limitations in Libreboot, allowing us to support much newer hardware and it basically makes Libreboot much easier to use; I expect Libreboot to become much more popular and useful over time, as a result.
Some caveats and extra notes are provided at the end of this release page, for migration purposes. For example, despite boasting about all, older 32-bit-only hardware still use bare GRUB/SeaBIOS. Nearly all boards use EDK2 however!
Finally, some notes about graphics cards that may be of interest to the more demanding users.
EDK2 alongside PCI-E relocatable BAR (rebar) means that you can now use modern graphics cards that require allocations above 4GB. Rebar is not enabled by default, but you can enable it in menuconfig (coreboot). Enabling it causes breakage of Intel GPU video output on libgfxinit in testing, and no fix was found yet, so rebar is disabled by default.
PCI-E Option ROMs are enabled by default where necessary, for EDK2 builds. This means you can use it on desktops with graphics cards. Tested on several desktops (Dell OptiPlex 9020 SFF and 5050 SFF).
On boards with only external GPU, rebar can be left enabled by default. On systems with functioning integrated graphics, accidental enablement of rebar can be remedied by inserting a graphics card; when the video output in the payload does become broken, the Linux i915 video driver still initialises video framebuffer properly in KMS/DRM.
This will be added to general documentation, pending fixes upstream. Note that even with this issue, Linux video output works fine. This will be corrected and rebar enabled by default, on a future Libreboot release.

That is why I went to such extreme effort integrating EDK2. Rather than replace U-Boot with yet another incomplete UEFI implementation, I simply added EDK2 which is already feature-complete as it is the reference, industry-standard implementation of the UEFI spec.
CrabEFI was what I initially considered, to replace U-Boot. This is a new payload written by Arthur Heymans, and the project itself is excellent, but rustc/cargo is inherently unreliable for several reasons:
Rust is awesome, but in my opinion not production-ready yet. Give it another ten years. EDK2 is infinitely more complex, but the size of the code is not a concern as it is generally stable, and written in C (some small bits of it are in C++). It has the most amount of eyes on it, and regularly receives security audits. It’s one of the most battle-tested codebases on the internet, and works well.
CrabEFI will likely be merged in Libreboot at some time in the future, when Rust tooling has stabilised across distros (including BSDs). It has a much better design, even if it’s not yet feature-complete, and use of Rust means that it will have fewer memory bugs. For now, Libreboot uses EDK2 to replace U-Boot and it has been enabled by default on all boards.
On memory safety, we can say contrarily that EDK2 is at least stable; bugs are also stable. That’s the point. We have stable C/C++ compilers, properly tested according to formalised standards (not really, because GCC and LLVM do all sorts of whacky things too, but they also try to be compatible with each other behaviourally). You want your code, when compiled, to behave exactly the same, bugs included. This is more strongly guaranteed with projects written in C and C++ (especially pure C projects, and most of EDK2 is in fact pure C).
It was originally intended that U-Boot would become Libreboot’s primary payload on x86, but alas. I bit the Intel-flavoured bullet and went with EDK2.

No ability to change the keyboard layout yet - a graphical menu option can be added for this later in EDK2, saved to SMMSTORE. The EFI build of GRUB uses terminal_input console only, and input is thus handled by the EDK2 input drivers (ditto USB mass storage and other storage devices); GRUB’s nativedisk driver is removed for example, and its native NVMe/xHCI drivers are no longer needed as in previous Libreboot releases.
EDK2 input drivers work with both USB and PS/2 keyboards in Libreboot. GRUB is made to use console input instead of at_keyboard as it otherwise lacks USB keyboard support in the EFI build. Thus, layouts must be handled by EDK2. There is code for this in EDK2, not currently handled by either Libreboot or MrChromebox so it all defaults to UK QWERTY in this release. Upstream (MrChromebox) uses US QWERTY by default, but Libreboot is a British project, so EDK2 was patched accordingly. Work will be done post-release.

This simplifies builds, and users can insert a keymap.gkb file inside CBFS from config/data/grub/keymap/ if they wish; if you add a grub.cfg to flash with terminal_input set to at_keyboard, this will work on devices with PS/2 keyboards if wanting to use the GRUB EFI payload (a command in grub.cfg to load the keymap is also needed, specifically keymap (cbfsdisk)/keymap.gkb.
EDK2 has been tested extensively on all Intel hardware from GM45 e.g. ThinkPad X200/T400, all the way to Intel Kabylake hardware supported by Libreboot e.g. ThinkPad T480, X280, Dell Optiplex 3050/5050.
The new compatibility modules in EDK2 should more than suffice for most users, who may still want GRUB/SeaBIOS for booting their existing Linux and BSD setups. It is recommended that you convert these to EFI or just reinstall them - use of UEFI is strongly encouraged as the default from now on, but Libreboot must (will) continue supporting older setups.
FreeBSD users booting with SeaBIOS text mode may be interested in this, as BIOS-mode FreeBSD bootloaders do not boot with anything else. A UEFI-based FreeBSD installation will work just fine in EDK2 though, so new FreeBSD installations are best recommended via EDK2 (do not use the compatibility modules of GRUB/SeaBIOS).
Some users might want it. Here are some brief notes. They won’t be added to the main documentation as they pertain to a configuration that Libreboot now regards as obsolete, but some users may nonetheless find it useful in the short term:
Older mainboards that are 32-bit only or with limited flash space still use the standalone GRUB/SeaBIOS payloads without EDK2; some newer ones with weird graphics setups also still use lone SeaBIOS/GRUB e.g. HP EliteBook 8560w.
To re-enable use of standalone GRUB/SeaBIOS (without edk2), modify target.cfg as follows for your board: remove build_depend, payload_edk2 and grubtree variables. Then add these lines:
payload_grub="y"
payload_seabios="y"
payload_memtest="y"
This is obsolete now in Libreboot and may even be removed in a future release. EDK2 is much better as it boots nearly everything, and supports more hardware setups. It is simply much more versatile than Libreboot’s old setup as in the previous release.
Then you can just re-build. NOTE: In the above guidance, build_depend is assumed to be grub/default seabios/build memtest86plus/default by default. You may want to set them explicitly:
build_depend="seabios/build grub/default memtest86plus/default"
You may also wish to set the GRUB tree explicitly:
grubtree="default"
Example config: config/module/coreboot/t480_vfsp_16mb/target.cfg
Just do this and re-build if you want these old setups back. This will be supported for as long as feasible, but will probably be removed at some point, maybe years from now, maybe a week from now. Use it while it’s there. The xHCI patches are still in there, and everything is configured as it should be.
In setups with EDK2, please note: txtmode not provided. If wanting e.g. SeaBIOS txtmode, look at e.g.:
config/module/coreboot/t480_vfsp_16mb/config/libgfxinit_corebootfb
Renamed it to libgfxinit_txtmode in this example and do:
./mk -m coreboot t480_vfsp_16mb
./mk -s coreboot t480_vfsp_16mb
In the menu that appears, go into Devices -> Display -> Framebuffer mode and select Legacy VGA text mode - this is not recommended for any new setups, but the FreeBSD bootloader starts in text mode and the framebuffer modes in coreboot can’t switch back to it. Booting in text mode is recommended when booting the FreeBSD BIOS-mode bootloader, and then FreeBSD can be set up for KMS in Xorg/Wayland. Otherwise, new installations should be done in EFI mode directly via EDK2 (no compatibility modules). More information about this is available in Libreboot’s BSD documentation.
CSMWrap is the SeaBIOS CSM build provided inside EDK2. It hogs an entire CPU core at boot, so e.g. quad-core will show up as three-core inside Linux. It’s a known limitation that will be corrected in a future release, due to the design of CSMWrap. If you need backward compatibility, it’s better to use the GRUB EFI build instead which does not have this problem. Both GRUB EFI and SeaBIOS CSM are available in the EDK2 menu via ESC keypress at boot time.
Users are best served just switching to native UEFI boot with ESP, using Libreboot’s EDK2 payload.

8. I know why Mathematicians weep

Article
In the last few months, OpenAI has stunned the mathematical community with the publication of hundreds of math findings. Many of these proofs, which span number theory, theoretical computer science, and logic, had stumped the world’s best mathematicians for decades, or centuries.
The reaction from the math community has been a mix of indignation, astonishment, and sorrow. “Tuesday night, I cried and it woke up my daughter,” said Hugo Duminil-Copin, the recipient of the 2022 Fields Medal, after OpenAI’s October release. “They have annihilated my field of research.”
“I literally cried when I spoke to Wired a few weeks ago,” said Steven Strogatz, a mathematician and author at Cornell University. “People are using language like ‘they’re strip-mining our subject’ or ‘they’re nuking the landscape.’ The feeling is disorientation. I’m discombobulated some days. I’m exhilarated some days. And there is enormous grief.”
I wanted to talk to one of the mourners to understand their technological grief. I wanted to know if theirs was a familiar case of bruised ego or disappointment—akin to, say, a boy who spends an hour building a tower out of blocks and watches his big brother smash the thing to smithereens—or something stranger and more profound.
To be honest, I also wanted to know what it might feel like for me. Today, the mathematicians are weeping, because the machine smashed their tower first. But tomorrow, it may be the physicists, the economists, the illustrators, or the writers who weep over their fallen blocks. I am not one of those AI people who believes that hundreds of millions of jobs will be wiped clean from existence in the next five years. But I am a realist who recognizes that a rapidly improving technology whose output is intelligence is destined to make violent contact with a modern sensibility that prizes human intellect and often equates it with purpose, dignity, and self-worth.
I wanted to know our future. So I called Steven Strogatz.
Craft + Conversation
To prepare you for where my conversation with Steven went, I want to step back from math to ask a question that feels a bit embarrassing in its bigness: What makes humans special?
Some might say intelligence and sociality. We are the smartest creature and Aristotle’s “social animal.” But we share each quality with other species. Crows solve multistep puzzles. Ants congregate and specialize. Elephants appear to mourn their dead.
What sets humans apart is the way these two capacities multiply each other. Human intelligence is unusually suited to imitation and iteration. We imitate our parents and peers with uncanny fidelity, and then we tinker. We copy, and we create. Our sociality spreads this copying-and-tinkering process across groups and generations, so that one discovery becomes the floor for the next. Chimpanzees in West Africa have cracked nuts with stones in the same way for 4,000 years. In roughly the same period, humans went from Babylonian clay tablets to space rockets to machine intelligence. What makes us special, it would seem, is this capacity to compound. Other animals craft and converse, but only humans blend craft and conversation into a culture that builds on itself.
Science itself is both a craft and a conversation. As a craft, science is a mode of thinking; a special way of paying attention to the world; a lens that regards phenomena as something more than mere appearance. To the scientist’s eye, the universe is akin to a cosmic attic of treasure chests, each filled with hidden gemstones that can be pulled into the light if we ask the right questions, find the right keys, turn the right locks.
But science is not a solitary treasure hunt. It is a social experience, akin to a game. Like many games, it is both collaborative and adversarial. Newton famously compared his achievements to “standing on the shoulders of giants,” and a part of science is extending that human pyramid infinitely upward. Feynman preferred to describe science as “the belief in the ignorance of experts”—which is to say, a game of giant-slaying. In both cases, science is a relationship to other people. Whether we’re asking “why, professor?” or insisting “no, that’s not quite it, Mr. Big Shot,” science is not just an act of discovery but a relation to fellow discoverers. Science is a conversation.
End of Conversation
When I called Steven to ask why he cried, he said he wanted to tell me about Archimedes.
Two thousand years ago, the Greek mathematician Archimedes calculated the area of curved objects and pinned down the value of pi by measuring the areas of dozens of tiny polygons that filled out a circle. Writing to the mathematician Eratosthenes about his work on curved shapes and infinity, he wrote that “there will be no lack of discoverers to add to the number of theorems which have fallen to our share.” To Strogatz, this sentence contains the essence of mathematics. I could hear his voice break even as he told this part of the story. “I feel like Archimedes is writing to me, or to people like me,” Strogatz said. “Archimedes is saying, ‘I can’t solve these things, but maybe you people in the future can stand on my shoulders.’ That is a very emotional, existential thing. There’s this feeling that we’re in this together, questing for something beautiful and transcendent and sublime.”
A hard-boiled tech optimist might tell Strogatz to get a grip. Math doesn’t care about your feelings, scientific reality is indifferent to your tears, and so forth. He knows it, too. “We’re being derided as guild protectors: Your guild is getting wiped out. Boo-hoo. This has happened to other people before. Suck it up.”
I know why the mathematicians weep. Yes, some are crying about bruised egos, the feeling of wasted years, a creeping sense of status obliteration, the frustration of an unfair competitor, the indignation of feeling cheated and mistreated, and uncertainty about the future. But they are also mourning, because they think something alien and disembodied is setting fire to Archimedes’ letters and dancing in the ashes. Humanity is being stripped from human science. When OpenAI publishes several hundred proofs that end several hundred treasure hunts, it is succeeding brilliantly along the craft axis of science, but it is interrupting the thousand-year conversation. The machines and their architects are saying: From here on out, it’s our turn to speak.
Knowledge Without Understanding
And what if OpenAI is right? What if we ought to treat math mostly as a quest, rather than as a conversation where feelings matter as much as discovery?
Strogatz compared the relationship between the math community and the AI labs to the two biggest tennis tournaments of the summer. “I was thinking about Wimbledon versus the US Open,” he told me. At Wimbledon, the players dress in white, the fans are respectful, and while winning matters, sportsmanship is at a premium. The U.S. Open, however, is brash, individualistic, and disruptive. “People wear their own colors and fans heckle,” he said. “It’s more democratic, and it’s less elitist.”
Like Wimbledon, the field of mathematics is old, and a certain decorum is still cherished among its modern practitioners. Like the US Open, the labs are young, brash, and even a little rude. But AI’s ability to democratize access to mathematical genius may be a virtue of its own. Earlier this year, Shanmu Jin, a Chinese medical resident, used ChatGPT to solve a problem called Crouzeix’s conjecture, which professional mathematicians had failed to solve for decades. (Several mathematicians examined the result and found that it works, but the paper is awaiting formal peer review.)
Think about a world where non-mathematicians leapfrog the field after a few AI prompts. It would be a world with more knowledge. But it might be a world with less understanding. AI models have a mind of their own, and it is not even what I would call a “mind.” Their mathematical proofs often take on alien shapes, and their reasoning can be inscrutable to experts. “There is a Twilight Zone aspect to all of this,” Strogatz said. “We are interacting with electrons moving in circuits, and we don’t understand the intelligence that they create.” He raised the example of Google Translate, which has essentially solved the problem of live translation between languages. “Google Translate is amazing, but it has taught us essentially nothing about linguistics,” Strogatz said. “We don’t understand why it works. It just works. That’s the Twilight Zone. It is knowledge without understanding.”
That might be a huge loss. Understanding isn’t just a source of pleasure, the dopaminergic rush of the aha! moment. Understanding is power. When Maxwell understood the laws of electricity and magnetism that other scientists had built up through the 1800s, he showed through calculus that electricity and magnetism were not forces between two objects but something more like a field. Understanding Maxwell, Einstein could look at gravity—commonly assumed to be a force between two objects—and prove that gravity, too, is a field that extends across the universe. One might argue that it required a human understanding of fields to come up with the most important theory in physics.
The ability to understand the components of each new scientific breakthrough has allowed people to build mighty branches off the tree of knowledge. But Strogatz is prepared for a future where the tree grows beyond our reckoning. He shared the story of an MIT computer scientist, Regina Barzilay. After surviving breast cancer in her early 40s, she was stunned to discover how little her doctors knew about breast cancer diagnosis. She led a team that trained AI on hundreds of thousands of mammograms to predict breast cancer cases many years in advance.
Strogatz asked Barzilay, “Do you understand what the AI is seeing?”
“No,” she said.
“Does it worry you that you don’t understand your own invention?” Strogatz asked.
Her response: “Why would we limit a life-saving model to what humans can understand?”
Lost + Found
Barzilay’s question is hard to answer. I don’t want to die of the cancers that killed my parents. I also don’t want to lose my job to a tangle of GPUs. What do we want from technology? Solve every problem we have without creating any problem we don’t have. Cure diseases, but safely. Jailbreak the secrets of the cosmos, but do it with some respect. This is a lot to ask for, but it is not too much to want. Give me the benefits of superhuman intelligence; keep for me the things that make human intelligence meaningful.
When I got off the phone with Strogatz, I thought some more about Archimedes. He left problems for other people to solve, and the unknown served as an invitation. The danger is that by automating the search for knowledge, AI might obliterate the human connections that sustained the search. But there is another possibility. If intelligence becomes less uniquely human, we may come to value more highly what remains uniquely human: our capacity to relate and to care for one another.
Archimedes solved problems, and Archimedes wrote letters. He discovered, and he shared. AI may take over much of the first job. The second is ours, if we’re wise enough to keep it. Mathematicians may still teach proofs, argue over what they mean, translate alien logic into human insight, and pass that understanding to students. The challenge will be to protect that half of science: the pleasure of encountering another mind and being moved by the encounter. When the machines have discovered every truth there is to find, there may be nothing left to find except each other.
