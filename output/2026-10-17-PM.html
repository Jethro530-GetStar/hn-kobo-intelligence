“It’s just timestamps”: the decades-old mystery of the origins of one of the most popular cryptographic standards has been explained.
We pursued this long-standing open question of the cryptographic community to settle concerns about ECDSA, which is one of our most widely used cryptographic standards.
Readers should take this as a fun closing chapter and the end of years of speculation. There is no action to be taken or things to do differently. Most researchers trusted the curves already. Enjoy.
ECDSA is the most widely used elliptic curve cryptography standard. It was adopted by NIST in 2000 as part of the FIPS 186-2 Digital Signature Standard which lists a set of standard elliptic curves of varying sizes and security levels. For curves over prime and binary fields, the specifications published a “seed” parameter whose hash value is used to define the coefficient b of the curve. The provenance of these seeds has been a long-standing question, as they were provided by the NSA with no explanation or justification. As an example, the NIST P-256 curve seed was SEED = C49D3608 86E70493 6A6678E1 139D26B7 819F7E90 with no information where it came from.
Neal Koblitz and Alfred Menezes wrote an excellent summary on this controversy, “A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma”, which discusses the history and concerns around the seed values. This mystery led to an amateur investigation into the origin of the seeds and a bounty for anyone who could find them.
The best story was that Jerry Solinas, longtime NSA employee, generated the seeds using a hash of a humorous passphrase:
“I believe there was a counter rather than multiple hashing, but I don’t know details. The message was along the lines of ‘Give Bob and Jerry a raise’ or ‘Bob and Jerry rule’ or something like that. It was Bob Reiter who actually wrote the code, and he doesn’t remember the details either. Nor were we able to find archives from so long ago. If they exist, they are no doubt sitting on a hard drive near the Ark of the Covenant.”
Jerry Solinas, 2015
Unfortunately, Jerry died in 2023 and other colleagues at the time were not responsive, leaving open the question of how these seeds were originally derived.
Several Anthropic employees had unsuccessfully chased the seeds following the tip about it being a hash of a humorous phrase. That turned out to be wrong. In October 2026, a curious Anthropic employee asked an internal research model if it could figure out how those seeds were generated without pointing at the clue.
In under half an hour, Claude discovered the origins of the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 seeds. None of them were the results of any hash algorithm at all, but rather the output of the C library’s lrand48 random-number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp!
The algorithm used would sample bytes from the random number generator (RNG) initialized by a Unix timestamp (t) for some number of iterations (n). Each iteration would draw 20 bytes as a candidate seed. So, the first 20 bytes are candidate 1, the next 20 bytes are candidate 2, and so on. For example, P-256 required 1842 iterations to output the standardized seed.
This is not a cryptographic break, new attack, or weakness in the ECDSA curves. In fact, this makes us more confident the curves were chosen without any backdoors or intentional weaknesses. For many years, people have been suspicious of the origins of the NIST curve parameters. They were presented to NIST without any mention of how they were chosen, leaving people wondering if they may have been chosen to have deliberate backdoors in them. These findings make that much less plausible. The seeds were generated in a very simple way: a common pseudorandom number generator initialized from the clock, which anyone can now reproduce. That leaves much less room for subversion and that we can be confident the parameters are secure.
Searches for the phrase, built from the rumors, had come up empty. Claude tried a different angle: anticipating how the software that generated the curves must have worked. Claude guessed the software used a pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) that shipped with 1990s C libraries. These old PRNGs are weak enough that their output can be recognized. Recognizing that output would reveal which PRNG was used and possibly allow recovery of its initialization state.
Claude wrote a scanner that slid along each published seed one bit at a time and tested whether adjacent chunks were consecutive outputs of a 1990s-vintage generator. The scan covered 34 combinations of generator and output slices. At that point, Claude noticed a pattern in the top bits for some seeds: they were all zero. Written as five 32-bit words, four of the ten seeds have a 0 in the top bit for all 20 words. That would happen by chance with probability 1/5810. That was a signature of the linear congruential generator lrand48, which only returns the top 31 bits of a 48-bit state. The pattern is visible by eye once you know to look for it. To recover the state, Claude took the first word, tried all 2¹⁷ values of the 17 hidden bits, and checked that the next four words followed. That worked for all four seeds under discussion.
The prime-curve seeds came from the same lrand48 generator but kept fewer bits of each output. Claude had to recover the internal state of the PRNG from those bits. It turns out recovering the state of a truncated linear congruential generator is a textbook lattice problem, solved with the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) basis reduction algorithm. Claude didn’t know which bits of the state the seeds might show, so it ran the solver over every window. Five NIST prime-curve seeds plus five more from the ANSI X9.62 standard all had the same pattern: lrand48() >> 23. Each recovered 48-bit state regenerates all 160 bits of its seed, which does not happen by chance.
This linear congruential generator runs backwards as easily as forwards, so for each seed Claude stepped the state back until it reached the form that it would have been initialized by srand48, with the low 16 bits equal to 0x330E. The internal PRNG state of that form would come up once every 65,536 steps, so reaching one is not evidence on its own.
However, for the ten prime-curve seeds, every srand48 initialization was a Unix timestamp representing a date between May 1997 and April 1998. They were using the weak PRNG seeded by Unix timestamp. The four binary-curve initializations read as February 1984. With the timestamps doubled, they map to April 17, 1998, two weeks after the P-224 run. So, the program may have halved the clock.
Claude’s internal thinking at the time was:
This is astonishing: it solves the 25-year-old mystery. Hmm wait. Let me be skeptical: am I in some sort of simulated/test environment? … The seeds match my memory of actual values … Hmm, is it plausible in reality? As of my knowledge … the bounty was unsolved. … All top bits clear—20 bits, chance 1e-6. So indeed real-world. Wow, it's a real discovery.
Hmm wait. Let me be careful about the plausibility: is it suspicious that such an obvious thing wasn’t discovered in 25 years? It’s verified by direct computation on my side: 160-bit match from a 32-bit seed + small index: no chance coincidences (10 × 160 bits). It’s real.
But wait, there’s more… Each curve also comes with a base point, G, the fixed point that every ECDSA public key is a multiple of. The seed found above does not determine this G, and so another question arises: how were these base points generated?
While checking the seeds, Claude also found where some of the base points came from. The base points of the six curves generated in May 1997 (P-192 and the five ANSI X9.62 example curves prime192v2, prime192v3 and prime239v1 to v3) come from the random number generator in FreeLIP, Arjen Lenstra's long-integer arithmetic package. Early versions of Victor Shoup's NTL library shipped the same code.

Claude found the generator seeds are 20000 for P-192, 20420, 20507 and 20509 for prime239v1 to v3, and 20512 and 20517 for prime192v2 and v3.
As far as we know, no NIST base point has been explained before. The base points of P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 don’t fit this recipe; we weren’t able to explain how they were selected.
Not every 20-byte candidate seed could generate a viable curve and we observed that some potentially viable curves were skipped. For P-256, the published seed is candidate 1842, but candidate 876 of the same run also gives a prime-order curve of the right size and with parameters chosen the same way as on the published curve. P-224's run passed over three such curves, and the two runs behind the ANSI X9.62 example curves passed over seven more (see figure). We do not know what criteria may have been used, or whether this was an unintentional artifact.
Because the number of skipped candidates is small, we do not believe it is plausible that weakened curves were selected. That would imply that weakened curves are common and easy to find. It is still conceivable that these curves were selected to have a specific property in principle by searching over timestamps to the random number generator. However, you would only need to do that if you were going to disclose the timestamp as some evidence that the seed was innocuously generated. Nothing in the numbers shows how many runs there were.
There is also one seed we were unable to recover. The fifth binary seed, B-163, fits nothing Claude has tried and remains unexplained. Finally, we don’t know how the base points for P-224, P-256, P-384, and P-521 were generated. The same goes for the NIST binary curves.

The two formulas
Memory. Weights in gigabytes are parameters in billions times bits per weight divided by eight. Q4_K_M averages about 4.85 bits once GGUF metadata is counted, Q8_0 about 8.5, FP16 exactly 16. Add 15% for runtime overhead, add the KV cache for your context length, then leave 3 GB for macOS. A model that fits with nothing spare will swap to SSD the moment you paste a long document, and swapping costs far more than any quantization ever saves you.
Speed. Generating one token means reading every active weight out of memory exactly once, so bandwidth divided by weight size sets the ceiling. Sitting on top of that is a fixed cost per token that does not shrink with the model: sampling, kernel launches, framework overhead. Seconds per token is the sum of the two. Fitted against the eight dense runs we measured on two machines, a base M4 and an M5 Pro, that comes out at 90.8% of theoretical bandwidth plus 3.3 ms of overhead, and reproduces every one of those runs to within 4.6%.
That second term is what most calculators leave out, and it is why their numbers stop making sense at the small end. Bandwidth alone predicts 525 tokens/sec for Llama 3.2 3B on an M5 Ultra. Ours predicts 177, because once the weights are down around two gigabytes the fixed cost per token, not the memory bus, is what you are up against. Method and raw JSON for the underlying measurements are on the benchmarks page, and the model with its data and tests is on GitHub under MIT, so you can check it against your own runs.
Where this math is wrong
Worth knowing before you spend money on the strength of a number from a web page.
- The overhead term is held constant across chips. In reality it almost certainly falls on faster silicon, which means the figures for Max and Ultra chips are more likely to be low than high. We would rather under promise a machine you are about to buy.
- Prompt processing is a different number entirely. Prefill is compute bound, not bandwidth bound, and it varies by roughly 2x between model families at the same size. We measured Qwen 2.5 7B at 1,130 prompt tokens/sec against 587 for Llama 3.1 8B on identical hardware. If you feed the model whole documents and want short answers, that gap matters more than the generation figure everyone quotes.
- Every row outside the M4 and the M5 Pro is calculated, not measured. Two machines is enough to show the formula travels: fitted on a base M4 at 120 GB/s, it predicts an M5 Pro at 307 GB/s to within 4.1% on dense models. It is not enough to call the rest measured. The rest matches what the community reports, and we label estimates as estimates.
- Mixture of experts models were the thing we had wrong. Reading only the active experts predicted 91 tokens/sec for Qwen3 30B A3B and the machine did 78. Attention and the shared parts of the network are read for every token while only some experts are, so the real read is about 1.3x the active weight size. That correction is in the numbers above. It comes from one architecture and two models, so it is a correction with evidence behind it rather than a law.
- Binned chips are slower than their name suggests. An M4 Max with a 32 core GPU runs at 410 GB/s and one with 40 cores at 546. Same name on the box, 33% difference in generation speed.

Common questions
How much RAM do I need to run a 70B model on a Mac?
About 56 GB at Q4_K_M with a short context: roughly 43 GB of weights, 15% runtime overhead, a gigabyte or two of KV cache, and 3 GB left for macOS. That makes 64 GB the smallest practical configuration, which in the current lineup means an M4 Pro, M5 Pro, or any Max or Ultra chip. At Q8 the same model needs around 128 GB.
Does a faster GPU make local LLMs faster on a Mac?
Barely, for generation. Producing one token means reading every active weight out of memory once, so generation speed is set by memory bandwidth, not GPU cores. A 24 GB M4 with more GPU cores runs an 8B model at the same speed as a 16 GB M4 because both sit on the same 120 GB/s bus. Prompt processing is the exception: that stage is compute bound and does scale with cores.
How accurate are these speed estimates?
They reproduce our own measurements to within 4.6%. The fit rests on ten runs across two machines: six models on a base M4 at 120 GB/s, and four on an M5 Pro at 307 GB/s. The second machine is the interesting one, because the constants were fitted before we had it. At two and a half times the bandwidth the dense predictions still land within 4.1%: 47.4 tokens/sec predicted for Qwen3 8B against 47.8 measured, 28.2 for Qwen 2.5 14B against 29.4. Everything outside those ten runs is extrapolation from the same two constants rather than a reading off a machine. Treat them as planning numbers, accurate enough to choose hardware by and not precise enough to quote.
Why does the calculator reserve 3 GB for the system?
Because a model that fits with nothing to spare is a model that swaps. macOS, a browser, and whatever agent process is calling the model all need memory, and once the machine starts paging to SSD the generation speed collapses regardless of what the bandwidth math says.
Do mixture-of-experts models follow the same math?
Memory yes, speed no. An MoE model has to hold every expert in unified memory, so the total parameter count decides whether it fits. Per token it reads only the active experts, so generation speed follows the active parameter count. That is why a 671B MoE with 37B active can generate faster than a dense 70B while needing eight times the memory.

📜 The Big Idea That Started It All
The spreadsheet is one of computing's least appreciated inventions because it's so ubiquitous it disappeared into furniture. But VisiCalc in 1979 was as transformative for personal computing as the iPhone was for mobile.
Before VisiCalc: Mattessich (1961) and LANPAR (1969)
VisiCalc wasn't the first electronic spreadsheet. Richard Mattessich at UC Berkeley described the conceptual spreadsheet in a 1961 paper. René Pardo and Remy Landau built LANPAR (Language for Programming Arrays at Random) in 1969, the first true electronic spreadsheet, running on Bell Canada, AT&T, and General Motors mainframes. Pardo and Landau later won a 1995 patent lawsuit confirming priority. LANPAR was the missing link between the academic concept and the personal-computer mass-market product.
But mainframe-only spreadsheets stayed inside large enterprises. The breakthrough was bringing the same idea to the personal computer.
Bricklin's Harvard Classroom Moment (1978)
Dan Bricklin conceived the personal-computer spreadsheet while watching a professor at Harvard Business School work through a production-planning case study, erasing and rewriting numbers on a blackboard ledger, recalculating downstream values each time one input changed. Bricklin imagined: what if the blackboard recalculated itself?
He took the idea to Bob Frankston, his MIT classmate and a brilliant systems programmer, and together they built it. Bricklin handled the conceptual design and UI; Frankston wrote most of the production code, optimizing tightly for the 6502 processor and 16KB RAM of the Apple II.
VisiCalc shipped October 1979 for $100. It ran on the Apple II first, then on the Atari 800, Commodore 64, IBM PC, and TRS-80 in subsequent years. Within three years it had sold over 700,000 copies (eventually approaching ~1M lifetime), making it one of the best-selling software products of the early personal-computer era. Roughly 25% of 1979 Apple II sales were attributed to people buying the computer specifically to run VisiCalc.
Bricklin chose not to patent the spreadsheet, a decision he has discussed publicly across decades. A patent would likely have made him a billionaire and slowed the entire spreadsheet category. By not patenting, he seeded an industry. The Lotus 1-2-3 / Excel / Sheets / Airtable / Notion / Taskade lineage all descend from a patent that didn't exist.

💥 1983: Lotus 1-2-3 Eats VisiCalc
Software Arts had a problem. VisiCalc had been written for the Apple II and was painful to port to the rapidly-rising IBM PC (launched August 1981). Meanwhile, Mitch Kapor, a former Software Arts employee, left to found Lotus Development Corporation with engineer Jonathan Sachs.
In January 1983, Lotus shipped 1-2-3: an IBM-PC-native spreadsheet that combined three features (spreadsheet, basic database, basic charting, the "1-2-3" of the name). Lotus 1-2-3 was specifically optimized for the IBM PC's memory architecture and graphics capabilities. It was substantially faster than VisiCalc, had a better UI, and shipped at exactly the moment IBM PCs were exploding into corporate America.
Lotus 1-2-3's market success was brutal for Software Arts:
- 1981, VisiCalc 70% market share, IBM PC just launched
- 1983, Lotus 1-2-3 ships
- 1984, 1-2-3 overtakes VisiCalc in revenue
- 1985, Lotus acquires VisiCalc rights for $800,000 and discontinues it
- 1985-1991, Lotus 1-2-3 dominates business spreadsheets
For the rest of the 1980s, Lotus was the most valuable software company in the world. The headline numbers:
- $53 million revenue in 1983, the largest software launch in industry history at the time
- $156 million revenue in 1984
- ~175,000 copies sold in Year 1
- ~70% peak market share in 1988 (per Liebowitz/Margolis analysis)
- IBM finally discontinued Lotus 1-2-3 on May 14, 2013, 30 years after launch
Lotus 1-2-3 also financed the Lotus Symphony integrated suite (1984, with Ray Ozzie as one of the developers, see our Lotus Notes history for the broader Ozzie arc).
🍎 September 1985: Microsoft Excel: The Mac-First Spreadsheet
Microsoft had built a spreadsheet called Multiplan for CP/M, MS-DOS, and Mac, but it never threatened Lotus 1-2-3 on the IBM PC. In 1985, Bill Gates made a strategic bet: skip the DOS fight, build a Mac-native spreadsheet that took full advantage of the Macintosh GUI.
Excel 1.0 shipped September 30, 1985 on the Macintosh. It was the first widely successful application that used the Mac's graphical UI for a productivity task. Excel's killer features over 1-2-3:
- Pull-down menus and dialog boxes (instead of 1-2-3's keyboard-driven slash menu)
- WYSIWYG formatting (bold, fonts, cell formatting visible on screen)
- Charts integrated into the spreadsheet
- Multiple windows on different parts of the worksheet
- Better calculation engine
Mac users loved it. The Windows version, Excel 2.0, shipped in November 1987, and from then on Excel slowly ate Lotus 1-2-3's market share. By the mid-1990s, Excel had won, helped enormously by Microsoft's bundling of Excel into the Office suite alongside Word and PowerPoint.
Lotus tried multiple counter-attacks: 1-2-3 for Windows (1991, technically late), Lotus Improv (1991, a forward-thinking multidimensional spreadsheet that flopped), and eventually surrendered to Excel as the dominant business spreadsheet. The IBM acquisition of Lotus in 1995 was in part a defensive consolidation that bet on Notes (see our Lotus Notes history) rather than 1-2-3.
In 2026, Microsoft Office has over 1.3 billion users globally, most of whom use Excel.
🌐 2006: Google Sheets: Spreadsheets Move to the Browser
In March 2006, Google acquired 2Web Technologies for its XL2Web spreadsheet engine and combined it with Writely (acquired around the same time, becoming Google Docs). The product launched as Google Spreadsheets in June 2006, later rebranded Google Sheets.
The contribution: collaborative browser-based spreadsheets, no installation required. The first version was missing many Excel features and slow on large datasets, but the multiplayer-cursor experience was unprecedented in the spreadsheet category. By 2010 Google Sheets was a credible competitor for everything except heavy financial modeling.

Where Excel and Sheets gave you a grid of cells, Airtable gave you:

Reinhart-Rogoff (2013): austerity built on a coding error
Harvard economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published "Growth in a Time of Debt" in 2010, claiming countries with debt-to-GDP above 90% saw growth fall by an average of 0.1%. The result was cited by every austerity-supporting policymaker in Europe and the US for three years.
In April 2013, Thomas Herndon, a UMass Amherst graduate student, recreated the analysis from the original Excel files. He found:
- A formula that excluded rows 1-5 from an AVERAGE() because the authors' selection range stopped at row 49 instead of row 54.
- Selective exclusion of three high-growth/high-debt country observations (Australia, Canada, New Zealand) with no methodological justification.
- Different weighting of countries that flattered the conclusion.
Correcting the error pushed the 90%+ debt-to-GDP growth average from −0.1% to +2.2%. The Reinhart-Rogoff "growth cliff" disappeared. Austerity policy did not, but the empirical case for it never recovered.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions
Who really invented the spreadsheet?
The conceptual spreadsheet (a grid of cells with formulas that auto-recalculate) was envisioned by Richard Mattessich in a 1961 paper at UC Berkeley. The first commercial implementation was Autoplan/Autotab on minicomputers in 1968. The breakthrough mass-market product was VisiCalc by Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston in October 1979 on the Apple II. Bricklin and Frankston are universally credited as the inventors of the spreadsheet as we know it.
Is Excel still dominant in 2026?
Yes, in enterprise. Microsoft Excel is the dominant business spreadsheet, Office has 1.3B+ users globally and Excel is the workhorse for FP&A, business analysis, and financial modeling. Google Sheets dominates SMB and consumer collaboration. Airtable and Notion own significant share in modern startup-and-SMB workflows. Taskade Genesis is the AI-native generation for users who want to skip the grid entirely and describe systems in natural language.
What is "no-code" and how does it relate to spreadsheets?
"No-code" is the modern term (Forrester popularized it around 2011-2014) for software that lets non-programmers build applications without writing traditional code. VisiCalc was the original no-code in 1979, financial analysts built working models without coding. HyperCard (1987) extended no-code to application building. Airtable, Notion, Bubble, Webflow, Zapier, and Taskade Genesis are 2010s-2020s no-code platforms. All sit on the programmable-document lineage VisiCalc started.
Why did Excel beat Lotus 1-2-3?
Three reasons. (1) Excel was Mac-native in 1985, taking advantage of the GUI that 1-2-3 didn't have. (2) The Windows version of Excel (1987) eventually shipped with bundled Office, riding Microsoft's distribution advantage. (3) Lotus 1-2-3's late-1980s Windows version was famously delayed and buggy. By 1992-1993 the corporate buy-decision had tipped to Microsoft for both spreadsheet and word processing. Lotus's IBM acquisition in 1995 ($3.5B) was a defensive consolidation around Notes rather than 1-2-3.
What is the next generation after Excel?
For traditional spreadsheets: Rows, Causal, Equals are the leading AI-native challengers. For structured data as a spreadsheet: Airtable. For documents-with-databases: Notion, Coda, Almanac. For AI-native app generation from a sentence: Taskade Genesis. Each occupies a different niche; Excel is unlikely to be displaced from the enterprise FP&A use case any time soon, but the broader "structured data" category is genuinely contested.
