Article
ZigZag is a visual embroidery editor built in Rust for performance, with built-in MCP (Model Context Protocol) support so AI agents can create and edit designs alongside you. Turn SVG artwork into stitches, fine-tune it in the editor, and export PES, DST, EXP, JEF, or VP3 for broad compatibility with embroidery machines—or send designs directly to a Brother Skitch (PP1) over Bluetooth.
Draw in Illustrator, draw in ZigZag, or let an agent draw. The Rust engine
and zigzag command-line tool power the same workflow outside the editor.

Satins wider than 7 mm are auto-split (shorter, staggered stitches) and use auto spacing (narrow columns more open, wide ones denser).
Planner features:
- Remove overlaps: fill hidden under later objects isn't sewn (keeps 0.5 mm of overlap).
- Cap order: center-out, bottom-up, grouped by color, still respecting layering.
- Routing: travel stitches stay out of sight: straight across where a straight line stays inside the fill, otherwise round the inside of its outline (Ink/Stitch's underpath), otherwise any way through the fill (round its holes, through its necks) or under whatever is sewn later on top. A fill in separate parts is sewn part by part, each part's underlay and top together. So a fill with cut-outs, or a dither in scattered blocks under another color, is sewn with few or no cuts. Trims only where none of that works (up to 50 mm of travel).
- Sewing control per object: a start and an end point (where the thread comes in and leaves, so the next object is close), trim after, stop after (to lay foam or trim appliqué). What's left to cut is the document's Connect bar: drag it to sew across gaps up to N mm (between objects, or the parts of one) with running stitches instead of cuts. Those stitches show, so the panel counts the cuts saved as you drag and the canvas lights up what would be sewn.
- Fabric presets: standard, thin cotton cap, structured cap, knit.
- Checks: narrow fills, wide/narrow satins, small lettering, tiny gaps, fully covered objects, hoop fit.
- Group colors: reorder to cut thread changes without breaking layering.

Laid out like Illustrator, styled like a Mac app:
- Tools down the left edge: Select (V), Direct Selection (A), Hand (H), Pen (P): click for corners, drag for curves (Option breaks the handles, Shift keeps 45°, Space moves the point being placed); while drawing, Option lends it the Anchor Point tool (drag a segment to bend it, a handle to move it alone, click a smooth point for a corner) and ⌘ lends Direct Selection (drag a point or handle, then keep drawing), and on a selected path Option-drag bends a segment too. Illustrator's Pen group is behind it: Add Anchor Point (+) clicks a point onto an outline without changing its shape, Delete Anchor Point (-) clicks one out (Option swaps the two while held), and Anchor Point (Shift+C) converts points between smooth and corner and drags one handle alone. Press and hold the Pen (or right-click it) for the group's flyout, Option-click it to step through the group, or tear the group off into a toolbar of its own with the strip at the flyout's edge; the Shape tools work the same way. Brush (B), with VectorCraft's path clean-up tools in its group: Smooth (drag along a path to take the wobble out: fewer points, smoother curves; Smoothness in the bar above), Path Eraser (drag across a path to cut that part away) and Join (scrub between two open ends to join them, or an open path's two ends to close it). Shapes (M rectangle, L ellipse, \ line; hold or right-click for polygon and star), Fill Angle (G), Measure (I), Text (T). Below them, the selection's thread swatch: click it for the thread color picker.
- Curves, the Illustrator way: the Pen clicks corners and click-drags curves (Option while dragging breaks the handle for a sharp turn; Shift keeps points and handles at 45°; hold Space to move the point you're placing). Click the last point to make it a corner, or drag from it to start the next curve in a new direction. Click the first point to close a shape; Enter, Esc, ⌘-click or another tool ends a line and keeps it. With a path selected, the Pen continues it from an open end, adds a point on its outline, deletes a point you click, and Option-click turns a point corner ↔ smooth (Option-drag pulls out new handles). While drawing, click another open path's end to join the two into one. The cursor shows what a click will do. Direct Selection drags points and handles of any shape from your SVG (circles and rects become paths); dragging a curve bends it under the pointer (Option-drag bends a straight one; a plain drag moves it); Delete removes points, arrows nudge them.
- Smart guides while drawing (View › Smart Guides, ⌘U): Pen points, shape corners and dragged points land on other objects' anchors and centers, where two outlines cross, on an outline, on 45° rays from the last point (level and upright handles too), or in line with any of those, with a magenta label saying which ("anchor", "intersect", "path", "align"…). With Shift, a point keeps its 45° angle and slides into line with the nearest target.
- Round corners: in Direct Selection a small circle sits inside each sharp corner; drag it in to round the corners (the selected points', or all of them), each only as far as its sides allow. Object › Path › Round Corners… does the same with a radius and Round, Inverted or Chamfer, previewed live.
- Brush (B), after Illustrator's Paintbrush: paint a line freehand and it comes out smooth, sewn as a satin column at the width you set (or as a running stitch). Smoothness takes out the hand's wobble; sharp turns stay sharp; end where you began to close the line. The cursor is a circle as wide as the satin.
- Shapes, the Illustrator way: Rectangle (M), Ellipse (L), Polygon, Star and Line (\). Drag from a corner; Option draws from the center, Shift makes a square or circle, keeps a polygon or star upright and a line at 45°. While dragging a polygon or star, ↑/↓ add or remove sides or points. Click without dragging to type an exact size. The bar at the top of the canvas switches shapes and sets corner radius, sides, points and the star's inner radius. Closed shapes come in as fills, lines as runs, in the current thread.
- Path tools: Object › Pathfinder (Unite, Minus Front, Intersect, Exclude; also buttons in the Transform panel when two or more objects are selected) and Object › Path (Offset Path…, Outline Stroke, Simplify…, Round Corners…, Clean Up…). Simplify… is VectorCraft's slider, from fewest points to closest to the original, previewed live, with Auto-Simplify picking the level for the selection's size. Clean Up… removes stray points, objects too small to sew (under 1 mm by default) and extra points (points on top of each other, points in the middle of straight lines), showing how many of each it found first. Results come back as smooth curves with their real corners (least-squares curve fitting), not as many-sided polygons.
- Logos from images (Image Trace): open, drop or place a PNG, JPEG, WebP, GIF, BMP or TIFF and the Trace Image window traces it into flat-color shapes, previewed on your hat's color. Choose how many colors (or Auto), the width, detail and the smallest speck to keep; remove the white background; snap every color to your Brother, Janome or own palette's threads; and sew thin lines as single lines. Flat logos trace exactly (edges placed to a fraction of a pixel, corners restored, circles and straight lines exact); photos are posterized. The command line traces too: zigzag convert logo.png -o logo.pes.
- Contextual task bar: under the selection, the next things you'd do: its stitch type, its thread, Edit Points, Round Corners, Simplify, Unite (two or more), Duplicate. View › Contextual Task Bar (or its ••• menu) hides it.
- Ask an agent: the Agent panel (full height in the left dock; the sparkle at the bottom of the tool strip opens and closes it, and ⌘K or Window › Ask Agent… opens it with the prompt ready) runs Claude Code or Codex in the background, connected to this studio's MCP server, so you watch it build the design. It can only use the design tools (no shell, no file edits). The transcript shows what it said and each step, with a spinner on the one running (the one place it's shown; collapse the panel and its header shows the step and the time instead). On the canvas, the objects it's working on get a moving outline, a shimmer and a label saying what it's doing (Drawing, Placing, Restyling…), for any agent connected over MCP, not just the panel's. The panel is named for the agent (Claude Code or Codex; switch in its ≡ menu); + in its header starts a new chat, Stop ends a run. Uses the claude or codex command you've already signed in to. Like any panel it can float, join another as a tab or dock right; sharing a dock it sits at the bottom, under a divider you drag to give it more room.
- Thread colors and palettes: the picker shows the nearest Brother/Janome thread, a palette to click (Brother, Janome or your own), and a small custom color field. Make palettes from scratch, from the design, or from a thread kit you own (the palette's ≡ menu › New Palette from Thread Kit: Brother's 12, 40 and 22 color sets, or 40 + 22 for all 61; then Match All keeps a design to the threads in your box), add the selection's thread with +, right-click to remove; they're remembered between launches.
- Threads panel: the design's threads in sewing order (each color block numbered, with its stitches, and "stop" where the same thread comes twice running) or By Color (each thread once, with how many objects use it). Click a row to select what it sews; click a swatch to change that block's thread, or in By Color that thread everywhere. Group Colors and the count of color changes and trims are under the list. The panel's ≡ menu has Match All to the chosen palette, and Show Palette puts a palette to click under the list (off by default, since the thread picker has the same palettes).
- Panels, the Illustrator way: the Agent has the left dock to itself; Objects, Stitch, Transform, Lettering, Threads and Document are on the right. The design's issues are in the status bar (bottom right): a warning sign with a count, or a check when all's well; click it (or Window › Issues) for the list, each linked to its object. Drag a panel by its header to reorder it, move it to the other dock, or drop it on the canvas to float it (each panel's ≡ menu also has Dock Left / Dock Right / Float). Drop a panel on another panel's header and they share it as tabs; drag a tab out (or use Separate in the ≡ menu) to part them, drag the header to move the group. » at the top of a dock collapses it to a strip of icons for a bigger canvas: click an icon and the panel flies out beside the strip (drag an icon to move that panel). Click a header to collapse a panel; Window › Reset Panels puts everything back. The layout is remembered.
- Tabs, one per document (as in VectorCraft and Illustrator): Open, drop or New opens a file in a tab of its own over the canvas (the empty document you start with makes way for the first one); a file that's already open just comes forward. Each tab keeps its own undo history, selection, zoom and stitches. Click a tab to switch, ⌃Tab / ⌃⇧Tab (or Window › Next / Previous Document) to step through them, drag one to reorder, middle-click or its × (or File › Close, ⌘W) to close it; + opens a new document and » lists every tab when they don't all fit. A dot in place of the × marks unsaved changes, and closing such a tab asks whether to save it first. Right-click a tab for Close Other Tabs and Copy Path. Quitting doesn't ask: every tab comes back next time, each with its unsaved changes.
- Menu bar (File, Edit, Object, View, Window) with the usual ⌘ shortcuts. It's the real macOS menu bar on a Mac, drawn in the window elsewhere.
- Cursors drawn for each tool: the Pen's nib (with what a click will do), crosshairs for the Shape, Measure and Fill Angle tools, Direct Selection's hollow arrow, and a curved arrow at rotate corners.
- Right-click menus on the canvas and the Objects list, styled like the tool flyouts: the stitch types with their icons, then the usual commands with their shortcuts.
- Stays quick on big designs: stitches are planned once per change, reusing every object that didn't change (overlap removal too: moving one object redoes only the objects it overlaps); a design that takes more than 12 ms to plan plans in the background (the status bar says "Updating stitches…" and the last plan stays on screen meanwhile; when you drop a moved object its stitches move with it until the new plan lands). Dragging points or objects, and typing, preview without re-planning until you let go. The stitches live on the GPU: each color block's segments are uploaded once and drawn by a shader (thread shading and needle points included), so panning, zooming, dragging and the stitch player cost nothing per stitch on the CPU, whether the design has a thousand stitches or half a million. The Objects list lays out only the rows in view. An object whose stitches hit an internal error is left out with a warning in Issues rather than taking the plan down. For development: TL_PROFILE=1 prints each frame's sections to stderr, TL_NO_GPU=1 draws the stitches with the painter instead, and cargo test --release --features gui --bin zigzag-studio perf_bench -- --ignored --nocapture --test-threads=1 times typing and dragging on a traced logo (examples/bench-grid.svg is a 213,000-stitch design for the studio itself).
- Follows the system's light or dark appearance (ZIGZAG_THEME=light|dark overrides) and uses the San Francisco system font on a Mac.
- Pick a stitch type per object: big buttons, the task bar, right-click, or F/S/R/C/X (Blanket and Manual from the Stitch panel). The preview re-plans live.
- Layout, the Illustrator way: drag to move (Shift: 45° steps; Option: drag a copy). Handles resize freely (Shift keeps proportions, Option resizes from the center, drag past the far side to flip); just outside a corner rotates (Shift: 45°). Smart guides snap edges and centers to other objects and the hoop (View › Smart Guides, ⌘U). ⌘D is Transform Again: Option-drag a copy, then ⌘D for more at the same spacing. Hold Space to pan with any tool (a tap plays the stitch-out). The Transform panel has Illustrator's reference point: X/Y show the chosen point and W/H resize around it. Also rotate, flip, center in hoop, fit to hoop (artwork bigger than the hoop is scaled down to fit when you open or place it; Undo restores full size), ⌘C/⌘V, arrows nudge 0.5 mm (Shift 5 mm). Edits are saved as SVG transforms.
- Groups work like Illustrator: a click or drag takes the whole group; double-click goes into it (the rest dims and a breadcrumb shows where you are), double-click again to go deeper; Esc or double-click on empty canvas comes back out. ⌘-click picks one object without going in. Layers (Illustrator's "Layer 1", Inkscape layers, Figma frames) aren't groups. Copy, paste and duplicate keep groups.
- Type (T), as in VectorCraft and Illustrator: click where the text should go and type straight onto the canvas (Enter for a new line, Esc or a click elsewhere when done); click text, or between its letters, to edit it with the caret where you clicked (or double-click it with the Selection tool, which picks text anywhere in its box and shows it as a box, not its letters' points; delete every letter and finish, and the text is removed) (←/→, ↑/↓, Home/End and ⌘←/→ move it; Backspace and Delete remove either side; Shift with any of those, a drag, a double-click for a word, a triple-click or ⌘A for the lot selects text, which typing replaces and ⌘C/⌘X/⌘V copy, cut and paste); click a selected path to put text on it. The Lettering panel is a compact Character panel (hat text isn't long copy): font (with search), bold/italic, letter height, tracking, pair kerning (pick the pair, or Option+←/→ while typing kerns the pair at the caret), horizontal and vertical scale, case (as typed, UPPER, lower, Title), alignment and leading for two-line text, baseline (straight, arc over, arc under, or on a path with offset and flip), and the stitch type. The Type menu has Type on a Path, Change Case, Align and Create Outlines (⇧⌘O). Text is a shape like any other: Direct Selection, Round Corners, Simplify, Pathfinder and Clean Up all work on it (it quietly becomes plain paths at the first point-level edit, one undo step), and it saves as real SVG text until then.
- Pen (P): draw filled shapes or lines with corners and curves, or place manual stitches one by one. Snaps to the grid when it's showing, else to smart guides.
- Shapes (M, L, \): rectangles (with rounded corners), ellipses, polygons, stars and lines.
- Measure, mm grid, needle points, First/Last, Group colors.
- Open machine files (PES, PEC, DST, EXP, JEF) to view, resize and re-export them, and images to trace.
- Settings per type: fill angle (with a drag-to-set angle tool, A), spacing, length, pull, underlay. Satin options: column vs border, stitch (satin, S-stitch, zig-zag), threads across the column, width, underlay passes, short stitches. Contour and ripple: rings or spiral. Cross-stitch: cell size. Tartan: the stripes (color and width each, mirrored), angle, row spacing. Run options: single or triple.
- Sewing row in the Stitch panel: Start and End (click the outline; green and red markers you can drag), Trim and Stop after the object. Edit › Copy / Paste Stitch Settings carries one object's type and settings to others (each keeps its thread and start/end); Edit › Select Same Stitch Type; Object › Path › Reverse Direction sews a path from its other end.
- Test swatches (Object › Test Swatches…): copies of the selection in a grid, one setting (spacing, length, pull compensation, satin width, fill angle) stepped from a value to another, each lettered with its value, to stitch out and compare on the real fabric.
- View › Density Map tints where stitches pile up (yellow from 2.5 per mm², red at 4), and View › Sewing Order numbers the objects on the canvas.
- Fabric preset, hoop, Remove overlaps and Cap order in the Document panel. Preview on your hat's color.
- Issues list (status bar): problems link to the object. Satins show their rails on the canvas.
- Sewing order: Earlier/Later. Stitch player (Space). Undo/redo everything.
- Save (Ctrl/⌘ S) writes settings into the SVG; Export PES/DST/EXP/JEF/VP3/PEC/PEN or a worksheet (HTML: picture, size, time, every color stop with the nearest Brother and Janome thread); Send to Skitch.
- Measuring and units. Measure (I) works like VectorCraft's: drag between two points to read the distance, angle, width and height. The ends snap to points, edges and the hoop (or to the grid when it's on), Shift keeps the line to 45°, and the reading stays until the next drag (Escape clears it). View › Units, or the unit menu in the status bar, shows sizes, positions, distances and the grid in millimeters, centimeters, inches, points or pixels (SVG pixels, 1/96 in, as files are read); it's remembered between launches. Any length field also takes a typed unit or a sum (1/4 in, 25.4mm, 10+5). The grid (⌘') follows the unit, inches in halves down to 1/32, and labels its major lines along the canvas's top and left edges from the hoop's center. Stitch settings (spacing, density, pull compensation) stay in millimeters.
- Picks up where you left off. Quit (or crash) and reopen: the same files come back, every tab with any unsaved changes, the zoom and the selection, and the same tab in front. Changes are kept as you work (session.json and one recovery file per tab, recovery.svg, recovery-2.svg…, in ZigZag's settings folder (~/Library/Application Support/ZigZag), each written whole or not at all), never written into your file until you save. Opening the app with a file skips this. If the app went down while bringing a document back, the next launch keeps it aside as recovery-crashed.svg instead of trying again (and an unreadable one as recovery-unreadable.svg), and says so in the status bar. A job on the Skitch comes back too: the design last sent is kept (sent.json), the Skitch window reopens if it was open and reconnects, and once the machine reports that design its progress shows on it again (the machine keeps the design and where the needle is; Stop ends the job on both).
The studio runs an MCP server on http://127.0.0.1:7878/mcp (Streamable HTTP,
localhost only, Origin-checked). Agents edit the live document you're looking at
(the tab in front), and every call is one undo step.
MCP in the status bar (bottom right, next to the Skitch) shows whether agents can reach the studio: a gray dot while it waits, green once a client has connected, blue and pulsing while calls come in, red if the server couldn't start. Click it for the address, the commands to connect Claude Code and Claude Desktop (with Copy buttons), the clients that have connected, the recent tool calls (failed ones in red, with the error on hover) and the tools on offer.

The Good Night button

The existing Home Assistant integration
Coway’s iPhone app is an absolutely classic IoT affair. It takes forever to load, and it asks me to change my password nearly every time I launch it. Thankfully, a brave soul reverse-engineered the app and wrote an integration for Home Assistant. The integration more or less pretends to be the phone app. It polls the Coway cloud service for status updates and sends commands through it to control the fan. Home Assistant bridges that to Apple Home and… voilà!
Why ditch the cloud?
The downside of this approach is that you’re still at the mercy (or lack thereof) of the Coway cloud service. It seems like at least once or twice a year, it breaks.
Here are some of the reasons I have seen it break:
- Coway didn’t like the integration hammering their servers and banned it. After a protracted argument with the community, they eventually settled on allowing it.
- Coway rewrote their phone app and rebranded it from IoCare to IoCare+, so the integration had to be rewritten as well.
- Coway made minor changes to the layout of the HTML that the integration scrapes, which broke it.
- Coway’s servers were periodically offline, which prevented any remote control of the device.
- Coway banned my account at one point for running an old version of the integration. I couldn’t control the fan remotely for weeks while I waited for their support team to reverse the ban.
On top of this, it seems unlikely that Coway will maintain this service forever. I suspect that eventually, like all IoT manufacturers, they’ll either charge for this service or discontinue it. My fan model is already discontinued (though there are other Wi-Fi enabled models, so… who knows).
A way in
I admit, I have been lazy. For years, I put off trying to figure out a way to ditch the Coway cloud, because if the device was really locked down, it would be pretty tough. Presumably, when the fan starts up, it connects to coway.com (or whatever) and checks that the server’s SSL certificate is valid. Most likely, I wouldn’t be able to make a fake certificate that passes that check, so the fan wouldn’t connect to my server.
Also, setting up a man-in-the-middle (MitM) environment is just a pain in the neck. The fan would have to connect to an isolated Wi-Fi network that routes all its traffic through my own server on the way out to the internet. To do that, you have to configure a bunch of routing and firewall rules to redirect traffic. All that said, two major things have changed.
First, there was a recent security incident (October 2026) where someone discovered that eMudhra, one of the trusted internet certificate authorities, had used a very weak parameter for some of its private keys.

Admittedly, that’s a lot of ifs, but assuming the device doesn’t have any major security mistakes in its design, this could be the foothold needed to take control of it.
The second thing is simply AI. An LLM can set up everything you need for a MitM environment, so you can get straight to sleuthing out how the fan works. It can do this unattended, in minutes.

After all that, we can see the provisioning works like this:
- Read the fan’s identity over Bluetooth from characteristic FFA0 (a “characteristic” is a Bluetooth endpoint you can read or write). This gives you some basic information about the device, including its MAC address.
- Write an encryption key (20 random lowercase ASCII letters) to characteristic FFA2:"P001" + protobuf {
command: 1,
key: Base64(random_letters)
}
- Construct the AES key by concatenating the key from step 2 with the 12 uppercase ASCII characters of the MAC address.
- Build the configuration payload, which looks like this:{
"cmd": 2,
"ssid": "Example Wi-Fi",
"pwd": "example-wifi-password",
"bssid": "AABBCCDDEEFF",
"type": 1,
"macAddr": "112233445566",
"caUrl": "http://192.0.2.1:8080/ca.pem",
"certSvrUrl": "https://192.0.2.1:9443/iot/v1/cert/r0",
"certToken": "example-enrollment-token",
"serial": "EXAMPLE000000000001"
}
That gets wrapped in protobuf, padded with PKCS#7, and encrypted using the AES key from step 3. Then it gets prefixed with the string “P002” and sent to FFA2 as well.
Needless to say, figuring this out end to end from the decompiled Android app would have been very time consuming to do by hand. It’d be at least hours of work (likely days). AI really comes in clutch here. It took the AI about 20 minutes.
The good part, though, is that all the data you need to decrypt that payload is in the Bluetooth logs we captured from the phone, so we can check that our reimplementation is correct just from those. The first tells the fan where to download the root CA cert over plain HTTP, and the second tells it where to POST over HTTPS to get its MQTT configuration.
Since we’re the ones sending this payload now, we can point those URLs anywhere we want. Most importantly, that means we don’t have to do any MitM anymore. We just have to run our own server and plug in its URLs when we set up the fan.

Since 2021, Project Zero has followed a “90 + 30” vulnerability disclosure policy, allowing vendors up to 90 days to remediate a vulnerability plus an extra 30 days “soak time” for patch rollout. Our goal was to encourage faster patching and give vendors time to deliver patches to users. The advent of AI-assisted vulnerability discovery and exploitation has changed the threat landscape such that the vulnerability remediation timeline encouraged by our disclosure policy is clearly insufficient.
LLMs have increased the speed at which attackers can find and exploit vulnerabilities, alongside the risk that multiple parties, including malicious ones, discover the same bug. A vulnerability reported today presents a much higher immediate risk to users than one reported last year. In this new environment, it is crucial that vendors move towards remediating vulnerabilities faster.
LLMs have also decreased the time it takes for attackers to reverse engineer and exploit a vulnerability based on a patch, to the extent that zero patch-to-exploitation time is a reasonable, though imperfect, model of attacker behavior. Patch soak time is no longer meaningful in a world where attackers can almost immediately determine bug details from a patch, and can put users at risk by creating a false sense of secrecy. “Patch gap” is the time between a fix being released for a security vulnerability and the user installing the relevant update. It is equally crucial that vendors reduce patch gap, either by reducing patch rollout time or using other strategies to protect users while remediation is in progress.
LLMs have also increased the volume of vulnerabilities that Project Zero reports. We are finding several vulnerabilities a week in targets where we once found a few vulnerabilities a year. Aspects of our policy that require vendor negotiation or agreement, including determining whether a bug is fixed and whether grace period applies, are quickly becoming unmanageable.
Today, we are announcing a new vulnerability disclosure policy, created with the following goals in mind:
- Encourage faster vulnerability remediation
- Encourage vendors to reduce “patch gap”
- Allow for the policy to be automated by both Project Zero and vendors
Our new policy: “Full X”
Vulnerabilities will be disclosed X days after they are reported to the vendor.
Starting today, our policy is “Full 90”, i.e. vulnerabilities will be disclosed 90 days after they are reported to the vendor.
On February 2, 2027, we will move to a “Full 60” policy where vulnerabilities are disclosed after 60 days.
On January 3, 2028, we will move to a “Full 30” policy where vulnerabilities are disclosed after 30 days.
We are also removing most ‘special cases’ from our disclosure policy, including grace period and variant policy. The only exceptions to our new policy are:
- Vulnerabilities actively exploited in the wild -- ITW vulnerabilities will be handled according to Google’s application security vulnerability disclosure policy
- Public vulnerabilities -- Project Zero may make details of a vulnerability public, at our discretion, if the details have already been made public by others
- Vendor request -- We will make vulnerability details public if the vendor we reported it to requests it
Why reduce remediation time gradually?
Project Zero solicited feedback from the vendors we report the most vulnerabilities to as a part of developing this new policy. An overarching concern was that immediately changing the policy could harm users by making unfixed vulnerabilities available to attackers while vendors are in the process of improving their remediation processes and technology. While the benefits of delaying disclosure are declining due to the increased probability that attackers will discover a vulnerability during the embargo period, we still feel there are benefits in the short term.
In addition, vendors mentioned two more subtle concerns. One was that an overly-aggressive disclosure policy would encourage fixing bugs reported under the new policy with more priority, even if they are less severe than other reported vulnerabilities. The other was that immediately enforcing a policy that some vendors aren’t equipped to meet might lead to a state where vendors accept that they cannot fix bugs within the deadline and don’t attempt to.
We decided on a policy that encourages vendors to improve remediation time on a schedule that is challenging but realistic, with the hope that this will lead to vendors broadly improving their remediation processes and infrastructure in a way that eventually enables them to fix all reported vulnerabilities quickly.
What about severity?
While our analysis isn’t always perfect, Project Zero only reports vulnerabilities with substantial severity and demonstrated security impact in our tracker. That said, we don’t feel severity is an effective metric for making disclosure decisions, especially in the current environment. A vulnerability’s severity does not affect the likelihood that it will be discovered by attackers, only its impact on users.
When vendors determine severity and use it to prioritize bug fixes, they are making decisions about what risks they are willing to expose their users to, and it is reasonable to accept the risk of low-severity bugs, like unexploitable null pointer crashes or minor info leaks in a lot of circumstances. But this doesn’t change the availability of these vulnerabilities to attackers, and we want our policy to reflect this reality.
It’s also important to acknowledge that Project Zero holds itself to high standards with regards to the reproducibility, readability, reachability and reported security impact of the vulnerabilities we report. This policy assumes bugs reports are of this quality, and it is reasonable for vendors to treat low quality-bug reports differently.
What about hardware?
We encourage all vendors to consider how they would respond to imminent disclosure of a serious vulnerability, including non-patch strategies, as there is no guarantee that defenders will be the first to find a bug. That said, Project Zero has occasionally granted exceptions to our policy on rare occasions, including Spectre and Meltdown, and will continue to do so in the future.
Are you really not going to check whether bugs are fixed?
The question of whether a bug is fixed for the purposes of our policy has always been challenging and somewhat unresolved, particularly with regards to code that is not shipped directly to users and not publicly available. It is not unusual for Project Zero to communicate with vendors multiple times for each bug report to come to an agreement on whether a vulnerability is fixed. As more vendors experiment with vulnerability remediation strategies that are not patching like temporarily disabling code paths, we expect this to become even more complex.
We still plan to track whether bugs are fixed and communicate if they are not, however, this will not be a decision point in our policy. This will enable us to share technical details of how users are protected without needing to make a binary judgement with consequences for the vendor.
6. Why Zig over C for Systems Programming
FULL TEXT
143 points · 91 comments · torutofu

Article
After I wrote Why Zig Over Rust for Systems Programming, the obvious next question was C. The Rust question was about safety and how much ceremony I was willing to accept for it. The C question is different, because Zig and C want the same things. Both are small languages without a Garbage Collector and both stay close to what the machine does. So this post is not about leaving C for something completely different. It is about the parts of C that cost me time and how Zig deals with them. All the code was compiled on a Mac with Zig 0.17 and clang.
What C gets right Link to heading
C is the language of UNIX. The kernels, the C libraries and most of the system libraries that every other language calls in the end are written in C and the man pages describe them with C prototypes and C examples. A C program from the 1990s still compiles today and there is a C compiler for almost every CPU that was ever made. The language is small enough that one book, The C Programming Language by Kernighan and Ritchie, covers all of it. Zig itself does not try to replace any of that. It talks to C libraries directly and it uses the C ABI when it needs to. I am not writing this post because I think C is bad; C is my favorite programming language! I am writing it because I do not want to write new code in C any more.
Cleanup without goto Link to heading
The C code that I like the least is a function that acquires several resources and has to release them in reverse order when something fails halfway. The usual answer, which is also the one the Linux kernel uses, is goto:
struct user *user_new(const char *name, size_t n_groups)
{
struct user *u = malloc(sizeof *u);
if (u == NULL)
goto fail;
u->name = strdup(name);
if (u->name == NULL)
goto fail_user;
u->groups = calloc(n_groups, sizeof *u->groups);
if (u->groups == NULL)
goto fail_name;
return u;
fail_name:
free(u->name);
fail_user:
free(u);
fail:
return NULL;
}
It works, but the cleanup is far away from the allocation and the order of the labels has to match the order of the allocations by hand. The same function in Zig looks like this:
fn newUser(allocator: std.mem.Allocator, name: []const u8, n_groups: usize) !*User {
const u = try allocator.create(User);
errdefer allocator.destroy(u);
u.name = try allocator.dupe(u8, name);
errdefer allocator.free(u.name);
u.groups = try allocator.alloc(u32, n_groups);
return u;
}
Each errdefer sits right below the allocation it undoes and runs only if the function returns an error. Zig runs them in reverse order, so there are no labels to keep in order. What I like even more is that I can test every failure path. The std.testing.FailingAllocator type fails on the first, second or third allocation, depending on the value of fail_index. The testing allocator below it reports any memory that is not freed:
test "newUser does not leak when an allocation fails" {
for (0..3) |i| {
var failing = std.testing.FailingAllocator.init(std.testing.allocator, .{ .fail_index = i });
try std.testing.expectError(error.OutOfMemory, newUser(failing.allocator(), "mihalis", 4));
}
}
$ zig test user.zig
1/1 user.test.newUser does not leak when an allocation fails...OK
All 1 tests passed.
Testing the same three paths in the C version needs a wrapper around malloc() or an external tool.
A slice knows its length Link to heading
In C an array that goes to a function becomes a pointer and the length travels separately, if at all. The following C function has an off-by-one error, <= instead of <:
int sum(const int *a, size_t n)
{
int total = 0;
for (size_t i = 0; i <= n; i++)
total += a[i];
return total;
}
With an array of {1, 2, 3} it prints sum = 7 on my Mac and exits with 0. The extra 1 came from whatever was in memory after the array. Neither clang with -Wall -Wextra nor the program itself complains. In Zig a slice carries its length, so the same mistake stops the program in the debug and safe modes:
fn sum(a: []const i32) i32 {
var total: i32 = 0;
for (0..a.len + 1) |i| total += a[i];
return total;
}
$ zig run sum.zig
thread 1278459 panic: index out of bounds: index 3, len 3
sum.zig:5:38: 0x1023c5b4b in sum (sum)
for (0..a.len + 1) |i| total += a[i];
^
In real Zig code you would write for (a) |x| and the mistake cannot happen at all. Strings work the same way. A Zig string is a slice of bytes with a length, so there is no strlen() call that walks the memory looking for a 0 byte and no buffer that is one byte short because somebody forgot the terminator.
Errors you cannot ignore Link to heading
In C a function reports an error by returning -1 or NULL and setting errno, or by returning an error code, or in some other way that the man page describes. Nothing forces you to check. A lot of C code calls close() and fclose() without looking at the return value and the compiler is fine with it. In Zig an error is part of the return type and the compiler refuses to compile a call whose error you ignore. You have to use try, catch or if. The failure path is in the source code, right next to the call, which is also what I liked about Go.
comptime instead of the preprocessor Link to heading
The C preprocessor works with text and knows nothing about C. The classic #define MAX(a, b) ((a) > (b) ? (a) : (b)) evaluates one of its arguments twice and a big C project usually has a lot of #ifdef lines for every Operating System it supports. In Zig, code that runs at compile time is ordinary Zig code. Instead of #ifdef __linux__ I write if (builtin.os.tag == .linux) and the compiler drops the branch that does not apply. Generic data structures are functions that take a type and return a type. The compiler checks all of it like the rest of the program. I wrote about this in Zig comptime.
Fewer undefined surprises Link to heading
In C the overflow of a signed integer is undefined behavior. The following C program adds 1 to INT_MAX:
int add(int a, int b)
{
return a + b;
}
int main(void)
{
printf("%d\n", add(INT_MAX, 1));
return 0;
}
Compiled with clang it prints -2147483648 and exits with 0, but the C standard allows the compiler to do anything, including removing code that it decides can never run. In Zig the overflow of any integer, signed or unsigned, is checked in the debug and safe modes and the program stops with panic: integer overflow and the line where it happened. When I want wrapping, I write +% and when I want the result to stop at the maximum value, I write +|.
Zig is also a C compiler Link to heading
The zig cc command is a C compiler that you can use instead of gcc or clang. It also cross compiles out of the box. On my Mac, zig cc -target x86_64-linux-musl -o sum sum.c creates a static Linux executable without installing anything else. In the debug mode zig cc also turns on the undefined behavior sanitizer, so the add() program above compiled with zig cc stops with panic: signed integer overflow: 2147483647 + 1 cannot be represented in type 'int'. A build.zig file can compile C files next to Zig files, so you can move a C project to Zig one file at a time and keep using the same build. If your Zig code needs C header files, Zig 0.17 removed @cImport() and you use zig translate-c instead. I wrote about that in @cImport() Is gone in Zig 0.17.
The honest tradeoff Link to heading
Zig is not C with all the problems solved. It does not stop you from using memory after you free it or from keeping a pointer to a stack variable that no longer exists. In the fast and small modes most safety checks are turned off and you are back to undefined behavior, just as in C. C is also stable in a way that Zig is not yet. When Zig 0.17 came out, I had to change 35 of the 152 Zig files of my book, while C code from decades ago still compiles. C has more tools, more books, more people who know it and compilers for chips that Zig does not support. If you write code for a microcontroller that only has a vendor C compiler, the choice is made for you.
Conclusion Link to heading
C is not going away and I still read a lot of it, because the UNIX source code and most system libraries are written in C and their man pages document them with C prototypes. But for new code I choose Zig. It lets me write the same kind of code as C, close to the machine and with no runtime, while the compiler and the debug mode catch many of the mistakes that C lets through without a word.
Happy coding in Zig!

FULL TEXT
1 points · 0 comments · jonasl

New #2
Original article · HN discussion
Article
A WordPress plugin author fixes a security bug, ships a new version, and writes “General bug fixes” in the changelog. There is no advisory, no CVE, and no warning. If you update, you’re safe. If you don’t, your site stays vulnerable, and every scanner that depends on a vulnerability database has nothing to warn you with.
This happens far more often than you might think. Well over 90% of WordPress vulnerabilities live in plugins and themes rather than in WordPress core, and our own measurements show that a large share of plugin security fixes are never clearly announced. So how do you protect a site against a vulnerability that nobody has written down?
Since the end of September 2026, our team at WPSec has been reading every new plugin release ourselves, looking for security fixes instead of waiting for someone to announce them, and publishing what we find as free, open advisories. In this article, we will examine why the usual warning system leaves a gap, how our new process closes it, what it has found in its first weeks, how you can use the data yourself, and where its limits are.
Related: Your WordPress Vulnerability Scan Came Back Clean. Are You Still Exposed?
The problem: the fix is public, the warning is not
Most WordPress vulnerability scanners, ours included until recently, work the same way:
- A researcher finds a bug and reports it.
- The vendor ships a fix.
- A vulnerability database publishes an advisory.
- Scanners pick up the advisory and start flagging sites that run the old version.
Think of it like a car manufacturer that quietly fixes faulty brakes in this year’s model but never sends out a recall notice. New cars are fine. Everyone driving last year’s model has no idea there was ever a problem. That chain has two weak links.
The advisory comes after the fix
We measured this against a random sample of published advisories. The fixed version was released a median of 6.2 days before the advisory appeared. For 44% of them, the gap was more than a week, and for one in seven it was more than a month.
During that window, the fix is public, so anyone can compare the two versions and see exactly what was broken. Attackers do exactly that. Scanners stay quiet, because no database has said anything yet.
Sometimes the advisory never comes
Vendors fix security bugs without saying so for many reasons. They found the bug themselves, a code-quality review flagged it, they want to avoid bad press, or they simply don’t see it as a security issue. In the same sample:
- 73% of known fixes were announced as security fixes in the changelog;
- 9% used only vague wording such as “improved validation”; and
- 17% gave no hint at all. The code change was the only evidence.
When that last group never gets an advisory, no database will ever list it. The vulnerable versions stay in use on real sites, unflagged.
Our approach: read every release ourselves
The fix is in the code, so the code is where we look. Every hour, we pick up new releases of the WordPress.org plugins that the sites we scan actually use, and for each one:
- AI reads the change. It reads the release notes and compares the new code with the previous version, looking for a hole that an attacker could reach in the old one.
- AI reviews the finding. Anything flagged is checked again by independent models, which read the code of both releases and draft an advisory.
- A person decides. A human reviews every finding daily and confirms, corrects, or rejects it. Nothing a model says is acted on by itself.
- We protect and publish. WPSec scans flag affected sites from the moment a finding is confirmed, and it is published as a free public advisory at wpsec.com/vuln/.
Several models, one human
No single AI model is good at everything, and different models make different mistakes. So we use several, each where it does best:
- Screening: a fast, inexpensive model reads every analysed release. It finds known fixes almost as well as the largest models, at a small fraction of the cost. It also flags more harmless “hardening”, which is why the next step exists.
- Confirmation and drafting: a frontier model re-reads only what the screening flagged, filters out many of the false alarms, and writes the draft advisory.
- Independent code review: the same frontier model, working as an agent with read-only access to both release packages. It can follow a request from where it enters the plugin to where the damage happens, then write an evidence report and a recommendation.
- Second opinion: a model from another AI company, with its own tools and no view of the first reviewer’s reasoning, so the two are unlikely to make the same mistake. It answers four questions: is the flaw reachable, is the attacker described correctly, is the fix complete, and is it a duplicate?
- Same flaw or not? When another database later publishes an advisory for the same release, a model checks whether it describes the same flaw as ours.
- The decision: a person, for every finding, every day.
Before building any of this, we tested it on known cases. Given only the code change, the frontier model found 24 of 26 known security fixes, including 8 of the 10 fixes with no hint in the changelog, and the fast screening model found 23. Of 14 ordinary releases with no known advisory, the frontier model flagged one. When we read that one by hand, it turned out to be a real, unannounced fix.
The two AI review steps also tell us when a human should look harder. The reviewer’s recommendation has matched the human’s final decision for 96% of findings. When the reviewer recommended confirming and the second model agreed, the finding was confirmed more than 99% of the time. Disagreements between the two models are where human attention pays off most.
What we have found so far
These figures cover 29 September to 12 November 2026, plus a two-week look back at releases from before we started:
- New plugin releases picked up: about 910 a day.
- Releases of plugins that none of the sites we scan use: 85%, which we skip.
- Releases analysed in depth: about 6,500, including the look back.
- Releases confirmed to fix at least one vulnerability: 1,781.
- Vulnerabilities confirmed: 3,225.
- Public advisories published so far: 3,011.
- Median time from release to human confirmation: 20 hours.
About three in ten candidate findings never made it: a person rejected it, or our own rules filtered it out automatically.
Four in five hit real sites
For 82% of the confirmed findings, at least one site we scan was running a vulnerable version at the moment we confirmed it. These are not theoretical vulnerabilities in plugins nobody uses.
One in five confirmed fixes was silent
For 21% of the releases we confirmed, the release notes and commit messages contained nothing pointing to a security fix. Only the code showed it.
Nearly half are still unknown elsewhere
Of the findings whose fix shipped more than two weeks ago, 47% still have no advisory for that release in any of the public WordPress vulnerability databases we also import. For those, as far as we can tell, we are the only source.
When others did publish, we were usually first
For 49% of our findings, another database has also published an advisory for the same fixed release. In 74% of those cases, our scans already flagged the release before that advisory appeared, a median of 2.3 days earlier. Our measuring window is still short, so slower advisories are not in these figures yet, and the earlier measurement (a median of 6.2 days from fix to advisory) suggests the real gap is larger.
These are not obscure plugins
19% of the confirmed findings are in plugins with 100,000 or more active installs, and several are in plugins with millions, including Elementor, Contact Form 7, WooCommerce, WPForms, Rank Math, and All in One SEO.

What the models get wrong
AI is good at spotting that something security-related changed. It is less reliable about how bad it was and who could exploit it. That is why every finding goes through review. The corrections we make most often are these:
- The attacker is overstated. The most common correction is a draft saying “unauthenticated” when the vulnerable code can only be reached after logging in, or when the input is already cleaned before it is stored.
- Hardening is written up as a vulnerability. Plenty of releases add an extra check where the old code was already safe. Those are good changes, but they are not vulnerabilities and should not set off alarms on your site.
- The affected range is too wide. A code change shows where a bug ended, not where it began. For a third of the confirmed vulnerabilities, reviewers had to find the first affected version by reading older releases. Without that correction, every older version would have been flagged as well.
- The reviewer can be wrong too. Once, the code-reading agent misread the old code. A human reading the key lines caught it, so high-impact findings now always get that extra read.
Published databases make mistakes as well. In one case, a CVE that another database had published was later withdrawn as a false positive by the organisation that issued it. Our reviewers had already rejected the same change as hardening. Reading the code ourselves is how we can tell the difference.
From finding to protection
Every finding is a vulnerability the vendor has already fixed and released, so the advice is always the same: update to the fixed version. We never publish proof-of-concept code. When a release turns out to fix a flaw only partly, our scans keep flagging every version, but we do not publish an advisory while the hole is still open.
WPSec scans flag a confirmed finding immediately. The public advisories follow shortly after the fixed release, and each one stays published. When another database later publishes the same flaw under a CVE, we link the two.
Giving back: open advisories for everyone
WordPress is built by a community, and so is its security. Researchers, plugin authors, and other vulnerability databases have shared their work for years, and our own scans have benefited from it. Publishing what we find is our way of giving back to that community.
So the advisories are not locked inside our product. Every one is published at wpsec.com/vuln/ under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence (CC BY 4.0). You may copy, share, and adapt them for any purpose, including in commercial products such as other scanners and vulnerability databases, as long as you credit WPSec and link back to the advisory (or to wpsec.com/vuln/ for the whole dataset). The about page explains the exact attribution and every data field.
We have also made the data easy to use, for people and for software alike:
- JSON: all advisories in one file, updated hourly, plus a JSON file for each individual advisory.
- RSS: a feed of the 50 most recent advisories, so you can follow new findings in any feed reader.

To protect your own site in the meantime, follow these steps:
- Keep plugins updated, ideally with automatic updates for plugins you trust. A silent fix only protects you once it’s installed.
- Remove plugins you don’t use. A deactivated plugin’s files can still be reachable, and every plugin you remove is one less thing to patch.
- Don’t rely on the changelog. “Bug fixes and improvements” can mean a security fix. Treat every update as potentially important.
- Scan regularly, so that when a fix you haven’t installed turns out to matter, you hear about it.
Limits
No method catches everything, and we would rather you know where ours stops:
- Commercial plugins. We can only read code that is public. Premium plugins sold outside WordPress.org are not covered this way, and for them we still rely on public databases.
- Only plugins in use. We analyse releases of plugins that the sites we scan actually run. That is where an advisory protects someone today.
- Young data. The process has been running for weeks, not years. The lead-time figures in particular will change as slower advisories arrive.
Why it matters
When a fix is released, the vulnerable code is public, and the patch shows exactly where it was. Attackers who compare releases do not need an advisory. Defenders who wait for one are always behind, and for silent fixes they wait forever. Reading the releases ourselves closes most of that gap, and the public advisories let everyone else benefit too.
So, is your site safe once the changelog says “bug fixes”? Only if you’ve installed them, and only if someone actually read the code.
