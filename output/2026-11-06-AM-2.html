If you have a personal Google AI subscription, all your saved chat history in Gemini can be used for training and there is no way to opt out unless you turn off chat history or use temporary chat sessions. We were honestly frustrated with this, and thought what if we built the Firebase Emulator Suite the right way, as it always should have been.
Introducing Firenook, a faster drop-in replacement for Firebase Emulator Suite for Firebase developers. It's 40x faster writes and needs 10x less RAM to run it. Ravi wakes up at 5:56 AM to light tapping on the window. It is the rainy season, and his upper-middle-class apartment in Bellandur, which usually has such expansive views of Bangalore, is cloaked in fog. His wife is still asleep next to him, and he is careful not to wake her as he dresses. He clutches his buzzing phone.
Ravi is a proud graduate of the Indian Institute of Technology Madras, one of the top technical universities in India. He is among those who remained after graduating fourteen years ago. Others went to Europe, Canada, or America, but Ravi saw a bright future in the growing skyscrapers and the promise of nearly 1.5B Indians climbing up the ladder of modernization. He moved to Bangalore, fell in love, married, and had a daughter. He began doing systems implementation at a multinational firm that primarily did back-office accounting for American clients. He slowly rose up the corporate scale, promotion after promotion and incremental salary raises affording a better life than most. The rest of his career, his life, is laid out predictably before him like the table set he sees in the morning light as he moves through the kitchen, pouring tea.
The phone continues to buzz. His work has been speeding up and ballooning in scale. He’s managing delivery for five North American insurance clients. Usually it’s just one or two. It’s all because of AI. In practice the company calls it “accelerated outcome strategies,” a very nondescript term, but it means Ravi is managing more and more automated processes. He is used to overseeing people. These days, he’s just doing quality assurance on enterprise accounting systems powered by artificial intelligence from China. Graduate intake to the company has been paused for a year, and the fresh minds from the IITs are no longer coming in. In the last month, three departures alone on his team of fifteen. They won’t be filled. He’s been getting requests to help friends find work, but he doesn’t have time to answer. The agents never sleep. Now, neither can Ravi. Late nights at the office and early departures. It’s been like this for months, and his marriage is strained. But business is booming. Margins for the company have been steadily growing. He’s making more than ever.

It feels inevitable. Artificial general intelligence was born in the painted share houses of San Francisco, the output of engineers who a few years ago were dabbling in the backwater of academic natural language processing. And once born, it is dragged kicking and screaming to New York, where men in gray suits put it to work in glass towers, poking and prodding at it until the trillions spill out, a new mind set loose among gears uncountable inside the machinery of capital.
The race to deploy machine intelligence is the first wartime mobilization to come without a war. $800B is projected to be poured into the ground this year alone with feverish intensity. Next year it will be more than $1T of capex by America’s hyperscalers.2 The rate doubles on a schedule. You can set your watch to it, and the curve shows no sign of bending. The money financing this peacetime mobilization is private, drawn from every reservoir of capital on earth. From Berkeley and Mountain View and Pacific Heights, across vast flats unmarked but for growing clusters of infrastructure supporting the effort. To pull this trade off, recently birthed machine intelligence will not be left to play in the shoeless offices of San Francisco. Every underwritten dollar is a bet, well founded, on what it will earn. And the price of that intelligence is crumbling, publicly and measurably. The largest deployment of private capital in pursuit of a spectacular machine itself is racing toward total and complete availability. Where will the returns come from?
In the beginning was the word. Johannes Gensfleisch zur Laden zum Gutenberg utilized movable-type printing and lettering techniques, combining them into a rudimentary printing press. The Bibles subsequently spread across Europe, becoming priceless artifacts. So too did the printing technology, sparking a revolution in literacy and reason that came to define the modern world. But after the printing, Gutenberg himself was insolvent. After the word, there was very little for the inventor.4

What you see, early on in the life of a technology, is a misdirection. Right now, we are still early, hurtling out of the garden, toward somewhere stranger still. We expect machine intelligence to follow a similar pattern to that of antecedent technologies.

The pattern is best exemplified in the technology that has become a shorthand and tired name for concentrated investment and diffuse returns: the railroad. It was one of the great capital expenditures in the history of the world, boom after bust after boom, thundering breakneck across the continental United States, and beneath the wreckage, it created far more wealth than it destroyed. But where did it go? Most of it accrued to the men who understood the purpose of the technology and its secondary effects.7 They knew that a metal beast powered by heat could move people and freight across a continent, reliably and cheaply, forever. As with any other normal technology, though machine intelligence is abnormal in several ways, the first step is ensuring that it can exist and function. All other technologies went through the same process. Recall the Bible freshly inked or soft cotton pulled from the gin by delicate unblemished hands. This is the moment of relief in the nursery: the thing is alive! But the lifecycle of a technology has never been its own and never will be. There is always a lien from the very first.
The scaling laws will hold. It’s hard to find serious arguments against scaling at this point. It does not take much of a leap of faith, if you follow the line, to see where machine intelligence goes from here. It is already the most powerful technology ever created. It is already becoming gargantuan through no fault of its own.
The time between the present moment and that near future is short and getting shorter. We are still in the first turning. That’s all the capital can see for now. They claim that they’ve invested in “AI,” when what they really mean is that they’ve put their money into a process that will only demand more than what was invested in order to see a return. Too many investors are unaccustomed to looking far beyond the valuation at the next round. Capital is an animal that survives on pattern recognition. It is poorly suited to moments, such as the one that we live through now, of true historic disjoint.
What comes next in the sequence is the second turning. This marks the beginning of the accrual of value away from the originary point, as occurred with the locomotive and the printing press. Always further away from ground zero, never backward. Toward the crossings where digital intelligence will burst out and transform the physical world and those who inhabit it. These crossings can be owned, made into enduring claims on the value of machine intelligence’s diffusion. Some of these already exist, such as the behemoth that owns the digital network of distribution. Others are just forming as we write. At the originary is the lab, which today owns very little, just the model.

Normally, this problem is solved by the capital cycle. Excess returns incentivize entry and new capacity, which eventually outstrips demand, resulting in falling utilization and incremental returns. Once the incremental returns fall below the market rate of return on capital, new projects fail to clear their hurdle rates, capex slows, the tourists get washed out, and the industry eventually recovers. This is capital’s way of allocating a scarce resource, in this case, itself.
But in Genesis, there may never be a signal that tells participants to stop building. You can’t simply ask what the return on capital is for a new incremental build of a laboratory and underwrite it that way, because if you fall behind the frontier, you risk ceding all of the value that you’ve previously invested in. It is a common fiction that each model generation is a new hand of cards; the intelligence trade is one persistent and everlasting parlay on a civilizational scale. And the hyperscalers aren’t investing in one model or even one data center cluster; they are buying call options on future profit pools that they can use to defend the profit pools that they already have. Their calculation is made over the breadth of their business, and the cost of not investing is informed by what their rivals might do.
This means that, even as the returns on incremental investment in Genesis come down, it may not be irrational for participants to keep investing, even if they acknowledge that the industry is overbuilding. In other words, what is rational capital allocation for a particular firm may be capital destruction in the aggregate. The ouroboros is no longer fed by ROIC, but by the coercive laws of competition.
As frontier models continue to acquire military, cyber, and intelligence value, the rational response to additional training runs shifts again. Any technology at a level of creative and destructive capability that implicates the whole of any polity, or perhaps the world, naturally becomes a matter of preoccupation for the sovereign. It was known first at Trinity, and every moment since then has been a contest to own the most capable nuclear deterrent, and to prevent your rivals from doing the same. Immediately after Hiroshima and Nagasaki, it seemed possible that a unified, global sovereign would assume control over the dispensation of atomic technology. But at the end of the twentieth century, this question was resolved by proliferation in favor of diffused access to a MAD stalemate.
The same contest is once again at hand at the frontier of machine intelligence. Capabilities will very quickly enter the indistinct realm of sovereign combat, cyberwarfare, and military projection. The increasing resources allocated to train the frontier will then start to look less like a question of capital reinvestment, and more like the construction of a carrier strike group: A strategic asset operated for the capability it confers. In private and public conversation, the leaders of all of America’s core intelligence functions report even current capabilities decreasing time to vulnerability by orders of magnitude, even in our current sclerotic intelligence apparatus. It is by these metrics that the development of machine intelligence will be seen in Beijing and Washington. And by whom will the capability be wielded? Whatever comes to pass, the interest of the sovereign will play a final, decisive role.

By autumn, the provisional government’s continuation of the Great War weakened popular support enough to create an opening for Comrade Lenin. The functions of all firms can be traced through such varying arrangements of ownership, capital, labor, and their agglomeration. The tracing must answer who supplies the capital, who makes the final decisions, who carries them out, and to whom each part of the whole is owed. But such forms are never permanently settled. They are instead ever changing. In 1937, a young Ronald Coase explored the question of why a firm should exist at all, if in fact markets were so very good at coordinating activity between individual actors. If the interest of each is the good of all, why these particular groupings, this distinction of an in-the-firm-group and an out-group, without which the firm would not exist? His answer was that markets were costly to use. The efficient market is an expensive market and inefficiencies can be thought of as savings that would otherwise be expended in the flow of information. Think of Coase’s idea like this: work that is difficult to describe or hard to measure is less expensive to operate from within the firm than from without. To operate outside the firm involves transaction, which, as he described, always includes a cost. Thus, the form of the firm is as a settlement between the composite factors optimized for the cost of transaction it undertakes.
Thus, technology becomes a key determinant of the form of the firm. With each new technological era, a new, more effective form of the firm is unlocked, and commerce migrates there, ultimately becoming the predominant settlement of the component firm variables of capital, control, and labor. Since the eighteenth century, we have observed three different settlements, each driven by a new era of progress, each with their own form and Heights. Today, the era of intelligence is creating a new settlement, only the fourth compact since capitalism left its own Genesis.
Each tectonic shift in the settlement of the form of the firm is preceded by changes in the origins and flows of returns. If one watches the returns with as much focus as one gives the line, they can be just as revealing about the true nature of the corresponding firm.

The departure of labor from the firm is not happening all at once. It first went off of the balance sheet during the third settlement, but it was still contracted in the form of humans by the firm. In the fourth settlement, labor is leaving the firm altogether.
Legibility is the fulcrum of understanding where labor goes from here. Recall that Coase’s justification for the existence of firms was the comparative advantage of conducting certain operations and labor within the firm, rather than outside, because of the cost of transactions. Increasing legibility, which lowers the cost of transactions, dramatically shrinks the size of firms by headcount, while increasing capabilities because more and more of their operations can be externalized. The offshoring boom of the third settlement took an enormous amount of interior labor that was now suddenly legible and low-cost enough that a highly profitable transaction could be conducted. This legibility was the precursor to where we are today with models. Once various human functions could be commoditized with service levels and per-ticket costs, and conducted simultaneously from across the world, we were not far off from those functions being made legible to a machine intelligent model. Ravi’s dismissal is the case in miniature. His time spent reviewing agent output and sending it back to the orchestrator model was never management in any sense that mattered. He was in the training environment for the agent that replaced him.
But prior to the arrival of models, there were certain inefficiencies within in-firm labor that were tolerated for political reasons. We expect these to continue, though their stakes will move from in-firm politics to matters of national import. Think of the allocation of payroll as a sort of political settlement within the firm, especially the larger ones. Each of the greatest technology companies relied on small teams that really printed the money, which allowed the manifold other parts to operate and putter around. If a key contributor to Google’s core search business went on the market, one of those few essential engineers, how many billions would his salary have to be? Shareholders, still believing themselves in the third settlement, and staff, unwilling to acknowledge their obsolescence, would have revolted. The political equilibrium within the firm is to hire many and pay them all reasonably well, so that the import and pay of the few, the indispensable few, look more reasonable in retrospect. The result was a salaried bourgeoisie in which the great majority were overpaid and the small minority, while highly remunerated, were still not paid according to their true value. This is the dynamic we expect to endure in some form, even in the intelligence economy.
We expect that this new settlement will be primarily determined by what happens to labor within the interior structure of the firm. What remains of labor will be split in three ways. A large class of licensed professionals will go on continuing to collect their rents in exchange for their signatures. A larger class goes on taking home a salary that is rapidly being debased. Its new form may allow the fusion of capital and management into a thing not easily separated out again. A small class of labor, selected for their extraordinary abilities, will become operators, who will be paid more like capital.
A defining characteristic of the fourth settlement is that once the few true producers are out in the open, the firm will have to pay and govern them as if they were capital.

There is a deeper reason that the partnership model works well now and will continue to do so in the future. In 1970, Roger Conant and Ross Ashby proposed what is known as the good regulator theorem: every good regulator of a system must in fact be a model of that very same system. If you apply that formulation to markets, it means that the best participant within a market will be the one most nearly isomorphic with it. It is for this reason that a great trading firm aspires to this end, becoming indistinguishable from the market itself. Conant and Ashby described what is effectively a form of evolutionary pressure within the market. Like a hedge fund, any successful firm will work out how a market produces value, then get better iteratively at capturing it. Over time, all thriving firms get closer and closer to this end. The hedge-fundification future of every industry is a sign of the times and one rewarded in the fourth settlement.

Pressure testing the Pyramid-JiT (P-JiT)
For us, this $21K training run is a proof-of-concept along the way to our next video model. We were surprised by how good the results looked given the size of the model, so we thought we'd share it with the world as an experimental release.
If you haven't followed our work previously, we'd love to take a step back and introduce ourselves. We're a 2-person research lab training our own generative video models entirely from scratch. The ultimate ambition is creative tools that democratize access to animation. You can read more about that here.
Today, generative video models have two major flaws: they are too slow/expensive and they lack proper controls. Creativity is an iterative process. You make, you tweak, you get better. With this double whammy, video models (as they stand today) are effectively useless for storytelling.
When we set out to build Linum v3 earlier this year, the primary goal was to bring the cost envelope of inference down significantly. If our model is fast and cheap, it's the first step (of many) towards useful creative tools.
We've written extensively about the steps we've taken in this direction, but here's the long story short:
- The bottleneck for training and inference for video is attention, O(n²). If we can compress more aggressively, we'll reduce the context window for attention, slashing the cost of training and inference.
- Typically, compression is handled by a separate specialized model like a Variational Autoencoder, but that's not necessarily optimal. VAEs seem to have an empirical ceiling on the amount of compression they can achieve. More importantly, improving the VAE on its task of reconstruction can produce worse generations in the downstream diffusion model.
- If you get rid of the VAE altogether, you should be able to compress more aggressively and learn faster. Diffusion models are a more powerful family of generative models than VAEs. We should be able to use them to compress and generate at the same time. Instead of using another model's latent space, the diffusion model should learn a latent space purpose-built for its use case. In turn, we should be able to squeeze more juice out of this compression.
In our last blog, we iterated towards a new architecture that we called the Pyramid-JiT (P-JiT). It's a pixel-space model that converges in 11× fewer samples than Linum v2 with 4× more aggressive token reduction (i.e., we represent a 512² image in the same number of tokens we used to represent a 256² image).
For the details on how we got there, we'd recommend you take a look at our previous blogs on JiT-DDT and P-JiT. But at a high level, we found a noise schedule for pixel-space models that helps the models better learn fine-grained detail. And, we added readouts along the trunk of the DiT.
Adjacent work in REPA, RAE-v2, and Self-Flow all point to weak gradients in the early blocks of the DiT. By operating in pixel space, every block of our DiT is quite literally predicting a version of the final image, so we can boost gradient signal earlier in the network by predicting low-resolution versions of the final image. This helps accelerate convergence.
The architecture did well in our initial experiments, but we wanted to see how it performed when we trained it for longer (i.e., ~600M samples instead of 50–100M samples). It's a pressure test of sorts along the way towards larger models (and video).
Training Regime
Text and image each pass through two single-modality "refiner" DiT blocks, then merge into one stream of 22 identical DiT blocks. The text representation comes from a frozen Qwen3.5-4B model, which we read at layers 7, 15 and 27 (its three full-attention layers). We concatenate and project these representations into the text refiner. The image is split into 32×32 patches and compressed into a 1024-dimensional space, before being projected up into the image refiner blocks.
At block 5, we have a 2-layer PixelREPA readout that aligns the model's latent space to DINOv3-L. We have two linear readout heads along the trunk after blocks 10 and 16. As discussed earlier, these predict the clean image at a quarter and half resolution, respectively. The total loss is given by MSE at the readouts, PixelREPA cosine-distance, MSE at the final output head, and perceptual losses on the full resolution image (LPIPS and P-DINO).
Every DiT block is conditioned on the timestep through a low-rank AdaLN, following Z-Image. The timestep embedding goes through one down-projection to 256-dim. Each block then has its own up-projection to a scale and a gate, zero-initialized so every block starts as the identity.
As described in our last blog, we employ a curriculum over the noise schedule. We start concentrated at high noise for the first 80M samples, so the model learns basic structure. Then, we widen the noise distribution for the remaining training time so it can learn fine-grained details. See Appendix A for details.
We start EMA at 100M samples, so the model has already learned decent representations of structure and detail. When we switch to 1024², we start with a fresh optimizer and resume training from the 512² EMA weights. At 1024² we double the noise scale from 2 to 4 to account for the redundancy of information (see JiT, Appendix A). The higher noise level and need to reproduce fine detail exactly at high resolution result in a higher overall loss, even as image quality improves rapidly. We use RoPE interpolation in the resolution transition. Moreover, we start the 1024² EMA 2M samples in, so it has adjusted to the new resolution before we start the averaging process.
For post-training, we used a variant of Finite Difference Flow Optimization (FDFO) with JEV-Image and human-in-the-loop judges.
To be blunt, post-training is a very young discipline within diffusion models, and we've only seen the first sparks of it working in the past few months.
Of course there's been a lot of work in model and schedule distillation, but when it comes to post-training in terms of improving fundamental model capabilities (like we've seen with coding in LLMs over the past 18 months), it hasn't really come to fruition just yet for diffusion.
DPO has been around for a while, but tends to dramatically overfit to specific aesthetic choices. Flow-GRPO was one of the first articulations of bringing RLVR-style frameworks to diffusion, and that only came out last fall. Since then there have been a few papers like Diffusion NFT and this June's FDFO (the best articulation of diffusion post-training so far, in our opinion).
We think that this difficulty in adapting techniques comes down to the fact that we don't have access to strong likelihood estimates in diffusion models like we have in auto-regressive LLMs. We'll dive more into these concerns and our full post-training stack in the coming weeks.
Model Limitations
This model was not trained with images of rendered text (no infographics, PDFs, pages of books, PowerPoint slides), so it cannot write text or generate posters. As we detailed in our data filtration blog, we don't consider this data useful for our ultimate goal of animation tools, so this text-heavy data has been filtered out entirely from our dataset.
This is a small model (2B-parameter DiT, 4B text encoder), and accordingly there are a few areas where the model struggles: full body, many entities (e.g. 6 cars, crowds), multi-person interactions, and world knowledge. The model is capable of correctly generating these images on occasion. Training a larger model (e.g. 7B parameters) and further post-training would likely mitigate these issues.
