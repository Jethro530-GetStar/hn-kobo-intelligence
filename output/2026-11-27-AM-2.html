2. Holy shit holy shit holy shit holy shit: The magnitude of what’s now happening
FULL TEXT
2001 points · 1823 comments · Kotlopou

Article
I feel like my previous posts (and post titles), and podcasts, and talks, have failed to do full justice to the magnitude of what’s now happening, so I’m trying again.
Last night, we had friends and colleagues over for dinner. We were still trying to process OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s proofs of VP ≠ VNP—hot on the heels, obviously, of the Alpöge/Anthropic proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, which already feels like last week’s news. But then before dessert was served, we saw that OpenAI claims to prove that ω, the exponent of matrix multiplication, is exactly 9/4—a lower bound (!!), matching the upper bound that OpenAI proved in the great Mathocalypse drop of October 6.
There are two separate shocks here. The first shock is that we now, finally, know the right answer to this central mystery of algorithms going back to the late 1960s, when Volker Strassen first showed that ω<3 (he got ω≤log27) … and that the true answer is actually a rational number (!). But the even bigger shock is that the AI was able to prove any lower bound of the form ω>2. I never expected this challenge to be all that much easier than P ≠ NP.
Over the decades, I often heard colleagues express extreme confidence that ω=2, and I expressed skepticism whenever this came up—not because I had any strong argument for ω>2, but just because I saw that there was no strong argument for ω=2 either. So, I’m glad to have been vindicated about that!
But wait, there was more. During dessert, we saw another OpenAI release proving that the true number of linear gates needed to compute the Fourier Transform is N (log N)1/2+o(1). The upper bound here improves, not only over the O(N log N) that stood since the 1960s (i.e., the Fast Fourier Transform or FFT), but also over the O(N log0.9999999999999 N) (give or take some 9’s) that OpenAI published back in the October 6 Mathocalypse. Again, though, the real shock here is that there’s also a matching lower bound (!!). All these questions from the ancient days are now just getting … straight-up answered, rather than slowly illuminated with a drip-drip-drip of clues.
To give an idea of how much things have changed, the other day Yupan Liu put a preprint on the arXiv proving that QIP(2) = PSPACE—that is, quantum interactive protocols with only two messages have the full power of those with three, or any polynomial number. As recently as 2025, this would’ve considered been one of the top complexity results of the year, and the fact that Yupan had used AI to help achieve it would’ve been strange and wonderful. These days, I had to remind myself it had even happened, and the use of AI was so standard that it didn’t even raise an eyebrow.
It’s now obvious that we’ll be getting, not one-off solutions to this or that complexity question, but a whole new continent of mathematical truth, one that we’d previously only glimpsed through fog. Picture van Leeuwenhoek first training a microscope on pondwater, or Galileo first training his telescope on the stars. That might give you some idea of how our resolution into the complexity-theoretic universe, and the whole universe of mathematical truth beyond it, has now improved.
All through last night, as OpenAI and Anthropic uploaded more files to their GitHub repositories—because that’s what they’re doing now, they don’t even bother to issue press releases anymore, they just upload papers—my guests and I sat at the dinner table with phones out and a laptop, wondering which shoe was going to drop next. It was as though the clouds had parted above Mount Sinai, and we were waiting for the next sentence uttered by the Holy One Himself.
I now feel in my bones, for the first time ever, that a proof of P ≠ NP is not far.
For all I care, despise the AI companies for bulldozing our garden. Or love them for opening a new vista, or anything in between. Just please don’t deny what’s happening. Now that the companies have repeatedly shown that they can back up their claims with Lean certificates, don’t be a raging doofus who seizes on some triviality (e.g., a minor discrepancy between the Lean and informal proofs, or unused sections of the Lean proof) to pretend that dozens of great open problems of math and theoretical computer science are not, in fact, getting solved. Because they are getting solved. It might take us years to understand the solutions, but my working assumption by this point is that any proof that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic is correct barring information to the contrary.
I can’t possibly put the matter better than one of my friends on Facebook:
After years of crackpot “solutions” claiming Millennium Problems are solved, now that we have actual solutions, we have crackpot “refutations” claiming Millennium Problems are not solved. It is really poetic, in a way.
Along the way to so many lower bounds that we wondered whether ever we’d live to see, there will also, of course, be further shocking upper bounds: that is, ultimate barriers to lower bounds, also known as “algorithms.” And yes, some of those algorithms will break currently deployed cryptosystems.
Because people have been hounding me about it: yes, I’m virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells in that area. However, until I either see details or hear directly from someone at a lab, I feel much, much less confident about the specific nature of those bombshells. I do not wish to be responsible for spreading unverified rumors. And I worry that exact statements of what was done may have been mangled in a game of Telephone.
I also, crucially, don’t want the cryptography part of the story to overshadow the intellectual enormity of what’s happening. If, in these days of long-prophesied revelation and wonders and terrors, your main worry is your Bitcoin wallet, then go somewhere else for rumors about whether or not ECDSA is broken or whatever. I don’t have what you’re looking for here.
Let me be clear: for those of my readers who celebrate, I believe that tomorrow would be a superb Thanksgiving to spend some quality time with your kids and parents and siblings and old friends.
Right now—this past week—is very much like I would’ve always imagined the beginning of an AI singularity to look and feel like. I just hope we’ll get lucky, and it will “merely” be a singularity for our little corner of the world that involves hard math problems.
At some point at dinner last night, Dana had a sneezing fit. So I said to her, I hope it isn’t a swarm of nanobots that’s come to infect us! But if it is, then I hope it’s the friendly kind, the kind that will usher us all into an AI utopia.
Now, of course, another morning has dawned, and we’re still here. May we find a world where we and our children will be happy to live at the bottom of this rollercoaster. May we work to make it so.
Updates (Nov. 26):
(1) Many commenters thought it was inappropriate and unnecessary of me to warn people to stop being “raging doofuses” who, fingers in their ears and seizing on the flimsiest of pretexts, just flat-out denied the reality of AI solving our longstanding open math problems. Surely no one was doing that?! And then, of course, tons of other people did exactly that. Gary Marcus, to his eternal shame (because he knows better), signal-boosted to his 260,000 Twitter followers Jenny Nielsen’s crackpot claim that the existence of unused sections of Alpöge’s Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis somehow invalidated the proof—while, in the spirit of Tucker Carlson, never quite endorsing the crackpot claim. Meanwhile, in my moderation queue, I have someone angrily accuse me of “sucking off the AI companies,” patronizingly explaining to me that, if I myself say it will take years for experts fully to understand these proofs, then obviously it must take years to verify them as well! The entire concept of Lean verification just sails entirely over his head. So I say one more time: either give evidence of bugs in Lean that would actually matter here, for example by showing how the “con-leche” verifier can accept a proof of 0=1. Or else stop being a raging doofus.
(2) Many other people said some version of, “so computers are solving a bunch of super-hard math problems. Whoop-de-doo. Either explain to me why this matters for my everyday life, or shut up and go away.”
Very well then: there are probably no direct implications of a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis or of VP≠VNP for your everyday life. The vast new ideas that AI introduced to prove such theorems might well have indirect implications, eventually leading to new fields of mathematics with scientific and technological applications, but right now we can only speculate.
In the meantime, however, the fact that AI now exists that’s capable of such things has potentially staggering implications for everyday life, which could be “merely” a transformation of the world’s economy, or could range all the way up (down?) to the destruction of all life on earth in a matter of years in a cataclysmic technological singularity. Opinions differ. But if you want to be part of the serious conversation on such matters, you’ll first need to set aside your “ooga booga, me only care about Riemann proof if it help me make sharper spear” caveman act.
(3) Finally, moving on to better questions, someone asked why I titled a blog post “HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT HOLY SHIT” only now, and not earlier. Of course, I could’ve used that title at any time since May, if not before! In my mind, though, even after the AI Mathocalypse began in earnest, there remained two interesting hypotheses: (a) that AI was picking off problems “in the convex hull of existing techniques” (like, I dunno, just the friggin’ Riemann Hypothesis) while P≠NP remained serenely untouched and would still take a human conceptual leap, or (b) that AI had really, actually climbing the mountain that leads to P≠NP. Even after VP≠VNP, you could wonder if that was just a bizarre one-off. But with the determination of the matrix multiplication exponent and all the other breakthroughs of the past couple days, option (a) has become much less tenable. Yes, I do now think that AI is climbing the mountain that leads to P≠NP.
3. Hard Drive pricing has gotten bad

For years now, I’ve made it an annual tradition to replace one or two hard drives on Black Friday. I typically run around ten physical disks, and swapping a couple out each year means I almost never have a drive older than five years in my systems. I’ve even previously made videos about shucking disks around this time of year.
But current hardware pricing is totally screwed. So what is a lifelong self-hoster to do in times like these? With disk capacities increasing massively over the last 10 years I can now fit 20TB per slot. This means I could realistically go from ten spinners to five, and not really feel it too acutely. This will mean redoing my zpool layouts, which in turn means shuffling some data around as ZFS doesn’t like changes to the layout of existing pools. But, at these prices, I’m starting to think the hassle might be worth it.
Let’s take a look at disk prices over the past 8 years in today’s post.
The 20TB WD EasyStore I bought for $249.99 in November 2024 is, today, on sale, $549.99 this Black Friday. $300 more than 2 years ago for the same product, from the same store. It shows just how bad hard drive pricing has gotten (BestBuy 20TB listing).
Shucking is homelab speak for taking a perfectly good external drive out of its USB enclosure and sticking the SATA drive in your server. At $12.50/TB it was a fantastically affordable way to get massive amounts of storage. At $27.50/TB, absolutely not.
I dug through my receipts

Buying refurbished or recertified enterprise drives used to be an easy way to bring the cost down. In August 2024 I bought four recertified 14TB Ultrastars from ServerPartDeals for $529.96 before tax. That was 56TB at $9.46/TB, even cheaper than the EasyStores and no shucking to do.

The sad truth is that across the board prices are up a minimum of 58%, and sometimes as much as 218% in just a year or two.
How we got down to three HDD makers
When the EasyStore is that bad, my first instinct is to shop around. Maybe I should try another retailer (to be clear I’m not blaming BestBuy for any of this), buy an enterprise drive, see what’s available recertified. But follow those listings back to the companies that actually make the drives and the choices narrow pretty quickly. There are three big HDD makers left. How did we end up with so few?
Maxtor bought Quantum’s hard drive business in 2001, then Seagate bought Maxtor in 2006. Hitachi combined IBM’s disk business with its own to form HGST in 2003, and Toshiba bought Fujitsu’s HDD business in 2009. Seagate bought Samsung’s HDD business in December 2011, and Western Digital bought HGST in March 2012. The FTC required WD to sell desktop-drive manufacturing assets to Toshiba to keep a third supplier in that market. That left Seagate, Western Digital and Toshiba.
The 2011 Thailand floods hit while those last two deals were underway. WD halted production at all its Thai plants, and its average hard drive selling price went from $45 in fiscal 2011 to $62 in fiscal 2012. There was also a price-fixing case further up the supply chain. NHK Spring pleaded guilty in 2019 to fixing prices for the suspension assemblies that hold the read/write heads, and the court approved a $28.5 million fine. WD’s 2012 annual report · Ninth Circuit opinion
Market collusion and cartel pricing
It’s hard to have anything positive to say in this post. The HDD manufacturing market is so consolidated nowadays - just like the memory market which is equally screwed - that it’s hard to see this as anything but mass market collusion by the HDD manufacturing cartel.
Three companies is an awfully short list when you need to buy a hard drive. And with 89% of WD’s revenue coming from its cloud business in fiscal 2026, I don’t reckon those of us buying a couple of drives for a home server have much impact here. Just like with Nvidia and Micron, consumer markets just aren’t valuable enough so we have to make do with the crumbs instead.
As of November 26, 2026, the 20TB is 2.2× what I paid for the exact same SKU in 2024. It’s also $270 more than I paid for it in May 2025. And if you’re thinking you’ll just buy a smaller one, Best Buy’s 8TB Black Friday special is $299.99. That’s $37.50/TB and fifty bucks more than my 20TB cost two years ago. Best Buy’s EasyStore listings
The jump gets sillier when you price a whole NAS. Four of those 20TB drives would have been $999.96 at my 2024 price. This week they’d be $2,199.96. That’s another $1,200 before you’ve bought a case, a board or a single stick of RAM. And the price per usable terabyte only gets worse once you set aside a drive for parity.
If I can wish for one thing in 2027, it would be for hardware pricing to return to reasonable levels. I fear it may take a lot longer than that, though. So making do with less is going to be the new normal for a while. And in that creative constraint, maybe we’ll have some fun along the way instead.

Update: We will be providing a Root Cause Analysis (RCA) with details in the coming weeks once our full investigation is complete.
What happened
On November 19, 2026, 1Password began investigating indications that an attacker gained access to an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The actor used the account to access source code and push malicious code to backend services, which downloaded administrative data pertaining to our customers. This data included customer names and email addresses, in addition to non-secret internal metadata about the account. At no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker.
What information was affected
The attacker downloaded account records for a subset of our users. These records include:
- Names and email addresses
- Account and user identifiers, and account creation and last sign-in times
- Public keys (which are public by design, this has no impact to user security)
- Secure Remote Password protocol parameters (which cannot be used to access your account)
- Tokens present in certain emailed links for invitations, provisioning and account recovery
- Other non-secret account metadata relative to our internal architecture (e.g. version numeric identifiers, file numeric identifiers)
The attacker also copied source code from private repositories and gained access to our CI/CD system. Our source code is not customer data, and contains no user secrets. CI/CD contains infrastructure related secrets in order for our product to work. We have triggered a rotation of all credentials which may have been exposed, and there is no impact to 1Password’s services, availability, or product security.
When we send an email to a customer to accept a provisioning invite to an account, or to accept recovery of their account, it contains a token. A provisioning invite is specifically used for our SCIM Bridge or Hosted Provisioning. During this incident there were a small number of users in this state, who have been specifically notified. This token is used to complete the invitation or recovery process, but only when combined with an SSO sign-in or a six digit code sent to the user's registered email address. The token is not able to get access to the account by itself. In order to reduce the ability for a malicious third party to successfully guess the code, we’ve expanded the code from six numeric digits to ten alphanumeric digits. This makes brute force guessing practically impossible.
What was not affected
- Your vaults and the items in them
- The keys that protect your vaults
- 1Password does not store your account password or secret key
- Account recovery keys
- 1Password apps and browser extensions
What we’ve done
- Disabled the affected GitHub account, revoked the stolen token, and removed the access the attacker created.
- Rotated or revoked all internal credentials which may have been exposed.
- Lengthened the codes used in invitation and recovery emails from six digits to ten characters as a precautionary measure.
- Tightened internal GitHub, CI/CD, and endpoint controls, and added new detections for this kind of attack.
- Validated no internal evidence of user account takeovers or compromise using any exposed account provisioning tokens.
- Brought in independent cybersecurity experts to validate our containment approaches and remediation efforts.
What you can do
- Be cautious about phishing attempts in the form of unexpected emails, texts or calls that mention 1Password, invitations, or account recovery attempts.
- 1Password will never contact you to ask for your account password or Secret Key. Only enter them in the official 1Password app, or on our official website, and only when you’ve opened the app or typed the address yourself. Your Secret Key is only needed the first time you sign in on a new device or browser.
- Keep your Secret Key and account password safe.
- If you see something suspicious, tell us at support@1password.com.
Our commitment to you

AI made self-hosting fun again
I’ve been self-hosting things for many years. There were times when I was quite enthusiastic about it, constantly trying out new software and tinkering with my infrastructure. But at some point, that enthusiasm faded a bit.
I never really stopped self-hosting, but I became less inclined to experiment with new things. Maintaining what I already had running was enough for me.
Over the last few months, however, I’ve found myself getting increasingly excited about self-hosting again. And, somewhat ironically, AI is a big reason for that.
Less friction
Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project.
Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort.
In the past, I would sometimes put off certain changes simply because I didn’t feel like spending an evening reading changelogs and documentation or troubleshooting issues. It’s not that I couldn’t have done these things before. But often, the amount of effort required just wasn’t worth it to me.
Now, even relatively spontaneous ideas often end up becoming reality.
Trying things I always found interesting
There are also technologies that I’ve always found interesting but never felt knowledgeable enough about to really make use of them.
NixOS is a good example. I’ve always liked the idea of declaratively configuring an entire system; I even used it on my laptop during my Linux honeymoon phase. But getting everything configured the way I wanted was quite a hurdle. With the help of AI, I’m now making much more extensive use of NixOS on my home server.
For my VPS infrastructure, I’ve also started building my own Debian-based bootc image (after thinking about it for some time).
Having my entire operating system configuration defined in a Git repository and automatically building a bootable container image from it is something I find really cool. But it’s also something I probably wouldn’t have invested enough time in to get working on my own before. Even with the help of AI, it still took me half a day when I set it up a few months ago. Without AI, it would probably have taken me at least a week.
AI makes experimenting with unfamiliar technologies much more approachable. I don’t necessarily need to understand every little detail before I can get started. I can learn about things along the way, ask questions, and gradually improve my configurations.
Automating almost everything
Something I’ve also finally managed to do is automate most of the build, update, and deployment processes for my infrastructure.
A lot of the work involved in self-hosting isn’t particularly exciting. Keeping track of new releases, updating container image tags, building images, and deploying configuration changes are all things that need to be done regularly.
I still remember what updating a service used to look like: bumping the version and adjusting the Docker Compose file on my laptop, pushing the changes to the Git remote, SSHing into the server, pulling the changes, and finally deploying them. Now, a single Git push is enough. If a push is even necessary.
With Renovate, I no longer need to actively keep track of most software updates. It creates pull requests for me, and often, all I need to do is review and merge them. I can even do that on my phone while sitting on the sofa.
Pushing changes or merging a pull request triggers the corresponding GitHub Actions or Forgejo Actions workflows, which take care of building and deploying everything.
Of course, there’s nothing particularly groundbreaking about any of this. DevOps has been around for a long time, and none of these automation tools require AI. But AI has helped me put all these pieces together in a way that I previously never quite got around to doing.
And the nice thing about automating these tasks is that the benefits extend well beyond the initial setup. I now have less tedious work to do every time I want to change something or update a service.
Back to more powerful hardware
As I’ve started hosting more things again, I’ve also moved more of my services from my VPS to my home server.
For a while, I was using an HP t640 thin client as my home server. However, with more things I wanted to run, I decided to switch back to my ASRock DeskMini A300, which had been sitting unused for some time. I’m thinking about repurposing the t640 as a “router on a stick”, though.
I even took the opportunity to upgrade its CPU from an AMD Ryzen 5 2400G to a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and replaced the stock AMD cooler with a quieter Noctua cooler. Noise was the main reason I switched to the t640 in the first place.
It’s nice to put some hardware I already owned back to use rather than buying an entirely new machine. I also managed to get both the CPU and the cooler for a good price through a classifieds site.
Having some additional computing power available gives me more room to experiment. And there’s something quite satisfying about having a small box at home running a growing collection of services that I use in my everyday life.
Building my own tools
But it’s not just about self-hosting existing software. AI has also changed how I approach problems for which there isn’t an existing piece of software that does exactly what I want.
Previously, I would often spend quite some time searching for a suitable open-source project, comparing different alternatives, and figuring out how to make one of them work for my particular use case.
These days, for smaller problems, building my own solution has become a much more attractive option.
I’ve recently built tools to automatically download invoices, e-papers or Linux ISOs.
I can build (or have AI build) these tools in Go, my preferred programming language, and tailor them to my exact needs.
They don’t need to support every possible use case, have an elaborate user interface, or be particularly interesting to anyone other than me. I don’t need to turn every little tool into a proper open-source project with extensive documentation and support for other people’s use cases. They just need to solve my problem.
What I particularly enjoy is how well this complements my increasingly automated infrastructure. Building a small tool has become easier, and so has building a container image for it, deploying it, and keeping it running.
The entire process, from having an idea to having something actually running on my home server, requires much less effort than it used to.
Just because I can doesn’t mean I should
Of course, there are downsides to all of this.
Every additional service is another thing that needs to be maintained. AI-generated software isn’t exempt from bugs, security issues, or technical debt. And having an AI agent make changes to my infrastructure doesn’t absolve me of the responsibility of understanding those changes.
There’s also the temptation to build or deploy things simply because I can. When implementing an idea becomes so easy, it’s perhaps more important than ever to occasionally ask myself whether it’s actually worth implementing in the first place.
On the other hand, self-hosting is a hobby for me. Not everything I do needs to be economically sensible or particularly useful. Sometimes, experimenting with something new is the whole point.
And with much of the routine maintenance automated, adding another service doesn’t necessarily mean adding as much recurring work as it used to.
A somewhat ironic development?
I find it somewhat ironic that AI, a technology largely dominated by big tech companies, is helping me become a little less dependent on those very companies in other areas of my digital life.
I’m aware that this also creates a new kind of dependency. But the software and infrastructure configurations I create with the help of AI can continue to exist independently of the AI services I used to create them. And since I mostly use open-weight models, I have a wide range of providers to choose from.
What I appreciate most, however, isn’t necessarily the time I’m saving. It’s that I’m more inclined to actually pursue the little ideas I have.
I’m trying out technologies I’ve been curious about for years. I’m automating things that I’ve wanted to automate for a long time. I’m building little tools for myself. I’m tinkering with hardware again.
AI hasn’t fundamentally changed why I like self-hosting. I still enjoy having control over my digital life, learning new things, and tinkering with technology.
It’s just removed a lot of the friction that made me lose some of my enthusiasm for it over the years.
And now, I’m having a lot of fun with it again.
