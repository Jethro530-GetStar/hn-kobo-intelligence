2. Smart home devices should work without the internet

Article
AI;DR: I wrote the first draft, AI helped me edit and rewrite it, and I made the final pass. The rules, setups, and talks are my own.
TLDR; Nothing core in my house depends on a vendor’s server. If the internet goes down, the house keeps running. That’s been rule one for about ten years, and my other rules came out of it. This post has the rules, then both talks I’ve given on home automation, slide by slide.
Why local
My Nest integration worked until Google broke the API. That’s the failure I plan around now. The talks have a few more: a Samsung fridge showing a TLS certificate error on its own screen, and a Camp Chef grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show you the temperature.
Everything that matters runs on hardware I own. That’s Tasmota on ESP boards, an MQTT broker, and Node-RED on a Raspberry Pi. I do use some cloud services, mostly Alexa and a few AWS ones. Alexa is just another way to flip a switch. If it’s down, the wall switches and the wall dashboard still work.
The rules
From the first talk, restated in the second:
- No internet, no problem. Core stuff has to work with the internet unplugged. Alexa can be a way in, but never the only way.
- Don’t remove the old way while adding the new one. If someone tapes a wall switch on so a smart bulb keeps power, I skipped this rule.
- Be agile. Rooms get used differently and people move in and out, so it all has to be cheap to change.
- Check your automations. I once had four scheduled triggers hitting the same garden pump.
- Exceptions must be exceptional. I can break a rule, but only for a reason I’d still agree with six months later.
Added after year five, once more people lived in the house:
- A cloud device needs an API with logs or it doesn’t get installed. I don’t want a black box in the house.
- Config lives in one place instead of five vendor apps.
- More than one way in: an app, Alexa, a QR code, or an NFC tag.
- If I keep doing the same steps, they become a routine.
- Devices talk pub/sub over MQTT instead of point to point.
- No batteries unless they last a year or more between changes. Nobody wants to swap a door sensor battery at 2am.
The build I get asked about most is the Tasmota sprinkler controller. It’s in both talks, and so is the garage door setup, which has been running about as long.
Talk One: Home Automation
Nobody needs any of this. I like building things that just work in the background, and each automation is a small puzzle.
Everything talks to a cloud now: phones, laptops, thermostats, locks. Those clouds may or may not be up when you need them, so know what you’re signing up for before you plug something in.
Most people start here: a thermostat, a plug, a lock. Each has its own app and account.
Every device lands somewhere on this triangle. A cloud camera is cheap and easy, and it costs you privacy. A switch I flash myself takes longer to set up, but I can hook it into anything. I figure out where a device lands before I buy it.
Someone (often me) automates a light, then has to tape the wall switch on so the smart bulb keeps power. If a switch in a room ever did nothing, this is why.
The better fix is an in-wall Wi-Fi switch. The switch still works like a normal switch and I can still automate it. It’s more work to install than a smart plug, but nobody has to change their habits.
Rule one: if the internet goes down, my lights still turn on.
It’s never done. Rooms get used differently, people move in and out, new devices show up. Whatever you build has to be cheap to change.
The garage cooling flow starts from a manual trigger and checks if the door is closed. Then it opens the door, waits, and turns on the fan. If cooling is already running, it just sends a notification. Small flow, but it has kept the garage from baking more than once.
The door sensor and reed switch both report into a transform step that sets a global garage-closed flag and logs to Grafana. Below that is the chart from a few real days: 21 opens, with spikes showing when each one happened. It’s easy to skip reporting/alerting. This can alert me if the garage was left open, which is useful when I accidentally leave it open.
Four scheduled triggers all hit the same pump. It’s easy to end up with automations fighting over one device, so every so often I check whether they still make sense together.
I’ve broken all of these plenty of times, so they’re more like guidelines.
Three ways to do “event in, action out.” Node-RED is my main tool. Alexa routines and IFTTT-style applets cover the stuff I don’t want to build myself.
MQTT keeps things decoupled. The temperature sensor publishes and doesn’t care who’s listening, so a phone and a backend can both subscribe without the sensor doing anything extra. The sprinkler controller in the Tasmota post works the same way.
A Sonoff Mini fits inside the fireplace wiring, and the dashboard or Alexa can flip it. A fake fire you can turn on by voice is a good party trick.
The whole sprinkler build: the relay and ESP module in the enclosure, the 3D board render, the Tasmota UI with BME280 sensor readings, the Node-RED schedule feeding the pump, and the finished PCB layout. The sprinkler post has the full writeup, including why I picked Tasmota over custom firmware. It’s a bit hacky but I love it :D
The whole talk in five lines. The one I break most is the first, don’t introduce a new way while removing the old one, and I regret it every time. The last one stops me from breaking the others for no good reason. “I was in a hurry” doesn’t count.
That was talk one. Most of the “memorable automations” later broke in some new way or got replaced, so there’s a sequel.
Talk Two: Home Automation, The Sequel
Enough time passed between the talks that I had a new list of problems.
More devices means more decisions made without me. It’s a little unsettling but mostly harmless.
Same slide as before, with more icons.
Everyone else is dealing with this too: a fridge showing a TLS certificate error, a grill controller that needs Wi-Fi to show the temperature, and a note taped to a pole about an internet-connected printer someone forgot to log out of.
The same rules from talk one, worded a bit tighter, since part of the audience hadn’t seen the first talk.
The rules stayed the same, but what they had to survive got harder.
My setup assumed one person lived in the house. When that changed, I had to patch a single-user system into a multi-user one. The next few slides are how that went.
More people meant more foot traffic and more keys, and longer trips away meant things could fail with nobody around to notice. A water flooding scare finally got me to take leak and presence detection seriously.
Same joke, new rules. The first set came after year one, these after year five.
These are rules 6 to 11 at the top of the post, written after the house went from one person to several.
Now the builds, my favorite part.
A motor and PVC pipe rig for outdoor lighting, a UV grow light clipped to a houseplant, and a UV strip under the cabinets for sanitizing.
Same sprinkler slide as talk one. It’s one of the few builds that never needed a rewrite.
A sensor that sits on a credenza, a robot vacuum that runs on its own, and an AirGradient kit I put together from a CO2 sensor, a particulate sensor and a microcontroller, with its own dashboard. The air quality kit gives me the most peace of mind of anything here.
This dashboard replaced more apps than anything else: garage door, side door unlock, front lock, all lights on or off, brightness, a coffee bar color picker, and warm-to-cool for the living room. It’s one cheap tablet next to the thermostat, so nobody digs through five apps.
The lights behind the TV run through the same system instead of their own remote.
The smart locks came out of the more-people-more-keys problem. The newer one takes a password, the app, a keycard or a regular key, so everyone uses what they’re comfortable with.
The parts bin, for anyone who wants to build this.
The ESP32 D1 mini handles anything custom. The smart plug is the off-the-shelf shortcut when I don’t need custom. The Pi 4 runs Node-RED and Grafana.
LED strips for ambient lighting, power transistors for higher-current loads, and small relay modules so a microcontroller can switch mains voltage safely.
The rest of the bin: a resistor assortment, color-coded wire so I can trace a circuit later, and a capacitor kit for smoothing and timing.
Node-RED for the logic, Grafana to see what’s happening, Tasmota on anything I flash, Telegram for notifications, and Python or JavaScript for glue code that doesn’t fit in a flow.
The garage flow today. The door sensor and reed switch feed Grafana and a global closed state. Toggle nodes publish straight to Tasmota command topics. Notifications go to Telegram and an audio chime, and a light-on counter resets at midnight. The tabs across the top (Lights, Sensors, Garage, Sprinkler, NFC Tags, Door Lock Security) show how much it has grown since the single garage cooling flow in talk one.
This is the “is everything okay” dashboard: humidity, temperature, dew point, pressure and gas on one side, and energy use, door opens and on-status history on the other. I built it because I got tired of guessing.
Dashboards and Grafana panels work for me. Everyone else in the house just wants the light on.
The “more than one way in” rule in hardware: the wall tablet for whoever is standing there, NFC tags and a QR code to tap, and an Echo Dot for anyone who would rather talk to the house.
The tools I build and debug all of this with.
A soldering iron for permanent connections, a 3D printer for enclosures and brackets you can’t buy, a multimeter for when something isn’t working, and a precision screwdriver kit for devices the manufacturer didn’t want opened.
That’s both talks, five years apart. The rules barely changed between them, but the list of problems kept growing.
A third talk is coming, built around agentic use cases. The same rules should hold when an AI agent is the one flipping the switches, and I expect to add a few.
What readers told me
I shared the “no internet, no problem” rule on LinkedIn and asked for the most pointless internet dependency people had found in a device. Dozens of people answered. A Hacker News thread added more. Here’s what came back.
Everyone has one
Most examples were basic functions locked behind an app, an account, or a server:
- A fridge whose temperature can’t be changed because of a bug in the manufacturer’s phone app.
- A TV that can’t even switch inputs when the internet is down, and another that has to be signed in before it works as a plain HDMI monitor. One reader’s older smart TV has dead apps but still works as a regular TV, which is how a device should fail.
- A heating system whose app doesn’t work from the local network.
- A kitchen vent and a dishwasher that need the internet even when you control them from Home Assistant.
- A smart plug that can’t be set up on an isolated network.
- Noise-cancelling headphones that search for a Bluetooth device forever, so you can’t just turn on the noise cancelling.
- Synthesizers and MIDI keyboards that need a cloud login for first use or firmware updates.
- A meat thermometer that only worked through its app and stopped working when the company went out of business.
The meat thermometer bothers me most. The hardware outlived the company, and it stopped working anyway.
Rules readers added
- Offline first, with the internet for extras. A device should do its main job on its own.
- Treat every third-party dependency as a risk. APIs change, so plan a local fallback or a clear degraded mode. One mobile developer works the same way: the core action works offline or from cache, and only the extras wait for a connection.
- Decide what must work offline. My answer is turning things on and off, temperature and safety controls, and anything a guest has to use without learning an app. As one reader said, if a visitor can’t turn something on or adjust the temperature without an app account, the design has failed.
- A stricter version of my first two rules: everything core works with the Wi-Fi or the automation server shut down, and smart features never take away the dumb ones. The wall switch should control the light directly and let automation flip it. A switch that only sends a request to the automation fails. That reader says my sprinkler controller fails this bar too. The only sprinkler controller they’d found with a local API and full control on the device itself was RainMachine, which is gone.
- Clean up when you throw things away. Remove a connected printer from your account first. An abandoned connected device can be an easy way in for an attacker.
Practical tips
- Use local protocols. One reader recommends Zigbee and Z-Wave over Wi-Fi, because a switch that doesn’t need the cloud tends to behave the same year after year. Another uses Zigbee for the smart plugs that can’t be set up without internet.
- Let an LLM find the firmware. One reader buys the cheapest hardware that does the job, ignores reviews that complain about the app, and asks an LLM for local-first custom firmware once it arrives. That has worked for their switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan. They’d have paid about 2.5x for hardware that was local out of the box.
- Old Nest thermostats have a way out. No Longer Evil is open source firmware for Nest Gen 1 and Gen 2 thermostats.
- Smart TVs can be tamed. Reset the TV and never connect it to the network, and the input problem goes away.
- Remote access is the cost of staying local. I use Tailscale on the router and keep the individual devices off the tailnet. One reader ran OpenVPN on OpenWrt, which worked but felt clunky, and is now trying Tailscale.
- There’s a gap to fill. One reader would happily pay for a pre-built, open “works with everything” Raspberry Pi image with MQTT. DietPi and OpenWrt come close and have a small footprint, but you need some understanding to get going. Another wanted a hub that ships with the house, like a water heater or a router. Home Assistant sells hub hardware that’s close to that.
- Right to repair is the bigger fight. Readers pointed to the right-to-repair movement and groups like the FULU Foundation, which work to keep companies from locking you out of what you bought.
Counterpoints
Connectivity has real uses. A connected meat thermometer lets you watch the game while the steak cooks. The same reader also had an oven decide to update its software in the middle of cooking. One reader pointed out the business model behind all of this: companies would rather sell you a service than a product. Another expects the shift away from internet-connected devices to come from resilience worries rather than consumer complaints, since enough connected homes make a target. And one skips connected appliances entirely, keeping only computers and phones online.

In this paper, we aim to answer a long-standing open problem in the programming languages community: is it possible to smear paint on the wall without creating valid Perl?
We answer this question in the affirmative: it is possible to smear paint on the wall without creating a valid Perl program. We employ an empirical approach, using optical character recognition (OCR) software, which finds that merely 93% of paint splatters parse as valid Perl. We analyze the properties of paint-splatter Perl programs, and present seven examples of paint splatters which are not valid Perl programs.
Accepted for publication at SIGBOVIK 2019, held April 1st 2019 in Pittsburgh. Winner of a Unwitting Participation Ribbon, “an unwelcome brand we’ve affixed to each paper determined after careful scrutiny to have included a genuine artifact, thereby furthering the admirable causes of open science and fruitful procrastination.”

My AI Agents (Re)discovered a Maya Site in 72 Hours
Update (11 November): it turns out the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025. The agents missed it while going through the literature, so they rediscovered it rather than discovered it (it just wasn't in the register of Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History, INAH, yet). Still work to do then! The rest of the story still holds.
By analyzing NASA lidar data, my AI agents found a Maya site in Mexico that wasn't in the official register. (It turned out archaeologists had mapped it already, more on that at the end.) It took less than 72 hours to go from a random thought while traveling to an official reply from the Mexican government. This is by far the best time ever to be alive.
Disclaimer: I'm not an archaeologist.
I'm going to tell this story as a diary, because the timeline is probably the best part.
18 October
I was traveling in Peru, Gemini-ing about the history of the place, when I learned about Hiram Bingham, the American explorer who "discovered" Machu Picchu in 1911. "What luck!" I thought. He was actually looking for a different city, Vilcabamba, the last refuge of the Incas, when a local farmer in the valley below guided him to the ruins. "Imagine if Bingham had our tools," I thought. "What would he do?"
I brainstormed a bit with a few agents (Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and Grok), and they all said lidar. From above, a ruin in the jungle is just a bunch of trees (like Machu Picchu before it was cleared), so satellite photos don't help much. Lidar, instead, fires laser pulses through the gaps in the canopy and maps the ground underneath.[1]
So the agents went looking for lidar data. Peru isn't well covered, and hiring a company to do a survey meant permits, costs and bureaucracy, so I gave up on that. Then I thought: why not the Maya? Their region is much better covered by public lidar.
That night, I asked the agents to analyze it and went to bed.
19 October
I woke up and checked the agents' reports. 'DAMN!' They had found lots of villages and other regular shapes that looked like structures or houses.
So how did they do it? They turned the lidar strips (about 1,200 km² of jungle) into images, and 48 agents went through them one by one like very patient archaeologists, looking for pyramids, platforms, and plazas while filtering out natural hills, sinkholes, and roads. There was no special model or classifier involved: the agents literally looked at the pictures, following a precise guide.
Me: That's awesome! Please check which of these villages haven't been discovered yet. Search both the literature and the archives.
…a few hours later…
Agent: One major village doesn't seem to have been registered.
This is the hilltop from the top of this post. It sits on the edge of a cliff, under the jungle, and it has straight edges and sharp corners. Nature doesn't really do straight lines.
Zooming in, there's a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid at least 8–12 m tall, a raised courtyard attached to it and a flat-bottomed hollow that might have been a water reservoir.
And this is what it looks like in 3D.
Me: OK, what should I do?
Agent: You should contact INAH (Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History), write a report on your discovery, and check whether the site is in their register. Sometimes they have unpublished results.
Me: OK, how do I contact INAH? How does the reporting process work?
Agent: …instructions for the report… + email addresses
Me: OK, please do the report.
And I went to bed.
20 October
I iterated on the report a bit, since a few things were off, and translated it into Spanish to improve the chances of getting a reply (at least, that's what Claude suggested). I don't speak Spanish, so I had to trust the agents on that one.
The agents also checked the raw point cloud, to make sure the shapes were really on the ground and not created by the trees. Green dots are leaves, brown dots are the ground.
Then I sent the report to INAH.
21 October
INAH replied: they checked their records and found no registered archaeological site at that location.
At that point I thought nobody had put it on a map before. I was wrong (see below).
So what was it?
This part is speculation, from a long chat with the agents. It looks like a small Maya civic centre from the Classic period (roughly AD 250–900), basically the "downtown" of a local elite. The plaza is where people gathered, for ceremonies and maybe even a market. The pyramid was probably a temple, and the raised courtyard next to it might have been where the local ruler lived. The hollow could have been an aguada, a rainwater reservoir: Maya towns needed them to get through the dry season. Or it's just a hole. The three platforms around were probably houses.

For similar studies, have a look at how NASA's G-LiHT instrument works (Cook et al. 2013) and how lidar collected for environmental research can be reused for archaeology (Golden et al. 2016). In 2024, archaeologists found a whole Maya city in Mexico this way (Auld-Thomas et al. 2024), others mapped settlements across the Maya lowlands, including this very site (Estrada-Belli et al. 2025), and another study found what may have been ancient Maya marketplaces (Šprajc 2026).
What's next
The site is now in the hands of the people who should have it: Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) and the archaeologists who mapped it first. If anything comes out of it, I'll post an update.
What I've learned
You can just do things. I'm not an archaeologist, so I didn't know this was supposed to be hard. I just picked an old problem, tried the most obvious thing and threw some compute at it. Being a naive outsider might be a superpower now.
And it turned out professional archaeologists had already mapped the same site. I guess that's a good sign: an outsider with agents ended up in a similar place to the experts.
And I don't think this is really about archaeology. So far, most agentic AI stories have been about coding, math and physics, but it's much more general than that, and I hope this small example makes it a bit more obvious. We are on the brink of a revolution in all the sciences, and the only bottlenecks left will be curiosity and imagination (and compute).
At this point, we should probably lidar the whole planet and let the agents discover the rest.

AWS Emulator is a local emulator of the AWS APIs. You use it to test your software without calling AWS, on your own machine, on a development server, or in continuous integration (CI). You don’t need an AWS account. Your requests go to the emulator, not to AWS. So you work with emulated resources instead of AWS resources. AWS Emulator has no additional service fee. You pay only for AWS resources when you deploy on AWS.
The emulator is a local program that serves an AWS API endpoint. It has no graphical interface. After you start the emulator, you set environment variables to point the AWS CLI or an AWS SDK at its endpoint. Then you run your code as usual. In embedded mode, you pass the emulator’s HTTP client to your SDK instead.
AWS Emulator is for building and testing. Don’t use it for production workloads or to test security. See Security in AWS Emulator.
Documentation: AWS Emulator User Guide
Install the aws-emulator skill, then ask your agent to set up the emulator. This needs the AWS CLI version 2.35.0 or later. See Getting started with an AI coding agent.
aws agent-toolkit add-skill --skill-name aws-emulator --region us-east-1
- Linux: install the RPM or Debian package and run aws-emulator. See Getting started on Linux.
- Windows / macOS: run the emulator in a container built from a short Dockerfile. See Getting started with Docker.
Point your SDK at a running emulator by setting environment variables. See Pointing your AWS SDK at AWS Emulator.
Add the emulator as a dev-dependency and run it in-process, one isolated instance per test. There is no endpoint URL, certificate, or environment variable to set. Your test wires the emulator's HTTP client straight into the SDK client. See Getting started with embedded mode, and Building from source below for its build cost.

AWS Emulator is configured to collect and report usage data to AWS, to gain insight into how AWS Emulator is used. Telemetry is on by default. AWS Emulator collects telemetry data with strict data minimization in mind, preventing identification or attribution to specific users, accounts, or organizations. Its records never include what’s in your requests or responses, your code, your credentials, or personal data. We don’t share telemetry with third parties.
For details on what is collected and how the data is handled, see Telemetry in AWS Emulator. To turn telemetry off, use one of the following options.
Start the emulator with the --telemetry option (short form -t) set to off. This setting applies only to that emulator process.
aws-emulator --telemetry off
In a container, add --telemetry off after the image name in the docker run command.
Set EMULATOR_TELEMETRY to off before you start the emulator. This setting applies to every emulator that you start from that shell.
EMULATOR_TELEMETRY accepts on, true, or 1 to turn telemetry on, and off, false, or 0 to turn it off. Values aren’t case sensitive. Any other value turns telemetry off, and the startup notice shows a warning.
export EMULATOR_TELEMETRY=off
aws-emulator
To turn telemetry off for every run on a machine, add these lines to the emulator settings file, config.toml. The file is ~/.aws/emulator/config.toml on Linux and macOS, and %USERPROFILE%\.aws\emulator\config.toml on Windows. To use a different file, set the EMULATOR_CONFIG environment variable to its path.
[telemetry]
enabled = false

10. Mothers' poor sleep in late pregnancy linked to higher obesity risk in children
FULL TEXT
1 points · 0 comments · gmays
eurekalert.org
New #2
Original article · HN discussion
Article
September 22, 2026 - New research has found that mothers with poor sleep quality in late pregnancy had babies with lower weight in the first 6 months of life, but higher weight after age 1-2 years. This effect was strongest in male infants and in babies born at term. The findings from the study in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine, published by Elsevier, suggest that incorporating sleep quality assessments into routine prenatal care could facilitate early identification of at-risk pregnancies, and addressing poor sleep quality could be beneficial for the health of both mother and child.
Sleep health in pregnancy and childhood obesity are two major and modifiable public health concerns. Global childhood and adolescent obesity rates have more than doubled from 1990 to 2022, now affecting more than 390 million children aged 5-19 years.
Studies have found that poor sleep health is associated with various health conditions, including diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular diseases. Human and animal studies have also suggested that pregnancy sleep quality can serve as a factor in the intergenerational transmission of obesity risk from mother to child.
Rapid weight gain during infancy is a risk factor for obesity in later childhood. Understanding the effects of pregnancy sleep health on early childhood growth trajectories could help mitigate impacts on children’s metabolic health.
Most previous research has been cross-sectional or conducted in children over 2 years old. Furthermore, the association between pregnancy sleep health and childhood obesity has not been extensively studied in Hispanic populations in the US, which have higher rates of being overweight or obese compared to other racial or ethnic groups.
This study investigated a group of 645 mother-child pairs from the prospective pregnancy cohort Maternal and Developmental Risks from Environmental and Social Stressors (MADRES) in Los Angeles. Researchers evaluated late-pregnancy sleep quality using the Jenkins Sleep Scale questionnaire. Height and weight of children were measured at follow-up visits at the University of Southern California clinic or abstracted from medical records.
Most mothers reported mild to moderate sleep disturbance but 26% of mothers had poor sleep quality. Children whose mothers reported the worst sleep quality had lower weights from birth to 6 months but had higher weights thereafter. At 1 year of age, the difference in weight was 346.7 grams, and the greatest difference was observed at 17 months (751.5 grams).
The study underscores the importance of maintaining good pregnancy sleep health. The findings suggest that the observed associations are primarily driven by poor late-pregnancy sleep quality and are not explained by other traditionally identified prenatal and postnatal factors such as childhood feeding practices.
Senior author Carrie V. Breton, MPH, PhD, Professor at the Bursky School of Public Health, Washington University in St. Louis, and Adjunct Professor in the Department of Population and Public Health Sciences, Keck School of Medicine, University of Southern California, says, “Our research supports the notion that exposures during critical developmental windows of pregnancy can program offspring health. Several biological mechanisms may link late-pregnancy sleep health to children’s weight trajectories. For instance, poor sleep may affect maternal or child metabolic health programming, or both, which in turn could affect neonatal fat mass distribution and lead to abnormal lipid profiles or higher inflammation. Alternatively, stress pathways associated with higher childhood obesity risk may also play a role.”
Lead author Xiaoran Yang, MPH, PhD graduate from the Department of Population and Public Health Sciences, Keck School of Medicine, University of Southern California, adds, “Poor sleep health in pregnancy is common and often overlooked, and our findings suggest that monitoring it in routine prenatal care could be a simple and powerful step toward healthier growth for the next generation.”
