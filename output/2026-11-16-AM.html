2. All AI-generated code should be Open Source

Article
This may be a hot take, but I strongly believe that all code generated by AI should be Open Source. And here's why.
First of all, AI models were trained on a massive corpus of Open Source code written since RMS announced the GNU Project and the Free Software Movement. That code was simply scraped from the internet and used without permission or proper attribution. The models were also trained on Stack Overflow and Stack Exchange sister projects, which are released under Creative Commons licenses that legally require attribution. Turning this collective knowledge into proprietary, closed-source code feels like license laundering.
The second reason is legal: purely AI-generated code is not written by a human, meaning it should not be copyrightable. The foundation for this principle in the US goes back to the famous Monkey Selfie dispute, which established that non-humans cannot hold copyrights. A precedent later cited by the U.S. Copyright Office regarding AI. Following the same logic, Wikimedia Commons requires all purely AI-generated images to be tagged as Public Domain.
However, copyright laws differ across the globe. For instance, countries like the UK grant limited copyright protection to computer-generated works. To make things fair across all jurisdictions, and to honor the open ecosystem that made AI possible, developers and companies should explicitly release AI-generated code under Open Source licenses or dedicate it to the public domain using the Creative Commons Zero (CC0) license.
Of course, code is not always 100% manual or 100% automated. However, developers more and more rely on AI without even reviewing the generated output, let alone modifying it to fit their specific needs. Code like this, where humans put little effort, except writing prompts, should definitely be Open Source.

Thousands of flights were canceled or delayed Saturday morning as airlines struggled to recover from a nationwide ground stop that halted U.S. departures for more than four hours Friday.
By 9:30 a.m. ET Saturday, flight tracking service FlightAware had logged more than 3,700 cancellations and 1,990 delays. Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, Chicago O’Hare International Airport and New York’s John F. Kennedy International Airport were among the hubs experiencing the most disruptions, according to FlightAware.
The Federal Aviation Administration lifted the ground stop at 8:15 p.m. ET Friday. More than 7,000 flights were canceled Friday, and airports across the country filled with stranded passengers. Delta Air Lines, United Airlines and American Airlines said Friday evening that they were resuming flights.
A senior government official told NBC News on Saturday that the system that caused the outage had been identified and taken offline. The FAA said a router sent too much data, overloading older air traffic control equipment.
What caused the ground stop
Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Friday evening that controllers began seeing inconsistencies in radar data and communications with aircraft at 3:36 p.m. ET. The FAA issued the ground stop at 3:54 p.m. ET, “due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.”
Duffy said the FAA retained some radar and communications capabilities and halted departures as a precaution. Medical flights, including those transporting organs for transplant, were allowed to continue.
Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said Friday night that authorities had found no evidence of a cyberattack.
“FAA was able to handle it. We did it out of an abundance of caution,” he said in a news conference from the White House Friday night. Earlier on Friday, he described the issue as a telecommunications outage.
Why disruptions continued Saturday
Airlines had to get planes and crews back into position after the ground stop, and some crews may have reached limits on how long they could stay on duty. Duffy said he expected air traffic to recover before the Thanksgiving rush.
But by 6 p.m., Reagan Washington National Airport had run out of room to park aircraft and Chicago O’Hare International Airport was nearing capacity, a source with knowledge told NBC News.
Deveren Werne was on an American Airlines jet that was about to take off from Indianapolis to Chicago when suddenly the pilots announced that all flights were grounded.
“At first, the pilot announced that there was some kind of radio issue,” Werne, 55, of Greenville, South Carolina, told NBC News in a telephone interview. “Then a little while later, the pilot said something like, ‘There is something going on that’s more serious than that.’”
Werne said he began hearing from friends who were also stuck on planes, including one on a runway in Chicago. His plane headed back to the terminal so passengers could get off.
Friday’s ground stop is perhaps the most severe consequence of what Duffy described as a decades-old air traffic control system that uses analog equipment, which has led to several issues and outages over the past few years.
In May 2025, radar screens at Newark Liberty International Airport went black during a 90-second outage.
Duffy said the legacy equipment will continue to fail and pushed Congress to approve $50 billion to overhaul the system. Congress last year approved $12.5 billion over five years to upgrade air traffic control, Reuters reported.
Mullin said although authorities cannot rule out a cyberattack, there was no evidence to suggest there was one. “There’s nothing completely certain right now. It doesn’t appear that way,” he said.
Meanwhile, Air Space Intelligence, the AI tech startup that is a vendor for the FAA on its new SMART system for air traffic control, insisted its software was not responsible for the nationwide ground stop.
“The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC,” the company said in a statement posted to X.
Duffy said the SMART system is “completely disconnected” from the equipment that suffered issues Friday.
Several hours after the ground stop was announced, a top aviation expert estimated that anywhere from 300,000 to 400,000 passengers were stranded — and it was anybody’s guess when they would get to their destinations.
“Daily, we have about 25,000 flights in U.S. domestic markets,” Vikrant Vaze, a Dartmouth professor who specializes in commercial aviation logistics, wrote in an email to NBC News. “I would say we should have around 3,000 to 4,000 flights held up at this time over the past 2 plus hours.”
With planes stranded on runways, the likelihood increased that some of these planes would have to be diverted to other airports, the source said.
Aviation safety expert Jeff Guzzetti called the outage “extraordinary.”
Guzzetti emphasized that pilots are trained to operate through communications outages and that there are redundancies in place for such instances. Pilots “could get information relayed through other means” to ensure planes are spaced properly for landing.
There are different layers of communications — high-altitude, midlevel and the towers on the ground. The high-altitude control centers are the ones affected by the outage.
Nationwide ground stops are extremely rare
Ground stops across the nation are a rarity in U.S. history.
After the Sept. 11, 2001, terrorist attacks, the FAA ordered an unprecedented nationwide ground stop. All civilian air traffic across U.S. airspace was halted and airborne planes were ordered to land at the nearest available airports.
In January 2023, the FAA grounded all flights for a few hours, blaming a failure in the Notice to Air Missions system, which alerts pilots to a host of potential dangers.
Officials at the time blamed contract personnel for the outage, saying they accidentally deleted files while working to correct the synchronization between databases. The error prompted a computer outage that lasted approximately an hour and a half, Department of Transportation officials said at the time.

INTRODUCTION
Even the smallest nuclear explosives release an energy equivalent to the detonation of several thousand tons of high explosive such as trinitrotoluene (TNT). As such they have a large effect on the surrounding environment (Glasstone & Dolan, 1977).
The energy released in a nuclear explosion is partioned as shown in the diagram below:
This is strictly for a fission explosion in the lower atmospher (below 30 km altitude). A fusion bomb and a higher altitude explosion will vary in the percentage energies shown.
A nuclear explosion releases electromagnetic energy across the entire spectrum, from radio waves through infrared, visible and ultraviolet light to x-rays and gamma rays. All of these provide a means of detection. The kinetic energy release contains a component of sound waves that propagate through the atmosphere and seismic or sound waves that propagate through the ground. For some time after the explosion (minutes to years), the radioactive materials generated in and by the explosion release radiation that can be easily measured. Very small particles travel as aerosols through the atmosphere to great distances where they may be collected and analysed.
MONITORING
| Every state that possesses nuclear weapons has some form of monitoring program to detect nuclear detonations. The USA probably has the most developed program. Not only ground sensors but also an array of space-based sensors are employed to continuously monitor the globe for nuclear explosions, both underground and in the atmosphere. On achieving access to space, the US was quick to place nuclear detection sensors in space. The first of these were payloads on satellites that were advertised as solar monitoring satellites. The first dedicated satellites were known as Vela (image at right), and these performed well up until 1980, when the role was taken over by the GPS satellite constellation. | |
When the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (CTBT) was introduced in the United Nations, the CTBT Organisation (CTBTO) was set up with one of its tasks to establish an International Monitoring System (IMS) that would be independent of individual nation states (although relying heavily on equipment developed by them). Such an organisation could then make available sensor data without secrecy restrictions that an individual state might impose.
The IMS consists of a large number of ground-based sensors designed to detect underground, underwater and atmospheric nuclear explosion. The emphasis however lies in the detection of underground explosions, which would indicate a nuclear test explosion. Atmospheric explosions have not occurred for some decades, and are more readily detected than below ground explosions. The IMS thrust was toward detection of clandestine underground explosions by states developing nuclear weapons, and for compliance by developed nuclear states.
The IMS consists of seismic sensors to detect undergound and atmospheric explosions, hydroacoustic sensors to detect underwater explosions, infrasound to detect atmospheric explosions and some underground explosions, and radionuclide monitoring stations (plus associated laboratories to anaylse collected radionuclide material).
The only sensors that are not included in the IMS suite of instruments are space-based sensors that can detect the instantaneous electromagnetic emissions produced by atmospheric and exoatmospheric (space) nuclear explosions.
UNDERGROUND NUCLEAR EXPLOSIONS
The most reliable and useful detection of undergound nuclear explosions is through the monitoring of the sound waves that carry away some of the blast energy from the explosion. Many seismometers and seismic arrays exist around the world. The graph belows shows the detection of a North Korean underground nuclear explosion by a seismometer at the Australian Space Academy site at Meckering, Western Australia:
At this distance no information about the seismic event can be obtained. However, several seismometers closer to the detonation can be used to determine the location and approximate yield. A good seismogram can be used to distinguish between an earthquake and a nuclear explosion, and the distinguishing characteristics are discussed by Bolt (1976).
Even though a nuclear explosion may be underground, there is often a leakage of radioactive material (particularly radioactive xenon) into the atmosphere, and this can be detected by aerial sampling or radionuclide monitoring stations quite some distance away.
Underwater nuclear explosions can be detected most readily by the hydroacoustic sensors of the IMS network. These are normally placed at a depth of about 750 metres and connect to a station on the coast by a cable that can be several tens of kilometres in length. These monitoring stations are the most difficult and costly of all the ground stations to set up.
In remote localities the nuclear detection stations are powered by solar photovoltaic panels and send their data via satellite to the central IMS station in Vienna.
ATMOSPHERIC EXPLOSIONS
Nuclear explosions that take place in the lower atmosphere generate a wide range of prompt electromagnetic radiation from radio to gamma rays. They also produce a very low frequency shock wave that propagates as infrasound (with a frequency below the range of human hearing) for vast distances.
| The use of VLF radio waves (3 to 30 kHz) to detect atmospheric explosions received a lot of early study. A device to allow amateurs to detect such explosions was even published by a British magazine, 'The Radio Constructor' in the January 1963 issue (see circuit at right). The utility of VLF detectors however, has proven very limited - too close and the receiver will be destroyed by the very large electrical fields produced (what is known as ElectroMagnetic Pulse or EMP), and more than about 1000 km away propagation effects render the signal almost identical to that from lightning). | |
One of the earliest special purpose devices to detect and measure above ground nuclear explosions was called the 'bhangmeter'. This was essentially a photocell connected to some form of oscillograph. The output showed the double hump flash that is so typical and defining of such bursts. It was soon shown that measurement of the time from the start of the flash to the minimum between the two humps was closely related to, and allowed prediction of the explosive energy yield. THE GPS NDS
| | The US GPS satellites now carry a Nuclear Detection System (NDS) as a secondary payload. The NDS consists of a suite of sensors that detect electromagnetic signals over a number of frequency bands. The information is transmitted to the ground at the L3 frequency of 1381.05 MHz, employing spread spectrum modulation similar to that used in the navigation signals. The enviromental data is encrypted and continually broadcast. There is also a capability to cross-link this data to other GPS satellites. |

The BDD is a dosimeter to provide an indication of the ionising particulate and gamma radiation in the environment of the satellite.
Although none of these sensors has imaging capability, they have access to the high precision on-board atomic clocks. These are used to time tag any events detected. Comparison of these times with the times recorded by other GPS satellites allow positional determination of the event source to high accuracy (as with the similar determination of position by the navigation system).
The GPS satellites are situated in a Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) within the van Allen radiation belts. This requires that their systems and electronics be hardened to withstand the high radiation environment. As such, they are uniquely suited to survive in the case of an exoatmospheric nuclear detonation.

7. The MacBook Pro's OLED PWM Problem: When a 'Better' Display Becomes Worse to Use
FULL TEXT
177 points · 88 comments · MacAnorak
macanorak.com
Top #2 · Best #45
Original article · HN discussion
Article
Apple has brought tandem OLED technology to the MacBook Pro, joining the Mini-LED display that has served the range since 2021 (and which Apple continues to sell). Apple calls this a “PROLED XDR Display”. It features individually illuminated pixels, effectively infinite contrast, wonderfully deep blacks and the sort of image quality that Mini-LED, for all its considerable strengths, can’t fully equal. Peak HDR brightness has increased, and Apple has introduced a new display engine designed to manage colour, luminance and pixel usage with extraordinary precision. And of course, in a first for any Apple notebook display, it has touch-input capability.
When Apple unveiled the new MacBook Pro, the initial reaction to the screen was overwhelmingly positive.
But there’s a burgeoning discussion taking place within the Mac community that deserves attention. On MacRumors forums, a thread titled RIP MacBook Pro PROLED for PWM Sensitive Eyes - apple cmon! has attracted people describing remarkably similar experiences. Discussions are also appearing across Reddit, with owners describing discomfort shortly after using their new machines. Some report headaches; others describe a strange sensation of eye fatigue, dizziness, nausea, or an inability to concentrate on the screen for extended periods. Some have already returned the device.
Mike Wuerthele, of AppleInsider, published an article on 10 November titled If you’re getting dizzy using PROLED MacBook Pro, you are not alone, examining these reports.
The following caveat probably doesn't need to be made, but I will make it anyway: these are individual experiences, not evidence that the new MacBook Pro is causing problems for everyone. Plenty of owners are likely perfectly content with (and dazzled by) their new displays. But that isn’t really the point. For the people experiencing these symptoms, the fact that somebody else can happily spend several hours staring at the same screen isn’t particularly reassuring, especially given Apple's long-standing commitment to accessibility. A display can be technically spectacular and still be a spectacularly poor display for a subset of people.
PWM
Much of the discussion centres on Pulse Width Modulation, or PWM. It’s a technique used to control screen brightness by rapidly switching or modulating the light output. On an OLED display, the pixels themselves produce the light, rather than relying on a separate backlight. By varying the way that light output is modulated, the display can control its apparent brightness.
At sufficiently high frequencies, this flickering isn’t something most people can consciously detect. But some people are particularly sensitive to this kind of modulation and can experience eye strain, headaches and other forms of discomfort when looking at displays that use it.
This is a pretty good demonstration of PWM on an M4 iPad Pro, which also has a tandem OLED display:
Neither PWM nor the discussion surrounding it is new. It’s been a talking point for years, particularly around OLED displays, and something manufacturers have increasingly had to consider as display technology has evolved (including Apple — more on this later).
Notebookcheck, which conducted laboratory testing of the 14-inch MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display, measured PWM at 240Hz. This is its worst-case reading, with flickering detected at brightness settings of 99% and below.
To put that into perspective, the M4 Pro MacBook Pro with Mini-LED display measured 14,880Hz in Notebookcheck’s testing. At 240Hz, the brightness fluctuation repeats 240 times per second; at 14,880Hz, it repeats almost 15,000 times per second. That’s one hell of a difference. The reported modulation frequency of the new MacBook Pro’s OLED panel is roughly 62 times lower than that measured on the Mini-LED model.[1]
Frequency isn’t the only consideration, though. The extent to which brightness fluctuates during each cycle also matters, as do the characteristics of the display’s dimming system. We shouldn’t assume that these figures translate directly into how comfortable somebody will find a particular display. There’s no simple, one-size-fits-all formula for predicting who will experience problems.
It’s also worth avoiding the temptation to treat PWM as a universal explanation for every headache or instance of eye strain. Screen discomfort can have numerous causes. Some users’ discomfort may have nothing to do with PWM.[2]
Wait, isn’t OLED supposed to be better?
The MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display is emphatically not an inexpensive laptop with a budget screen. This is Apple’s flagship professional notebook. The PROLED XDR configuration represents a substantial investment, and the display is arguably its most important selling point. Apple has spent years refining its displays, and there’s a reasonable expectation that moving to OLED should represent some sort of improvement in almost every meaningful respect, especially given the savagely high price tag of these new machines.
PWM isn’t exclusive to OLED. LCD displays can use it, too, including Mini-LED displays. The difference is that OLED panels often use PWM as part of their brightness-control behaviour, and manufacturers have considerable flexibility in how they implement it. Some OLED displays use relatively high-frequency modulation; others use a combination of PWM and DC (Direct Current) dimming to manage brightness.
DC dimming generally involves reducing the current supplied to the display rather than relying entirely on rapidly switching the light output on and off. It’s often considered preferable for people who are sensitive to PWM. But the presence of DC dimming doesn’t necessarily mean PWM has been eliminated. The new MacBook Pro appears to use a combination of PWM and DC dimming. Notebookcheck notes that DC dimming is present across brightness levels, but that the panel still exhibits 240Hz flickering.
The people who didn’t have a problem before
One of the problems with PWM discussions is that they tend to quickly become highly polarised. Someone says they experience headaches, and someone else responds that they’ve been using OLED displays for years without any issues. Two things can be true at once.
The most important aspect of this discussion, to me, is the number of people insisting they had no difficulty using OLED devices prior to using this new MacBook. This matters because PWM sensitivity is often discussed as though it’s a straightforward, binary distinction between people who can tolerate OLED and people who can’t. That binary distinction doesn’t exist. Sensitivity exists along a spectrum.
Different displays use different modulation frequencies, amplitudes and dimming characteristics. Two OLED panels can behave differently, even if they’re based on broadly similar technology. Someone who has been happily using an OLED iPhone Pro for several years might find a different OLED screen uncomfortable; someone who has experienced headaches with one OLED laptop might have absolutely no problem with another. And there is a distinction between OLED technology itself and the particular implementation of OLED in this particular MacBook.
To give an example, someone on the PWM_Sensitive subreddit posted this about their experience with the new MacBook Pro (shared with their permission):
I was really surprised I was affected by this. Have been looking at various OLED screens for years and did not expect to have issues.
Then I got my PROLED 16 inch last week and during the first few hours I thought I just had a bad day. Now it’s so obvious. As soon as I look at the screen I get lightheaded, I feel like I am floating away … and when I then get up and start walking, I feel dizzy for like an hour or two, then it gets better again. But as soon as I look at the screen for more than a minute, the dizziness comes back.
This is first machine in 20 years as an Apple customer that I return literally anything. Too bad, because the laptop is amazing in all aspects otherwise, I was in love with it the moment I took it out of the box (coming from an M1 Pro 16 inch). It would have been my daily driver for the next 5 years. So, now I am waiting for the M7 variant and hope they will find a way to mitigate this issue. I am returning the MacBook tomorrow morning after less than a week of using it.
Apple has built up a loyal audience of professional Mac users who spend enormous amounts of money on the machines, and enormous amounts of time in front of their displays. Photographers, designers, developers, writers and video editors can spend entire working days looking at a MacBook screen (I do). So imagine upgrading from an M4 or M5 Pro MacBook Pro with Mini-LED, expecting a more beautiful version of something you already love, only to discover that you can’t comfortably use the new machine for more than a short period. Good customer experience is usually Apple's guiding North Star. Bad customer experience is usually Apple’s Kryptonite.
Has Apple compromised on the wrong thing?
There are plenty of engineering considerations involved in developing a tandem OLED panel for a laptop — brightness, power consumption, thermal management, longevity, colour accuracy and uniformity all have to be balanced.
Apple's displays have always involved trade-offs to one degree or another. Take the Mini-LED display — while it's truly excellent in many respects, it notoriously suffers from blooming (an inherent limitation of Mini-LED local dimming) and has also typically had poor pixel response times. These are compromises Apple has had to accept in order to deliver the particular combination of brightness, contrast and HDR performance that makes the Mini-LED display so impressive.
Apple has made some ambitious claims about the new PROLED XDR display, particularly around its brightness and the intelligence of its display engine. We don’t know why Apple has ended up with this particular dimming behaviour, or what trade-offs were involved in achieving it. But I think it’s reasonable to ask whether display comfort received enough consideration in the process. The new MacBook Pro produces extraordinary blacks, stunning HDR highlights and exceptionally accurate colours, and so on. But none of that matters particularly much if the person sitting in front of it develops a headache, or feels like they want to throw up when they're using it.
When the majority experience isn’t everyone’s experience
People experiencing unusual, poorly understood or relatively uncommon health problems have often found themselves struggling to be heard — they have to explain their symptoms repeatedly, defend their experiences and convince others that their difficulties are genuine. I don't personally know anybody who suffers from PWM sensitivity, and I appreciate that it can be difficult for someone without these experiences to understand how something so ordinary could become a source of physical discomfort for another person. But personal experience shouldn't be a prerequisite for compassion. We don’t need to experience somebody else’s symptoms to acknowledge that they are struggling.
Stories like this tend to surface only once an ill-defined and arbitrary threshold of people complaining is crossed. But even if something only affects a small minority, doesn’t that minority matter? Apple certainly appears to care about the minority — they put an extraordinary amount of work into accessibility features, many of which are incredibly important to relatively small groups of users. It’s one of the things which sets Apple apart. Features such as VoiceOver, AssistiveTouch and other accessibility technologies exist because Apple recognises that different people interact with technology in different ways. Why shouldn’t display comfort receive similar consideration? It doesn’t mean Apple must abandon OLED, or compromise the experience for everyone. It means offering alternatives where technically feasible.
Apple previously introduced a ‘Display Pulse Smoothing option’ on supported iPhone models. It’s an accessibility setting which can be toggled in the Settings app. Apple describes Display Pulse Smoothing as a way to create a smoother dimming experience at low brightness on supported iPhones. Its support documentation cautions that disabling PWM may affect low-brightness display performance under certain conditions. It took Apple a long time to include this feature by the way (it arrived nearly 8 years after the iPhone X was released). Incidentally, Apple has also included Display Pulse Smoothing on the new iPad mini with OLED display.
Could something similar be offered on the Mac? We shouldn’t assume that a software feature would completely resolve the issue. Changing the way the display is driven could introduce other compromises, and Apple would need to implement it carefully. But even a degree of user control would be gratefully received.
For now, anyone concerned about PWM sensitivity should be cautious about buying the new machine without trying it first. A quick demo in an Apple Store isn’t necessarily representative of spending an entire working day in front of the display. Don’t assume that being comfortable with an OLED iPhone means you’ll be comfortable with this MacBook. If you experience discomfort, don’t assume that increasing brightness or changing colour settings will necessarily resolve it.
The existing Mini-LED MacBook Pro thankfully remains a viable alternative for people who have found that display comfortable. But the Mini-LED MacBook Pro might not stay around for much longer. Apple looks to be transitioning to OLED across their devices — this may have consequences unless Apple changes the way they're implementing this particular technology.
The best MacBook display isn’t necessarily the best display for everyone
We sometimes get so caught up in the pursuit of better specifications that we forget what these devices are actually for. The most important part of a display is that it's something you sit in front of for hours at a time.
Displays to my mind are like mattresses — they're not something you should compromise on, unless financial constraints demand it. I've often been puzzled why people baulk at paying a lot for a display when they’re likely going to be using it for several hours a day. Similarly, I’ve often wondered why people baulk at paying a lot of money for a mattress, when they’re going to be spending roughly a third of their lives lying on one. A decent mattress is probably something you should invest in. The specs of the mattress matter, of course, and individual comfort matters enormously. The same thing is true with a display, which is why I have no issue having spent the money I did on a Studio Display XDR.
And this is what's so problematic with the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR display. According to many reviewers this is one of the best displays they've ever seen, and it has a price to match. Starting at £2899 for the 14-inch version (£3399 for the 16-inch), boy is it expensive. If the technology responsible for making the PROLED XDR Display look spectacular also makes it uncomfortable for some people to use, then we need to ask if that trade-off is worth it.
I suspect most people buying the new MacBook Pro will never think about PWM. They’ll open the lid, admire the display and get on with their lives. But for the people who have discovered that they can no longer comfortably use the latest MacBook Pro, this is a really big deal.
I’d like to see Apple explain why the new display behaves so differently from the Mini-LED panel used in other MacBook Pro models, and whether there’s any possibility of improving its dimming characteristics through software. Perhaps there’s a perfectly reasonable engineering explanation. Perhaps there are limitations inherent in this particular panel.
I’d be interested to hear from anyone who has experienced this first-hand, particularly those who have previously used Apple’s OLED iPhones or iPads without difficulty, but are now struggling with the new MacBook Pro.

A modern implementation of the Steamworks SDK for HTML/JS and NodeJS based applications.
I used greenworks for a long time and it's great, but I gave up for the following reasons.
- It's not being maintained anymore.
- It's not up to date.
- It's not context-aware.
- You have to build the binaries by yourself.
- Don't have typescript definitions.
- The API it's not trustful.
- The API implement callbacks instead of return flags or promises.
- I hate C++.
const steamworks = require('steamworks.js')
// You can pass an appId, or don't pass anything and use a steam_appid.txt file
const client = steamworks.init(480)
// Print Steam username
console.log(client.localplayer.getName())
// Tries to activate an achievement
if (client.achievement.activate('ACHIEVEMENT')) {
// ...
}
You can refer to the declarations file to check the API support and get more detailed documentation of each function.
To use steamworks.js you don't have to build anything, just install it from npm:
$: npm i steamworks.js
Steamworks.js is a native module and cannot be used by default in the renderer process. To enable the usage of native modules on the renderer process, the following configurations should be made on main.js:
const mainWindow = new BrowserWindow({
// ...
webPreferences: {
// ...
contextIsolation: false,
nodeIntegration: true
}
})
To make the steam overlay working, call the electronEnableSteamOverlay on the end of your main.js file:
require('steamworks.js').electronEnableSteamOverlay()
For the production build, copy the relevant distro files from sdk/redistributable_bin/{YOUR_DISTRO} into the root of your build. If you are using electron-forge, look for #75.
You only need to build if you are going to change something on steamworks.js code, if you are looking to just consume the library or use it in your game, refer to the installation section.
Make sure you have the latest node.js, Rust and Clang. We also need Steam installed and running.
Install dependencies with npm install and then run npm run build:debug to build the library.
There is no way to build for all targets easily. The good news is that you don't need to. You can develop and test on your current target, and open a PR. When the code is merged to main, a github action will build for all targets and publish a new version.
Go to the test/electron directory. There, you can run npm install and then npm start to run the Electron app.
Click "activate overlay" to test the overlay.
