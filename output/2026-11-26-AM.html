What happened
On November 19, 2026, 1Password began investigating indications that an attacker gained access to an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The actor used the account to access source code and push malicious code to backend services, which downloaded administrative data pertaining to our customers. This data included customer names and email addresses, in addition to non-secret internal metadata about the account. At no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker.
What information was affected
The attacker downloaded account records for a subset of our users. These records include:
- Names and email addresses
- Account and user identifiers, and account creation and last sign-in times
- Public keys (which are public by design, this has no impact to user security)
- Secure Remote Password protocol parameters (which cannot be used to access your account)
- Tokens present in certain emailed links for invitations, provisioning and account recovery
- Other non-secret account metadata relative to our internal architecture (e.g. version numeric identifiers, file numeric identifiers)
The attacker also copied source code from private repositories and gained access to our CI/CD system. Our source code is not customer data, and contains no user secrets. CI/CD contains infrastructure related secrets in order for our product to work. We have triggered a rotation of all credentials which may have been exposed, and there is no impact to 1Password’s services, availability, or product security.
When we send an email to a customer to accept a provisioning invite to an account, or to accept recovery of their account, it contains a token. A provisioning invite is specifically used for our SCIM Bridge or Hosted Provisioning. During this incident there were a small number of users in this state, who have been specifically notified. This token is used to complete the invitation or recovery process, but only when combined with an SSO sign-in or a six digit code sent to the user's registered email address. The token is not able to get access to the account by itself. In order to reduce the ability for a malicious third party to successfully guess the code, we’ve expanded the code from six numeric digits to ten alphanumeric digits. This makes brute force guessing practically impossible.
What was not affected
- Your vaults and the items in them
- The keys that protect your vaults
- 1Password does not store your account password or secret key
- Account recovery keys
- 1Password apps and browser extensions
What we’ve done
- Disabled the affected GitHub account, revoked the stolen token, and removed the access the attacker created.
- Rotated or revoked all internal credentials which may have been exposed.
- Lengthened the codes used in invitation and recovery emails from six digits to ten characters as a precautionary measure.
- Tightened internal GitHub, CI/CD, and endpoint controls, and added new detections for this kind of attack.
- Validated no internal evidence of user account takeovers or compromise using any exposed account provisioning tokens.
- Brought in independent cybersecurity experts to validate our containment approaches and remediation efforts.
What you can do
- Be cautious about phishing attempts in the form of unexpected emails, texts or calls that mention 1Password, invitations, or account recovery attempts.
- 1Password will never contact you to ask for your account password or Secret Key. Only enter them in the official 1Password app, or on our official website, and only when you’ve opened the app or typed the address yourself. Your Secret Key is only needed the first time you sign in on a new device or browser.
- Keep your Secret Key and account password safe.
- If you see something suspicious, tell us at support@1password.com.
Our commitment to you
You trust us with some of the most sensitive information you have. 1Password is designed so that a breach of our systems can’t expose your vault contents. Your vault secrets are encrypted with keys that only you hold, so we can’t access them, and neither can an attacker who gets into our servers.
We are committed to transparency in the interests of our customers' security and the security of the broader industry, which is why we have published and maintained our Security Whitepaper since 2015. We’ll keep this blog post current, and we’ll update it if we find anything that changes what we’ve said here.

7. A few questions for the guy who went snorkeling in a Home Depot retention pond

Article
One night earlier this fall, a biologist and YouTuber in Florida named Joseph Ricketts went snorkeling in a retention pond next to his local Home Depot.
He filmed what he saw and posted a video to his channel titled “Night Snorkel In a Home Depot Retention Pond.” It quickly blew up, hitting more than 2 million views as of this writing, and for good reason: It’s not at all what you might expect.
Ricketts found an aquatic world teeming with life on his dive into what is, from the surface, a stagnant pool of water filled with old beer cans. A water snake undulated through the pond, a bulge in its stomach revealing a recent meal. Tadpoles darted about, some of them half on their way to froghood. Mysterious shrimp scuttled along the silty bottom.
Ricketts, who works as a crocodile and alligator research biologist in Florida, went for his first snorkeling trip when he was 16 years old. On that trip, he came face-to-face with a manatee — and was instantly hooked. “I experienced so much joy in that moment, and I just kind of subconsciously knew I’d be chasing that for the rest of my life to some degree or another,” he told me.
Ricketts still takes a lot of joy in snorkeling, in part because of how it allows him to quickly access a totally different world hiding just under the surface of the water; his YouTube bio declares, “I’ll snorkel pretty much anywhere.” And his recent retention pond dive among the suburban detritus bears that out.
I talked with Ricketts recently about urban ecology, snorkeling, and what motivates a person to jump into the pond behind the Home Depot three miles from his house (his shortest-ever commute to a dive yet). Our interview has been edited for length and clarity.
Why snorkeling?
That’s a good question. I’m scuba certified, and I enjoy anything underwater, but snorkeling is just super accessible. I have a camera housing and a wet suit and other gear, but really all you need is just a mask and snorkel. As long as the water’s not freezing cold, you can get in there and take a look around. I really enjoy how, as soon as you immerse your head, it feels like you’re in a totally different world.
But the beautiful thing is that you’re not. You’re in the same world, it’s just this is the one that you didn’t have a vision into before. It’s kind of a meditative, calming practice. Just taking a look around at what is happening in nature and how things survive in their own environment is pretty awesome. I love it.
Okay, but why this pond?
I honestly had the idea for it a couple years ago. I’d see people out there birding with binoculars and telephoto lenses, and I started wondering if I could snorkel in there. We’ve had a drought so I couldn’t do it for a really long time, and when we finally got some rain I decided to go for it.
Tell me about the retention pond. What is it — and why is it in this Home Depot parking lot?
Florida has a lot of low-level, very wet natural ecosystems, but it’s also an extremely developed state. There are strip malls and roads and concrete everywhere! Natural wetland environments can soak up intense stormwater, but when you totally change environments and put in shopping centers and neighborhoods and pave everything with asphalt, you kind of lose that ability. That stormwater needs a place to go and collect — or it’s just going to flood parking lots and stores and houses.
So they have to put these retention ponds in; nowadays, you can’t build in certain areas unless you put in a retention pond. Not far from the Home Depot is a new-build neighborhood, and they have a retention pond that they had to put in, but they set it up with a little walkway for people that live in the neighborhood. That’s nice because there’s some wildlife that’s able to survive and live there, and people can enjoy this sort-of natural area. But it’s also not as good as the original thing that may have been there, you know?
So you were snorkeling in what’s essentially a replacement for a wetland that might once have been there.
I don’t know the history of that area exactly, but I imagine there was some form of wetland.
What was the most surprising thing that you found?
One thing that I found was a tadpole shrimp, which people call triops. It’s a freshwater crustacean that apparently is not native to Florida. When I saw it underwater, I had no context for what it is. As a biologist, people assume you just know wildlife, but people forget how much wildlife there is in the world! I know a lot about alligators and crocodiles, but there’s still a ton I don’t know. I think it’s important to sometimes be able to be like: “I don’t know what I’m seeing or why I’m seeing it” — and then hopefully I can learn about it.
But anyway, I saw this thing, I had no clue what it was, and I asked a bunch of my coworkers about it but they had no clue what it was either. So then I had to do some research online, which is how I finally figured it out.
“I think that was kind of a personal challenge I had for myself: Can I see something beautiful in a retention pond?”
Apparently they are native to the Midwest. They lay their eggs in soil, and then during times of rain, they really quickly hatch and feed and grow, and then they reproduce, lay more eggs, and then wait for the next cycle while it’s dry. It’s a real quick boom-and-bust life cycle. It’s not a native species, but I think there’s just a couple areas in this state where you can find them — and one just happens to be right there in the retention pond.
I saw that there were also more common animals there, like frogs, snakes, and dragonflies. But how do you think they all got into this parking-lot pond?
A lot of frog species adapt to urban environments and find any bit of water they can. They were probably used to a forest and wetland, and now they’ve had to adapt to what’s available currently, which is pretty limited.
I think that’s true for most of the animals you find in there. The natural world is pretty resilient, though sometimes I think we give it too much credit for how resilient it can be.
What do you mean by that?
Life survives, but the way things originally were does not necessarily survive, and I think that’s something that’s really worth protecting. The way some people interpreted the video was like: ”Wow, look at all this life persisting even in this place!” And it was amazing and there is beauty there, but you can’t just accept that and then get rid of all our natural wetlands. We need untouched, expansive ecosystems.
It can be cool to be excited about this little retention pond and what lives in here, but coupled with that also needs to be excitement for protecting huge untouched habitat as well. Both are full of worthy things.
Do you think the retention pond, now that it’s there, is worth preserving?
I think so. One thing I didn’t really get to dive into with the video too much is the idea of a nature desert. I don’t know if I’m making that term up, but it’s kind of related like a food desert: Depending on your demographic or where you’re born, that might be the only bit of nature you have access to. It’s a privilege to be able to travel to a national park or a big state park or something, and not everyone has that opportunity. Your only exposure to nature could be a retention pond that’s in the middle of a city somewhere. And so I do think those places are very worth protecting, because I think it’s important that regardless of your background, if you’re a kid, you have a place that you can go to and feel safe and be exposed to wilderness even if it’s very small urban wilderness.
Do you think people should be snorkeling in more retention ponds?
[Laughs] I don’t recommend that people do that! There are some retention ponds where you could encounter some nasty stuff.
It was definitely a little bit of a stunt. But I also want to help people think about ecological ideas that they might not otherwise think of, while still enjoying the watch.
Another thing I want to get at is so many people responded like: “That’s gross, how could you do that?” But my question is why are we just totally okay with having these huge bodies of water that are gross and contaminated in our everyday lives? Clean water should be the norm!
Still, it’s a place that should be valued and thought of differently.
Do you feel like your own perception of urban ecology or urban environments changed after this?
I think so. That’s another reason why I love snorkeling: I feel like somehow each time I do it, it changes me a little bit. I didn’t know that much life was out there, and I didn’t know what a lot of those creatures were when I first got in. It deepens my personal connection with my local Home Depot in a way I hadn’t really thought would happen five years ago, but here we are.
Do you think you’ll do more urban snorkeling? I know you just went to the Dry Tortugas, which is a very different environment from Home Depot.
I think I am going to look into it. I’m not going to become the retention pond snorkeling guy; I hope to diversify my snorkeling more than that. But I definitely think this is an idea that resonates with people, and it gives me an opportunity to explore some interesting ecological and even philosophical concepts.
I’ve got some other ideas that I’ve been waiting for the right time to film. There’s a place up in the Carolinas that’s a Frisbee golf course with a little creek running through it, and there are these amazing minnow spawning events that happen in the creek, so maybe one day I’ll be out there in the creek filming minnow spawning while people are playing Frisbee golf nearby.
What are you hoping people take away from the video?
One thing that I hope people take away from a lot of my videos is just a sense of wonder at what’s out there in the natural world, and maybe a broader definition of beauty. I think beauty can be found in unexpected places and from unexpected creatures. I think that was kind of a personal challenge I had for myself: Can I see something beautiful in a retention pond? And do I have the skill with my camera to present that so that maybe other people catch a little glimmer of it too? And I think it happened, which is really exciting.
But at the same time, I hope people realize how important it is that big expansive habitats are protected and conserved too, and that it’s not just about the big animals like polar bears and manatees, which are kind of easy to care about, but also little rare invertebrates or spiders or snakes, all of which I believe have inherent value and are worth protecting.

In Bernhardt’s retelling of events, the date is worked back from 2035, however unsurprisingly, large language models have rapidly sped up the timeline. What was once perceived to be the end-state at 2035 has arrived years earlier and you can witness it unfolding at trynix.dev.
One of the most impressive creations I’ve managed to build leveraging nixpkgs-multiverse is https://trynix.dev.
It still baffles my mind that I can run any Nix package, over 300,000 distinct package versions, straight from my browser. Truly insane capability. I woke on November 20th and saw Domen Kožar sharing a single message in our TacoSprint Discord channel: unkernel.dev
It was the fast browser Linux experience I had sought to achieve, but with Nix.
Aside What is unkernel? It is Linux compiled to WebAssembly, allowing the kernel and system components to run at wasm speed while only user programs are emulated. It was published by Pooya Parsa on November 19th, 2026.
In an AI induced manic frenzy, I adopted the new engine along with a slough of long-wished features almost immediately.
The proof is in the pudding: unkernel delivers remarkable speed improvements over qemu-wasm,11With a very healthy set of patches applied to make it even faster. especially on first runs where kernel work dominates. The new engine is able to deliver 2x to 32x faster performance depending on the workload.22The benchmark page tracks both engines over time, measured through the real page and shell.
JavaScript & WebAssembly nirvana.

Windows. A program’s Wayland surfaces are composited in the page by wland, and they float over the terminal you started them from.

Sound. The VM has an ALSA sound card, and can play audio through your browser.

An open, minimal sEMG wristband, like the Meta Neural Band. Eight bipolar surface-EMG channels around the wrist, a 24-bit biopotential front end, Bluetooth LE, and a module the size of a small watch face — designed from first principles to record data good enough to train gesture models, and cheap enough to just build.
Status: design complete, not yet built. Schematics, both PCBs (DRC-clean), mechanics, firmware (builds and links, untested on hardware), recorder and analysis code are done; everything below that says "simulated" or "estimated" has not been measured on a real board yet. See what's next.
| Channels | 8 bipolar (P–N 20 mm along the forearm) + a driven bias pad in every pod |
| Front end | TI ADS1298, 24 bit, gain 6, 4 kSPS acquisition, ~1.2 µVrms (20–850 Hz) |
| Electrodes | 24 dry 6.5 mm ENIG gold pads on a 2-layer polyimide flex, foam-backed, 1 mm proud |
| Radio | nRF52832, BLE 5 2M PHY, printed antenna; 2 kSPS × 8 ch, 16-bit stream |
| IMU | LSM6DSL accel + gyro, 208 Hz |
| Power | 401223-class LiPo, about 130 mAh, ~12 h streaming (estimate); 2-pin magnetic charging connector in the module's end wall (charge with the band off the wrist) |
| Module | 31.6 × 37.1 × 9.65 mm body (48 mm across the strap bars), tapered and crowned, one size for 145–180 mm wrists |
| Strap | 20 mm hook-and-loop watch strap through two thick bars moulded into the base; the electrode pods snap onto it |
| Cost | ≈ $290–350 for a first build: 5 boards (2 assembled), 5 flexes, prints and off-the-shelf parts (breakdown) |

Like Meta's research band (A generic non-invasive neuromotor interface for human-computer interaction, Nature 2025), it has what matters: bipolar gold contacts 20 mm apart along the forearm, µV-level noise, 2 kHz data. It drops what only costs money: per-pod amplifiers, 16 channels, custom electrodes. The reasoning, decision by decision, is in docs/rev_c/README.md.
Four independent reviews and simulations went into this design:
- Signal chain (docs/science/signal_chain.md): noise budget, dry-contact impedance, mains pickup, sampling and BLE throughput, with pass/fail bench gates.
- Circuit simulation (docs/science/simulations.md, ngspice): bias-loop stability (57° worst-case phase margin), mains residual (≤ 1.5 µV on battery), RF filtering, radio-burst coupling — which is why the bias reference is filtered.
- Forearm volume-conductor model (docs/science/emg_forward_model.md): a 3-D finite-difference model of the distal forearm (bones, muscles, fat, skin, anisotropic muscle), lead fields of all 8 channels, travelling motor-unit action potentials. 8 channels separate single muscles at 98–100 %; pad size, spacing and band rotation are quantified.
- Data & models (docs/science/data_and_models.md): what 8-channel wrist EMG can decode, how to use Meta's open datasets, a first-week recording protocol, and a working pipeline.
Honest expectations (per user, with calibration): 6–8 hand/wrist postures at ~90–98 %, pinches and thumb swipes at ~75–90 %, 2-axis wrist control. Not expected: models that work across people without calibration, typing, fluent handwriting.
Build guide: docs/build/assembly.md: the shopping list, the prints, ten illustrated steps, troubleshooting and the tests to run on a first band (also as a PDF). There is also a 36 s assembly animation.
A first prototype costs around $300 (≈ $290–350 for five main boards with two assembled, five electrode flexes, the prints and the off-the-shelf parts; breakdown). Most of it is the ADS1298 and the fab's assembly fees.
- Order two things:
- the assembled main board, the electrode flex and the prints, with Buy it in Backplane
(boards/neurband_c/ORDERING.md, boards/neurband_c_flex/ORDERING.md, mech/rev_c/*.fab.json);
- about EUR 75 of off-the-shelf parts (docs/build/sourcing.md): a 20 mm hook-and-loop watch strap, 6 mm foam dots, a 401223 LiPo, a 2-pin magnetic charging connector and cable, a Pico 2 as the SWD probe and an nRF52840 dongle.
- Build it (docs/build/assembly.md, PDF alongside):
- flash the bare board once through four soldered wires on the SWD pads (3V3, SWDIO, SWDCLK, GND);
- solder the cell and the charging connector;
- bond the six free pods in the flat jig and the base in the pedestal jig;
- fold the tail into J1 and close the module;
- snap the strap into the pods, lock one end on bar A, tighten through bar B.
Design details are in docs/mech/mechanical_c.md.
- Record: python3 host/neurband_ble.py -o rec.npz, and in a second terminal python3 -m analysis collect basic --out data/s1.
- Check and train: python3 -m analysis report data/*.npz, python3 -m analysis train data/*.npz.
Try the whole pipeline without hardware: python3 -m analysis synth /tmp/nb.
Safety. Wear it only while it runs on its own battery. Never connect a charger, USB cable, debugger or grounded instrument while the pads touch skin. This is a research prototype, not a medical device.
