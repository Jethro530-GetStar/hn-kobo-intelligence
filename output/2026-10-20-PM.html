Article
Three armies surround a city. If they all attack together, they win; if they all retreat, they live. If they split, the ones who attack are wiped out. Their only way to talk is to send riders through enemy lines.
Read9 min
ForAnyone curious; no background needed
HowScroll, and tap where it asks
Puzzle 1 · The lost messenger
Everyone is honest. Messengers can vanish.
Puzzle 2 · The liar
Every messenger arrives. Someone lies.
Can the loyal generals ever be certain they will act as one? The story above hides two different problems, and computer scientists study them separately. In the first, called the Two Generals problem, everyone is honest but riders can vanish. In the second, every rider gets through but someone is lying; that is the Byzantine Generals problem itself. Both have surprising answers. We’ll take them one at a time, but first, the rules.
Puzzle 1 · The lost messenger
“Got it.” “Got your ‘got it.’” …
Start with just two generals, A and B, both completely honest. A wants to attack at dawn, but only if B attacks too. The only danger is the valley between their camps: a rider sent across it can be captured, and his general never finds out.
Predict
General A sends “Attack at dawn.” General B sends back “Got it.” Both riders happen to get through, but each general knows only about the rider who reached him. Can each general now be certain that the other will attack?
(c) is right, and it surprises most people. B sent the last rider, so he can’t know whether “Got it” arrived. If it was captured, A would still be wondering whether B ever got the order, and might hold back. So B can’t be sure A will attack.
For (a): A did get the confirmation, but B can’t know that, and a general who isn’t sure his partner will attack may hold back. So A can’t fully count on B either. For (b): the extra confirmation can be captured too, and then A is the one in doubt. Whoever sends the last rider is always unsure. Scroll on to watch it happen.

Answer the question above to start.
A’s rider gets through, so B knows the plan. But A has no way to know that. For all A can tell, his rider is lying in a ditch.
So B sends a rider back with “Got it.” When it arrives, A can relax. Now B is the one wondering whether his rider made it. If it didn’t, A may hold back, and B would attack alone.
So A sends a rider to confirm the confirmation. Now B can relax, and A is the one left wondering. See the pattern?
Your turn. Tap Send another rider as often as you like. Can you find the rider that ends the doubt?
There isn’t one. Whoever sends the last rider can never know that it arrived, so certainty would take an endless chain of confirmations. This was the first problem in computer communication to be proven unsolvable.
Real networks, including the internet’s TCP protocol, don’t solve it either. They live with it and shrink the doubt, much like sending a hundred riders: failure becomes very unlikely, but never impossible.
Puzzle 2 · The liar
The liar in the ranks
Now flip the danger around. We’re back to three generals, but this time the roads are safe.
A messenger can be captured on the way. (No longer true.)
Every messenger arrives, carrying his message exactly as it was given to him.
The general who receives a message always knows which camp sent it.
Each general knows when a message is due. If one he’s expecting hasn’t arrived by then, he knows it isn’t coming. That deadline comes back at the end.
Messengers are still the only way to communicate. The danger now is a general who lies.
One general is the commander. He decides, and sends his order by messenger to the other two, his lieutenants.
To double-check, each lieutenant sends the other a messenger saying which order he got. With nobody lying, both attack, and both goals on the card are met.
Predict
Now every messenger arrives, but one of the three generals might be a traitor who lies. The two loyal generals can send each other as many messengers as they like. Can they always end up doing the same thing?
(c). That surprises almost everyone; the 1982 paper that named the problem calls it “deceptively simple” and the result surprising. Two-against-one only works if you know which one is the liar, and as the next section shows, a loyal general can’t. For (b): the loyal generals don’t know who the traitor is, so their plan has to work whoever it is, including the commander.
Through Lieutenant 1’s eyes
Here is how one traitor beats two loyal generals. Put yourself in one lieutenant’s boots.
Answer the question above to start.
You’re Lieutenant 1. The strip at the top shows every message that has reached you, and the two don’t agree. One of the other two generals is lying. Which one?
Maybe Lieutenant 2 is the traitor. Then the commander is loyal, and goal 2 says you must follow his order. You attack.
Or maybe the commander is the traitor, giving each lieutenant a different order. Then Lieutenant 2 is loyal, and is honestly passing on the order he got.
You get the same two messages, word for word, in both worlds, so nothing you receive can tell you which world you’re in. You must attack in the first world, so you attack in the second one too.
Explain
Lieutenant 2 is in the mirror image of your spot: the commander’s message to him says RETREAT, and your message to him says the commander said ATTACK. Using the same reasoning you just used, what must Lieutenant 2 do? Think it through, then reveal.
Retreat. From where Lieutenant 2 stands, it looks exactly like a world where the commander loyally ordered RETREAT and Lieutenant 1 is the liar, and in that world goal 2 says he must follow the commander. So in the second world, Lieutenant 1 attacks and Lieutenant 2 retreats. The traitor commander has split the two loyal generals by telling them different things, and neither can catch him.
The trap is symmetry. Each loyal lieutenant is doing the right thing given the messages he has, and that’s exactly what lets the traitor split them.
So in the second world you attack and Lieutenant 2 retreats. The loyal generals have split, and goal 1 fails. Two loyal generals couldn’t outvote one liar, because neither could tell who the liar was. And a smarter rule wouldn’t save them: the full proof shows that whatever rule the lieutenants agree on in advance, one traitor among three can break one of the two goals, either by splitting the loyal generals or by making them disobey a loyal commander.
Add a fourth general
Now add a third lieutenant, and keep the same moves. The commander sends his order to each lieutenant. Each lieutenant then sends the other two a messenger reporting the order he got. Finally, each lieutenant goes with the majority of the three versions he now holds. Walk through two cases, then put on the traitor’s mask yourself.
However you play the traitor, the loyal lieutenants agree. A lying lieutenant is outvoted by two true copies of the order. A lying commander can send different orders to different lieutenants, but because the others relay honestly, every loyal lieutenant ends up holding the same three values, so they all reach the same majority.
That turns out to be a general rule. When a liar can put anything he likes in a message, agreement can be guaranteed only if more than two-thirds of the generals are loyal. Three generals with one traitor falls short; four is enough.
Call the number of traitors f and the number of generals n. For the loyal generals to be guaranteed to agree, no matter what the traitors do, you need:
n ≥ 3f + 1

Apply
A system has 10 computers, and they send ordinary messages, so a liar can claim anything. At most how many of them can be traitors, with agreement still guaranteed?
(b) 3. You need more than two-thirds loyal. With 3 traitors, 7 of 10 are loyal (70%). With 4 traitors only 6 of 10 are (60%), and that’s not enough. In the rule’s terms, 10 computers ≥ 3 × 3 + 1.
“Just under half” is the voting instinct, but a traitor doesn’t cast one honest vote: he can tell different people different things, and that’s why a plain majority isn’t enough. For (c) and (d): one traitor needs only 4 generals, and with 10 there’s room for more.
The way out
How real systems get out of the trap
This puzzle came out of NASA-funded research into making computers reliable enough to fly airliners. Engineers have never beaten the impossibility. Instead, they change the rules of the game, in one of three ways.
Unforgeable seals
Change
Every order carries a seal nobody can forge. In computers, that’s a digital signature.
You get
Agreement despite any number of traitors. Even three generals are enough, because a two-faced commander’s seal turns up on two different orders.
The price
Every general needs a seal that can’t be faked, and every message still has to arrive.
Clocks and deadlines
Change
Assume messages arrive within a known time. That’s the deadline from Puzzle 2’s rules, and it’s how a missing rider gets noticed: if he isn’t there by the deadline, he isn’t coming.
You get
Systems like PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, 1999) never give conflicting answers, even when the network is slow, and they keep making progress whenever messages arrive on time. They need 3f + 1 machines: the generals’ two-thirds rule again.
The price
If delays can be unlimited, no method can promise to ever finish, even if just one machine might crash. That was proven in 1985.
Settle for almost certain
Change
Votes count by computing power instead of by identity. That’s Bitcoin’s “proof of work.”
You get
≈0.09%chance that an attacker with 10% of the computing power reverses a payment 5 blocks deep. More blocks, smaller odds; never zero.
The price
It holds only while honest computers control most of the computing power.
Predict
A crypto fan says: “Bitcoin solved the Byzantine Generals problem. Once your payment is confirmed, it’s mathematically impossible to reverse.” Which version is accurate?
(b). Bitcoin didn’t beat the impossibility. It changed the question from “certain” to “overwhelmingly likely”. In the original paper’s numbers, an attacker with 10% of the computing power has about a 0.09% chance of reversing a payment that’s 5 blocks deep, and waiting longer makes it smaller but never zero.
For (c): the protection is real and grows fast. It just isn’t absolute. Every escape route works this way: it changes an assumption rather than breaking the rule.
What to remember
If messages can be lost, two sides can never be certain they agree. Every confirmation needs another.
If messages arrive but someone may lie, agreement is guaranteed only when more than two-thirds are loyal: 4 generals to survive 1 traitor.
Real systems don’t beat these limits. They change the rules: unforgeable signatures, deadlines, or “almost certain.”
So can the generals ever be certain? Only by changing the rules of the game, and only for as long as those rules hold.

A brief digression from my usual teaching posts to complain about a devastating regression in iPadOS 27.
If you’ve followed my work up until now, you’ll know that I rely heavily on writing on handouts from my iPad, projected via AirPlay to an external screen that my students can see. When I’m doing this, my iPad is also doing many other things: controlling a PowerPoint presentation on my Mac, showing me notes that I’ve taken on points I want to mention and students I want to call on, and showing me the results of polls before I reveal them to the class. This ability to do other things on your iPad while the audience just sees your presentation or handout is an essential part of a variety of pedagogical moves that I use in my teaching.
Unfortunately, Apple just completely broke it.
The problem
As of iPadOS 27, if you have multiple windows open while AirPlaying (whether or not Stage Manager is on), tapping on anything other than the presenting app will snap the external display into showing your whole iPad screen. This means that my students see all my notes on who I want to call on, or poll results that should be hidden from them. They also get distracted from what we’re learning by all these jarring windows popping up and disappearing. If you’re presenting at a big conference, it’s extremely embarrassing.
In GoodNotes, the presenter view will return once you tap back on the app. In Keynote, it will just exit the presentation entirely. In PowerPoint, it will put the app in a limbo where you have to restart the presentation to proceed. Here’s a Reddit post illustrating the behavior in GoodNotes. But don’t just take my word for it; try it at home!
I sort of understand why Apple did this. In iPadOS 26, which app could “control” the external display was hard to identify. Essentially, you had to swipe up as though going to the home screen, then tap on the app you wanted in the dock, and that app would take over the external display if it had a presentation mode. This was weird and hard to understand, but Apple’s solution is to just make it so that whatever is the most recently tapped app takes over, which is much, much worse.
Ask what you can do
As for what the behavior should be, I’m not a developer, but here’s a proposal: if you’re using an app that is in presenter mode (e.g., PowerPoint playing a presentation, or a GoodNotes document open), it should always get precedence on the external display, until that presentation gets stopped, the document is closed, or the app is quit. Period.
I talked/griped/whined/begged about this on an episode of Mac Power Users in early September (here’s a link to the video), and as of now this behavior hasn’t changed, including in the latest betas. I’m hoping that this post can get the word out, or that maybe someone has figured out a workaround. I’ve also filed a feedback report with Apple and encourage you to do the same (click here and mention my feedback code FB23615646).
I’ll update this post if anything changes, but for now, I can’t recommend this operating system to anyone using their iPad to teach or present.

Something funny happened a little while ago: I read about FreshRSS, and it sounded nice, so I installed it on a server and started using it. That would not have happened a year ago, but then again, a year ago I didn’t have any NixOS servers.
For the past few years, my rss feeds have lived locally on my phone only. There was a good idea behind that1 I can mostly take time to read when I’m away from my computer anyway., but it never quite worked in practice. For one, many things are easier to read on a larger screen. But it was also brittle to trust a phone app to manage the read/unread status of thousands of articles across a hundred feeds.2 In particular, when I switched to a different phone I could migrate all feeds to it, but the read/unread status was lost along the way. What I really wanted was a server-side feed aggregator. This is software that runs on a server, manages rss feeds, and then I can read the rss feeds either through a web interface, or through client applications. I’ve wanted that for a while, but I’ve never taken the step, because it would be yet another side project to maintain the deployment of.
But now, with some NixOS experience, adding a working FreshRSS configuration
(including backups) took 9 lines of Nix code3 Plus a dozen more lines for the
nginx reverse proxy configuration. It got a little complicated because I wanted
to proxy_pass from a specific location block which required rewriting cookie
paths. But that’s a me problem, not a NixOS problem.. That installation has
just hands-off worked for the past few months. If or when that server breaks, I
can spin up a new one running the exact same configuration, probably in
something like 15 minutes. When we can do something like that, there is
literally no reason to not just try the new software.
Contrast this against how FreshRSS is installed outside of NixOS. Then we’d
- download and extract an archive to the right location;
- configure permissions according to instructions;
- install nginx and php-fpm with necessary extra dependencies to the server;
- configure php-fpm pools and make nginx pass requests through to php-fpm;
- set up Let’s Encrypt certificates;
- set up backups; and
- hopefully remember how to do all the above if something breaks and we need to reinstall FreshRSS on a new or upgraded server later.
Of course, the FreshRSS project provides a container image which would shorten the process a little bit. It purports to set up Apache with mod_php and configure them the right way. Then we’d only need to configure backups, create a systemd service that runs the container, and manage nginx as a tls terminating reverse proxy in front of that, because the container image reasonably does not come with tls support. This is still stuff to remember, and there’s also no guarantee the same thing works six months down the line when reinstalling, unless we wrote down which exact container image we downloaded.4 It does not help to build the container image from source, because two builds from the same Dockerfile separated by time can result in rather different container images.
I have mentioned before how much I dislike maintaining side projects. That includes my own code, but also self-hosted services developed by someone else. I’m okay with a few minutes of maintenance here and there, but having to schedule hours of uninterrupted work to upgrade something, or spending any amount of time at all on emergency maintenance, really kills my desire to work with computers.
You’d think someone of my disposition would be deep into configuration
management, container orchestration, or both, but no. I have made honest
attempts with CFEngine, Ansible, podman, and k3s but none of them improved my
installation/configuration/deployment experience significantly over (a)
carefully writing things down, and sometimes (b) turning those careful notes
into a shell script performing a sequence of ssh commands.5 Yes, this is
essentially reinventing Ansible – but less brittle. It won’t break the next time
someone glances sideways at pip. The drawback of the shell script is that it
often ends up relying on assumptions that become invalid with time, or when it
is used in a different environment. A NixOS configuration reads a lot like the
careful instructions to myself, except they’re executable and they produce the
same result every time.
