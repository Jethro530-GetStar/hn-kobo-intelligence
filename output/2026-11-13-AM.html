Where the evidence comes from
From a 2016 review of 138 trials with 19,951 people (Harkin and others, Psychological Bulletin). In each trial, some people were prompted to keep track of a goal and others weren't. On average, the people who were prompted did better at reaching it.
- Keeping track helped more when other people could see it (0.55) or when it was reported to someone (0.47) than when it stayed private (0.19).
- It helped more when people kept a written record (0.43) than when they didn't (0.29).
The numbers are effect sizes: bigger means it helped more.
Nearly all the trials were about health goals, like weight or blood pressure. Nobody has tested red lines for AI, so read this as a guide, not a promise. Writing a line down doesn't mean you'll keep it. These steps make it more likely.

Los Alamos
1944
The one who left
Joseph Rotblat was a Polish physicist working in Liverpool when the war began. In 1944 he went to Los Alamos with the British team on the Manhattan Project. He had one reason for being there: the fear that Nazi Germany would build an atomic bomb first.
By the end of 1944 it was clear Germany would not. The reason he had come no longer held, so he resigned and went back to Liverpool. He is the only scientist known to have left the project on grounds of conscience.
Most of us never write down why we're somewhere. When things change slowly, nothing tells us to stop. Rotblat had that moment because he knew his reason. This page started as The Rotblat Line, to help you write down yours: the specific things that would make you act, and what you'd do, whether that's quitting, speaking up or finally getting involved. He was born on 4 November, so that's the day it suggests re-reading your red lines each year.

5. Tim Peters has died
FULL TEXT
1373 points · 172 comments · ayhanfuat
discuss.python.org
Best #2 · Top #35

Article
I wrote this some time ago about Tim:
“”"
I worked with Tim and the rest of Python Labs for a couple of years in the early 2000s. Tim was a hugely positive influence on me throughout my Python career.
Before I had ever spoken to Tim, he was already legendary. He had worked for Cray Research writing compilers for supercomputers, and for Dragon Systems, writing speech-to-text software at a time when such a task was considered largely impossible. He took up Python on a lark in its very early days, becoming something like Python’s second real user after Guido.
Guido came to rely on Tim heavily. Tim will demur about this, but as far as I can tell, Tim and Guido were largely partners in the design evolution of very early Python, and before most important features made it in to Python, you could be sure that they had been examined from every angle, shaken, stirred, and thunk about very hard by both of them.
Tim often played the role of gatekeeper and polisher. You have Tim to thank at least partially if you were a refugee from Java or Microsoft languages, which made excuses for their sprawling verbosity, complexity, and poor readability. Python, at the time, made none of those and had none of that (it now does). Tim was crucial in ensuring it stayed that way during his most active years. He will claim differently, but, by my observation, it is no accident that Python ballooned in complexity after his exit as day-to-day core developer. Perhaps this was more due to the resignation of Guido as BDFL and the new Steering Council model, which was also coincident, but not having Tim around didn’t help either.
It was pure joy to read Tim’s Usenet and mail list posts, which were full of humor and good nature and measured positivity and not a little winking darkness. They somehow resonated with me on my journey from becoming a new programmer to a not-so-new programmer and they lit a path to becoming one on which you could be dramatically competent without becoming cryptic and insufferable. He was intensely knowledgeable, and unfailingly precise when arguing for or against some design change. But he was always gentle and patient and matter-of-fact, preferring to use humor and warmth rather than bile and authority as the currency in which he dealt.
Tim penned “the Zen of Python” (“beautiful is better than ugly…”) which can be seen via “import this” to this day. You can see a bit of his wonderful humor and style in the prelude to his introduction of the poem . His enthusiasm was infectious, and in the days when he was most involved, most everyone could be witnessed spouting Tim-isms (winks and “'lys” and whatnot).
When I did get the chance to work with Tim, he was just as influential. He had absolutely no pretensions. If I hadn’t already known him to be a computer savant, I would have assumed he was a biker or mechanic due to his long grey locks, full mustache and and working-class fashion sense. I would later find out that he had indeed worked at Harley-Davidson. I think he’s originally from Wisconsin, but an East- or West-Coaster not fluent in Midwest tongues could be excused for believing his disarming accent is Chicagoan. He seemed just like a regular-looking neighborhood punch-the-clock guy, not unlike all the mechanics and deliverymen and cooks and dishwashers and event staff I’d worked around as a kid and in my 20s. Except he was pretty much the smartest guy in every room he entered.
Tim’s demeanor and lack of pretense inspired me to think that despite my own lack of education and pedigree, with enough effort and grace, that I might become a half-competent programmer. And eventualy I did. But before meeting Tim, it was not a given. I was alienated by the pretensions of my community peers, and I was often reminded that they were not necessary only by Tim.
He was somewhat private. He could often be found with a pen and paper outside the building just standing alone, staring blankly off into space, presumably thinking about some mind-boggling algorithmic topic with which he had just been charged by our CTO, or perhaps mentally designing some aspect of a pet project, scribbling something every few minutes. He applied himself to our code, which included a custom database written in C and Python. He diagnosed and fixed a data-destroying bug in that byzantine codebase for our largest customer. This saved the account, and likely gave us enough runway for another year or so.
Everyone who worked there was in awe of his programming and debugging skills, and while we tried our best to let the poor man be, we often asked him questions, many of which were pedestrian and dull. I remember one of our teammates trying to engage him into a debugging session, claiming that some fault seemed to happen every six runs. He smiled winkingly and asked “have you grepped the source for six?” It was a minor jab but given the sincerity of the question, and the person from which it had come, it occurred to me at the time that it might not be the worst theory of debugging from which to start.
Tim was a Director of the Python Software Foundation from 2001 to 2014, bringing Python across an uncanny valley from a time where it was “interesting but ignorable” to “the planet’s default language” along with a cast of other incredible people. If you use Python, it’s not in small part due to Tim.
When I started my own open source projects, it was Tim whom I channeled during design discussions and sprints. I quite consciously used tricks I had learned from him to make the process fun and welcoming. Rather than become prickly about some technical conflict, I would try to use low-brow humor to defuse things and I would try very hard to learn everything I could about the topic before responding such that I could gain and rely on the respect of other contributors. I often failed, of course, because I’m not Tim, but I was at least a twentieth a Tim, and at the time, I think my co-contributors would have agreed that the projects I led were fun and worthwhile to work on.
It is in this way that Tim’s influence is likely most obvious to people today, because I’m not the only one who channeled him; any Python project started between 1995 and 2010 has a bit of Tim culture in it.
“”"
In sadness,
. C

Frequently asked questions
What is Claude Fable 5.2?
+
Claude Fable 5.2 is a frontier model from Anthropic that can work autonomously on long coding tasks. Given one detailed prompt, it can plan, write, test and refine a complete game over several hours of agentic work, with no human writing code in between. Every game in this arcade was produced that way.
How do single-file HTML 3D games work?
+
Everything ships inside one .html file: markup, CSS, JavaScript, shaders, procedurally generated textures and synthesized audio. 3D games draw through WebGL (often with an inlined copy of Three.js), sound comes from the Web Audio API, and the levels, models and music are generated in code at runtime rather than loaded as separate assets. Open the file in any modern browser and it runs, offline, without a server or build step.
What is a one-shot prompt?
+
A one-shot prompt is a single instruction given to the model at the start: the design brief, constraints and acceptance criteria all in one message. The model then builds autonomously. Where the original or a faithful reproduction is available, open the “View Prompt” tab on any game to read and copy it.
Is it safe to play these games in my browser?
+
Yes. Games we host are served from a sandboxed endpoint with a Content-Security-Policy sandbox and an iframe sandbox that allow scripts and pointer lock only, so game code cannot read this site’s cookies or storage, open pop-ups, or navigate the page. Games marked “Author-hosted” are embedded from the creator’s own website in a sandboxed iframe on their own origin, which keeps them isolated from this site too.
Can I download, modify or remix a game?
+
Every game has a “Download .html” button and a source inspector. Because each one is a single file, you can open it in any editor, change it and run it locally straight away. Please respect the original author’s license and give credit when you share a remix.
How do I submit my own Fable 5.2 game?
+
Use the Submit page: upload your .html file (up to 10 MB), add the prompt and controls, and link to your post. No account is needed. Submissions go live after a quick review.

ParseBench-2 is a document parsing benchmark that combines broad coverage of real-world documents with severity-aware metrics. It has the following characteristics:
- Six capability dimensions. Text, formatting, tables, charts, visual grounding (layout) and forms. The new Forms dimension covers 600 form pages.
- Severity-aware metrics. Each page score is the lower of a parsing score and a severity cap, so even a page that is 99% correct is still identified correctly.
- Calibrated against stated priorities. The metrics are tuned against 330 controlled scenarios in six severity test suites. Each scenario has a target score drop, and the severity levels are critical, major and minor, with benign variations that should cost nothing.
- Over 140 error types. The metrics check for changed facts, broken structural relationships, missing content, invented content and more. The evaluation reports per-error diagnostics for every page.
- 4,317 page-level test cases from 2,645 source documents. The test cases are the revised ParseBench-1 pages plus 2,293 new cases that target hard situations: complex table headers, financial and insurance tables, dense tables, headings, strikeout, underline and government forms.
- Evaluation code suite. The scorers ship with the open-source parse-bench package.
ParseBench-2 keeps most pages from ParseBench-1 and revises their annotations so the metrics can compare complete outputs. It also adds targeted datasets for cases that sparse checks miss.
- Text. Omissions, hallucinations, reading order and changed facts. A flipped negation or a misread digit caps the page score even when the rest of the text is intact.
- Formatting. Formatting that carries meaning: headings and their hierarchy, bold, strikethrough (superseded content) and underline (insertions on redlines), superscript and subscript.
- Tables. Values attached to the correct row and column headers, merged cells and hierarchical headers. A swapped header is critical, while extra HTML markup is a benign variation and costs nothing.
- Charts. Every data point of the chart, with series and axis labels, recovered as a table. An order-of-magnitude error is critical. Abbreviating a series name is minor.
- Layout (visual grounding). Every extracted element traced back to its location and class on the page.
- Forms. Field values, checkboxes and signatures on real government forms. A misread value is critical. Fields left empty must stay empty, and inventing a value for them counts as an error.
The overall score is the unweighted mean of the six dimension scores. Within a dimension, page scores are first averaged per dataset. Each targeted dataset gets 1/8 of the weight and the broad dataset gets the rest.

Text and Formatting share the same 508 text/ pages, scored with different metrics.
The ParseBench-2 scorers are part of parse-bench. One command downloads this dataset, runs a pipeline on every subset and scores the results into the six dimension scores and the overall score:
pip install "parse-bench[runners,fast]"
# See `parse-bench pipelines` for the built-in pipelines
parse-bench run llamaparse_agentic --dataset parsebench-2
# Only some subsets; every subset that already has results is still scored
parse-bench run llamaparse_agentic --dataset parsebench-2 --group charts,tables
The dataset is downloaded to ./data_parsebench-2. Results for each subset are written to output/parsebench-2/<subset>/<pipeline>/, with per-page CSV and HTML reports. The combined score goes to output/parsebench-2/_score/<pipeline>/_next_score.json. A dimension is reported as missing until all of its subsets have been scored.
To score outputs from your own parser, write them in the ParseBench results layout and run parse-bench evaluation run <dir> --next=SUBSET=RESULTS_DIR::TEST_CASES_DIR,... (see docs/extending.md in the ParseBench repo).
Each subset's --group name is also its folder in this repository and its split in the dataset viewer.
Dataset Format
The repository has one folder per subset, in the layout the parse-bench harness reads directly, and one <subset>.jsonl index per subset for browsing:
<subset>/
├── <name>.pdf | .jpg # source page
├── <name>.test.json # answer key and test rules for that page
└── <name>.md # the reference Markdown, where it is a separate file (every text page)
<subset>.jsonl # one row per page (the dataset viewer's split)
Each case is one page. Names keep their source group as a prefix where a subset has several (text_simple__10k, annotated__0000050863-25-000054_p7, filled__...). A row of <subset>.jsonl has the page's id, subset, dimension, document path, expected_markdown (null where the answer key is held in the rules), test_rules and metadata (both JSON strings, as in the .test.json) and tags.
Differences from ParseBench-1
- Metrics. The v1 metrics (Content Faithfulness, Semantic Formatting, GTRM, ChartDataPointMatch, Element Pass Rate) are replaced by severity-capped metrics: text_next, formatting_v2, table_next, chart_next, layout_grounding_pass_rate and forms_v2.
- Annotations. v1 pages are kept but their references are revised and made more complete. Charts are annotated with full data tables in place of spot checks.
- New datasets. Targeted sets for complex table headers, financial tables, insurance filing (SERFF) tables, dense tables, headings, strikeout, underline and forms.
- New dimension. Forms, scored on 500 real government form pages (typed fills, 10% blank) plus 100 fillable-form pages.
- Scores are not comparable with ParseBench-1 scores.
We welcome community contributions to the ParseBench-2 leaderboard. To add a model's scores, open a PR on the model's Hugging Face repo that adds a .eval_results/parsebench-2.yaml file, with one entry per task. The tasks are overall (the unweighted mean of the six dimensions) and the six dimensions text, formatting, tables, charts, layout and forms. Report scores on a 0–100 scale; parse-bench writes them as 0–1 fractions in _next_score.json.

The Quake 4 expansion that never shipped is now playable. openQ4 0.14.1, the Awakening Edition, is out now, and it is the biggest update since 0.13.2. It brings a second campaign, stable shadow mapping, a rebuilt interface, Vulkan on Windows, smarter bots and an experimental Android app, then spends the extra weeks polishing all of it toward production quality.

Raven Software and Ritual Entertainment built an expansion for Quake 4 that was never released. Justin Marshall recovered and published it, and openQ4 now plays it beside the original campaign: pick Single Player > Campaign and choose Quake 4 or The Awakening.
openQ4 ships none of the expansion's content; you supply the files. The Awakening guide shows how.
Prefer the original look? The classic screens and HUD are one setting away: seta ui_retained 0.
Three code audits and static analysis fixed crash, memory-safety and save-restore defects throughout the engine and game code. Saves are now written in a portable format and reported only once they are safely on disk, and openQ4's own data ships as separate packs so future patches can be small downloads.
Experimental means experimental: VR has not yet been qualified on headset hardware, Android has been qualified on one phone so far, and The Awakening is built from unfinished alpha content, with full playthrough qualification still in progress. Your reports help.
